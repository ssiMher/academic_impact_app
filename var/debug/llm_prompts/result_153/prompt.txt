Acoustic Eavesdropping From Sound-Induced
Vibrations With Multi-Antenna mmWave Radar
Wenhao Li , Riccardo Spolaor , Chuanwen Luo , Yuchao Sun, Huashan Chen, Guoming Zhang,
Yanni Yang , Xiuzhen Cheng , Fellow, IEEE, and Pengfei Hu
Abstract—Acoustic eavesdropping against private or conﬁden-
tial spaces is a signiﬁcant threat in the realm of privacy protection.
While the presence of soundproof material would weaken such an
attack, current eavesdropping technology may be able to bypass
these protections. Fortunately, existing studies either inadequately
cover the full spectrum of human speech due to low-frequency
responses or rely heavily on the prior knowledge used to train a
model. To address these challenges, this paper introduces mmEcho,
a new acoustic eavesdropping method that utilizes millimeter-wave
signals to sense vibration induced by sound precisely. Through
signal processing techniques such as the intra-chirp scheme and
phase calibration algorithm, mmEcho achieves micrometer-level
vibration extraction without requiring target-related data. To im-
prove the range of eavesdropping attacks while reducing noise, we
optimize radar signals by leveraging the widespread availability of
multiple antennas on commercial off-the-shelf radars. We com-
prehensively evaluate the performance of mmEcho in different
real-world settings. Experimental results demonstrate that, with
the aid of multi-antenna technology, mmEcho can more effectively
reconstruct the audio from the target at various distances, direc-
tions, sound insulators, reverberating objects, sound levels, and
languages. Compared to existing methods, our approach provides
better effectiveness without prior knowledge, such as the speech
data from the target.
Index Terms—Audio eavesdropping, mmWave radar, vibration
sensing, signal processing, multiple antennas.

I. INTRODUCTION
T
HE threat of acoustic eavesdropping has long been a
concern for protecting users’ privacy or conﬁdential infor-
mation. Despite soundprooﬁng materials being widely used in
modern buildings to mitigate non-effort eavesdropping attacks,
the continuous advancement of technology has led to increas-
ingly sophisticated eavesdropping methods, posing a continuing
challenge to user’s information security.
In recent years, acoustic eavesdropping has emerged as a
subject of extensive research aimed at recovering sound utilizing
various types of signals. These eavesdropping techniques can be
classiﬁed into two main categories: intrusive and non-intrusive
attacks. Intrusive attacks employ motion sensors, such as ac-
celerometers and gyroscopes[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8],
[9], to achieve near-ﬁeld eavesdropping. These attacks suppose
that an adversary can have access to sensor data on the victim’s
device or be in close proximity to an audio source in the
same room. On the other hand, non-intrusive eavesdropping
attacks do not require such unrealistic assumptions. Instead,
such attacks utilize devices such as high-speed cameras[10],
laser transceivers [11], [12], and photodiodes [13] to recover
speech from vibrations induced by sound waves. Addition-
ally, researchers have utilized WiFi[14], [15] and millimeter
wave [16], [17], [18] signals to extract these vibrations and
intercept audio. However, from a practical standpoint, these
methods have the following limitations:
(i) Rely on machine learning or prior knowledge:Numerous
eavesdropping schemes utilize machine learning (ML) meth-
ods to obtain satisfactory results[16], [17], [18]. Nevertheless,
ML-based approaches typically require extensive labeled data
for training, incurring signiﬁcant time and computational costs.
Moreover, the effectiveness of ML is contingent on data quality;
it might require data speciﬁc to the intended user to perform
optimally. Acquiring such user-speciﬁc data is frequently chal-
lenging or unfeasible.
(ii) Constrained vocabulary and low frequency response:
Most current approaches treat the eavesdropping attack as a
classiﬁcation task, identifying known individual phonemes or
a limited selection of hot words by segmenting audio[1], [2],
[3], [4], [5], [14], [15], [18]. Consequently, they fail to grasp the
meaning and intent of the speech. Furthermore, most existing
techniques are only capable of reconstructing low-frequency
audio, such as below 1kHz[10], [13], which does not encompass
the full spectrum of human speech.

(iii) High cost, non-portable, and low resolution:Previous
works have utilized sensors such as laser transceivers[11], [12],
high-speed cameras [10], and telescopes [13]. These devices
either require specialized customization or come with a high
cost, with most being unwieldy and inconvenient to carry. Addi-
tionally, most of these solutions suffer from inadequate vibration
resolution and usually only provide vibration measurements at
the millimeter level and above.
(iv) Single-target eavesdropping only:Most recent eavesdrop-
ping methods recover speech from a single audio source. For
instance, the methods described in[1], [2], [3], [4], [5] utilize
inertial measurement unit (IMU) to capture the audio vibrations
emitted by a smartphone’s internal speaker. However, there are
usually multiple targets in a room, and eavesdropping from only
a single source cannot capture the full content of a conversation.
To reconstruct audio covering the spectrum of human
speech without prior knowledge, we propose mmEcho, a non-
invasive acoustic eavesdropping method utilizing millimeter-
wave (mmWave) radar signals. To implement mmEcho, we must
address three primary challenges:
r How to improve frequency response to cover the spectrum
of human speech?
r How to measure micrometer-level vibration accurately?
r How to utilize multiple antennas to optimize the audio
reconstruction performance?
First, mmWave radars utilize ”chirps” to measure the distance.
In the best case, each chirp is measured independently and
results in a distance value. However, the traditional method of
measuring distance every few chirps (inter-chirp) dramatically
limits the frequency response of the reconstructed audio (below
1 kHz), which results in the loss of most of the spectral infor-
mation of human speech. Therefore, to enhance the frequency
response, it is essential to improve the utilization of chirps.
To address this challenge, we achieve high-precision distance
measurements by measuring and calculating the phase of a single
chirp (intra-chirp). This approach provides a faster distance
estimation while maximizing the use of the chirp rate. Thus,
using a radar with 10 k chirp rate, the frequency of the audio
reconstructed by mmEcho can be up to 5 kHz.
Second, existing schemes can only reconstruct vibrations at
the millimeter level and above, hence typically targeting large
speakers. However, when audio sources lack distinct reﬂective
surfaces and are in motion, previous methods become inef-
fective. Therefore, utilizing surrounding objects (reverberating
objects) to reconstruct the original audio becomes a feasible
solution, while requiring us to achieve micrometer-level vi-
bration measurements. To tackle this challenge, we propose a
phase calibration algorithm. It calibrates the signal phase with
frequency and phase interpolation, enabling distance estimation
with micrometer-level precision. By leveraging the advantage of
the mmWave radar’s high chirp rate, we merge all distance data
from chirps to reconstruct the amplitude of the audio signal.
Finally, single-antenna-based vibration sensing is limited
in high-precision application scenarios due to the insufﬁcient
transmit power of commercial mmWave radars. Multiple-input
multiple-output (MIMO) designs for mmWave radars have the
potential to solve this problem, however, it is a challenge to
increase the vibration sensing range using optimized radar sig-
nals from multiple antennas. To address this, we combine the
data from multiple antennas according to their weights to form
an optimized signal by modeling the radar signal and proposing
a weighting algorithm. This optimized signal not only contains
all the signals that are beneﬁcial to sensing but also enhances the
strength of the target’s reﬂected signal. Using multiple antennas
on the radar to complete beamforming, we can break through
the original power limitations to achieve a wider sensing range.
The main contributions of the work in this paper are summa-
rized as follows:
r We present mmEcho, a mmWave-based acoustic eaves-
dropping system that reconstructs audio information from
sound-induced vibrations on reverberating objects without
any training dataset and prior knowledge.
r We propose intra-chirp scheme and phase calibration algo-
rithms to provide micrometer-level sound vibration sensing
without sacriﬁcing the chirp rate, which enables mmEcho
to penetrate sound-insulating materials to eavesdrop on
reverberating objects made of everyday materials.
r We propose a multi-antenna combination technique for
implementing beamforming. The algorithm enhances the
strength of the target reﬂected signal by combining signals
from multiple antennas according to optimized weights
while reducing the effect of noise interference.
r Extensive evaluation of mmEcho across diverse environ-
ments demonstrated its effectiveness in audio reconstruc-
tion, achieving a Mel-Cepstral Distortion average of 2.76,
a Mean Opinion Score average of 4.22, and a Word Error
Rate average of 10.81% .

A. Motion and Optical Sensor-Based Acoustic Eavesdropping

fusion [5] (i.e., accelerometer, gyroscopes, and geophones) can
be used to capture sounds within a room. Other methods involve
using built-in accelerometers to rebuild the audio output of the
loudspeaker [19] or leveraging a vibration motor and a magnetic
hard drive to recreate sound[6], [7]. Additionally, converting
computer-connected speakers into microphones[9] or deploying
the vibration sensors on the glasses [8] have been used for

Acoustic eavesdropping has also been attempted using cam-

B. Wireless-Based Acoustic Eavesdropping
Recently, a noticeable trend considers launching eavesdrop
attacks employing WiFi, RFID, and mmWave technologies. The
authors in [15] and [14] have demonstrated that the channel
state information and received signal strength from WiFi can
be used to identify limited words. The ultra-wideband impulse
radio technology used in[21] enables the separation of multiple
audio sources by detecting vibrations. Similarly,[22] employs
the same wireless-based technology to capture audio with fre-
quencies below 400 Hz. These methods cannot recover human
speech due to the low frequency response.[23] utilizes RFID
tags and cGAN to perform acoustic eavesdropping, but this
approach is less practical as it requires pre-installing RFID tags
near the target. Overall, these methods based on WiFi signals
and impulse radio [14], [15], [21], [22], [23] are limited by
the inadequate vibration resolution caused by low packet rates
and long wavelengths. Moreover, they require larger antenna
conﬁgurations than portable mmWave radars, resulting in a more
visible presence of the attacker.
In mmWave-based eavesdropping research, MILLIEAR[17]
utilizes coarse-grained phase estimates to detect millimeter-level
amplitude vibrations [24]. However, these methods based on
inter-chirp are limited by low-frequency response and accuracy
issues, which are mitigated using cGAN. The authors in[18],
[25] employ millimeter waves to eavesdrop on audio from phone
calls, but[18] approach eavesdropping as a classiﬁcation task,
restricting its detection to speciﬁc words or numbers. Simi-
larly, [26], [27] also propose millimeter-wave eavesdropping
schemes based on the word or number classiﬁcation. The authors
in [26] enable directional beam formation by applying additional
phases to different transmitting antennas to enable resultful
remote detection of small vibrations, but the additional resource
consumption of deep learning and a limited vocabulary leaves
speech eavesdropping constrained. Both studies[17], [24], [25],
[26], [27] and [18] depend on machine learning, necessitating
extensive labeled datasets that are difﬁcult to acquire for eaves-
dropping objectives.

C. Other Wireless-Based Methods not Used for Eavesdropping
There are several other methods based on wireless signals but
not suitable for eavesdropping,[28] utilizes Doppler radar for
keyword recognition, limited to frequencies below 200 Hz.[29]
introduces a method to recover the view of an LCD screen
through millimeter-wave radar. In industrial scenarios, the au-
thors of [30], [31] achieve mechanical vibration sensing by
using mmWave and WiFi COTS devices, respectively, yet these
schemes are restricted to sub-500 Hz frequencies. Notably,
the approach in[30], which capitalizes on the non-parallelism
of multiple antenna beams for geometric angle calculation, is
highly effective in near-ﬁeld scenarios. However, in far-ﬁeld
conditions, where beams tend to be parallel, this method may
lead to inaccurate angle estimations. The approach in[32] em-
ploys millimeter-wave radar for vibration sensing below 1 kHz,
requiring proximity to the audio source within 5 cm to eavesdrop.
Instead of focusing on speech reconstruction,[33] develops a
user veriﬁcation system using a mmWave radar.[34] leverages
FMCW radar to reconstruct audio below 1 kHz, although it
lacks comprehensive experimental validation.[16] reconstructs
high-quality sounds using mmWave radar, requiring the target
to remain still and close to the radar (within 2 m). In[35],
a custom-made millimeter-wave radar is used for vibration
detection, while [36] enhances speech signals captured by a
similar radar with speech enhancement algorithms.[37] presents
a noise-resistant speech recognition system that merges millime-
ter wave technology with microphones, using machine learning.
However, the frequency response of the works in[28], [30],
[32], [34], [37] cannot fully encompass the human speech
spectrum range from 300 Hz to 3.4kHz[38]. Furthermore, the
machine learning-dependent methods in[16], [28], [29], [33],
[37] require extensive datasets for training, limiting them to a
ﬁxed vocabulary and thus cannot eavesdrop on the entire speech.
The methods proposed in[35], [36] depend on custom hardware
and are only effective in line-of-sight scenarios, making them
impractical for cost-effective, non-line-of-sight eavesdropping.
Consequently, none of the previous methods satisfy all the
demands for acoustic eavesdropping in practical situations.

A. Reverberating Object Location (ROL)
The ROL module analyzes radar data to locate reverberant
objects in the victim’s room. It identiﬁes objects with sound-
induced vibrations and ﬁlters out objects with insufﬁcient vibra-
tions for processing in subsequent modules.
For facilitating signal processing, our approach starts with
the collection of raw ADC data from the radar, which is then
transformed into multi-dimensional IQ arrays. Subsequently,
the acquired IF signal will be windowed (Hanning) to avoid
spectrum leakage. Then, a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is per-
formed to generate the spectrum of the Range-FFT,SpecIF(f)
and Specφ(f), containing the range bins of one single chirp.
By utilizing the results of Range-FFT, IF-based differentiation
of multiple targets can be achieved. Peaks in the Range-FFT
spectrum are identiﬁed using a peak detection algorithm, namely
the Continuous Wavelet Transform[40].
In the radar scope, each frequency peak in the spectrum
represents an object. Although Range-FFT provides the distance
of the target, the reverberating object cannot be localized by it
alone. Based on the results of Range-FFT, we employ Doppler-
FFT to achieve the purpose of localization. Fig.4 illustrates a
Doppler-FFT spectrum, where objects within the radar scope are
identiﬁable, with vibrating objects exhibiting signiﬁcant lateral
velocity. By establishing a threshold on the velocity direction
(i.e., speed gate), we can distinguish vibrating objects from
others along the range direction (i.e.,range gate). Through this
process, we can detect potential reverberating objects and ﬁlter
out stationary objects for our eavesdropping attacks.

C. Vibration Extraction and Audio Reconstruction (VA)
The V A module stands at the heart of our methodology, as it
focuses on reconstructing audio from passive vibrations induced
by sound waves on reverberating objects. The main goal of the
V A is to quantify these vibrations as a time-ordered arrangement
of radar-to-object distances.
In particular, after obtaining the optimized signal from mul-
tiple antennas, we utilize the peak detection algorithm based
on CWT [40] to analyze the frequency spectrum. All peaks
identiﬁed in the Range-FFT correspond to different objects and
can be used to calculate their distances. However, due to the
limited number of sample points in a single chirp (e.g., up
to 400 sample points per chirp on our mmWave radar), the
resulting peak frequencyfin has limited resolution. To enhance
precision, we employ the parabolic interpolation algorithm. This

speech is closer to natural speech. It is widely used to evaluate the
performance of reconstructed or generated speech. It is generally
accepted that an MCD lower than 8 is recognizable by speech
recognition systems[44].

VII. DISCUSSION
This section discusses some capabilities of mmEcho and
potential defenses against our attack.
Near-Real-Time Eavesdropping:Our methodology for signal
processing is implemented in Python v3.10, augmented by Scipy
v1.9.0, Numpy v1.23, and Cython v0.29.32. The signals are
gathered from the radar sensor and organized into 4-second
data batches. Both the processing duration and memory foot-
print scale with the batch length, d. For a 4-second batch, the
memory allocation peaks at 400 MB. Currently, processing and
reconstructing audio from a single batch on a single CPU core
takes approximately 2.58 seconds, as detailed in SectionVI.
With the processing interval falling below the batch period, we
achieve an uninterrupted audio feed, albeit with a total latency
of about 6 seconds from the initial signal capture.
Signal Processing Unaided by Machine Learning:Our system
utilizes signal processing methodologies to sidestep the limita-
tions commonly associated with machine learning approaches.

Unlike machine learning, which leans heavily on large datasets
and extensive training phases, signal processing equips mmEcho
with the capability to conduct effective eavesdropping free from
data reliance. This design ensures that mmEcho can accurately
reconstruct audio without the need for any pre-acquired target in-
formation, such as voice samples or the audio source’s position.
Consequently, our system is adept at practical eavesdropping,
unrestricted by vocabulary constraints or the necessity for prior
target knowledge.
Vibration Direction of the Target Object:To further improve
attack performance, the adversary can detect the vibration di-
rection of the target object to adjust the direction of mmWave
radar. One solution is to analyze phase shifts between mul-
tiple antennas and estimate the vibration direction based on
phase variations. However, this presents challenges. First, due
to the small vibration amplitudes, accurately identifying the
direction requires high phase measurement precision and a good
signal-to-noise ratio, as noise can interfere with the extraction
of vibrations. Second, detecting vibration direction depends on
antenna conﬁguration; estimating it through phase differences
requires complex signal processing, and the spacing and ar-
rangement of the antenna affect the accuracy. To overcome these
challenges, a high-power MIMO mmWave radar with increased
antenna spacing could be customized to capture the trend of
the target’s vibration direction, and the radar angle can then be
adjusted based on this trend.
Impact of Environmental Complexity: During our experi-
ments, we observe that mmEcho performs slightly less effec-
tively in complex environments compared to controlled settings,
especially concerning MCD. This slight degradation is partly
due to background noise in complex environments, where the
spectral energy distribution can affect overall similarity to the
original spectrum. In addition, multipath reﬂections and Doppler
effects add complexity to the target audio components in the
spectrum, reducing spectral similarity. However, these factors
have a limited impact on mmEcho in indoor settings, as the pri-
mary audio components remain detectable within the spectrum.
Thus, advanced speech recognition algorithms can still extract
sensitive audio information.
Defenses against mmWave-Based Eavesdropping:To miti-
gate the risks posed by our eavesdropping attack, we discuss
multiple defensive strategies: jamming, intelligent reﬂecting sur-
face, and RF shielding. 1) Jamming disrupts the radio frequency
environment by introducing additional or false signals to radar,
distorting echo signals, and hindering the acquisition of sensitive
voice information. Key strategies include suppression jamming,
which emits high-power noise to mask genuine echoes; targeting
jamming, which conﬂicts the jamming signal’s frequency with
the radar; and sweeping jamming, which periodically scans a
frequency band to suppress enemy signals. Nevertheless, these
techniques require substantial resources and can consume sig-
niﬁcant energy. 2) Intelligent Reﬂecting Surface (IRS) is an
emerging technology that can be used to effectively prevent
eavesdropping by dynamically adjusting the phase, amplitude,
and polarization of reﬂected signals to redirect wireless commu-
nications. By controlling the signal path, IRS reduces the likeli-
hood that radar signals are reﬂected back to eavesdroppers and
diminishes the quality of the signal reception at their location,
making it difﬁcult to obtain clear audio reconstructions. How-
ever, implementing IRS requires substantial time, resources, and
expensive hardware. 3) RF shielding is an effective deterrent
against RF signal penetration. While encasing a room entirely
within a Faraday cage[55] is the most comprehensive approach,
it is impractical for everyday use. A more feasible alternative is
the application of an RF shielding mesh, which signiﬁcantly
reduces the intrusion of RF signals into the victim’s space[56].
In our experiments, we assess the impact of RF shielding on
mmEcho’s audio reconstruction capabilities. We introduced a
stainless steel mesh with a 0.5 mm wire diameter and 5 mm
aperture as a barrier. This resulted in a notable reduction of the
radar signal’s SNR from 29.52 dB to 18.93 dB. Concurrently,
the MCD values of the audio reconstruction change from 3.12
to 10.90, exceeding the threshold of 8 beyond which speech
recognition systems fail to decipher the audio content [44].
These ﬁndings conﬁrm the effectiveness of RF shielding as a
countermeasure.
Improved mmEcho:First, to enhance mmEcho, we introduced
a Multi-antenna Data Combination (MDC) module into the
mmEcho system, enabling more efﬁcient extraction of useful
information. In addition, we evaluated the impact of the MDC
module on mmEcho’s performance and conducted comprehen-
sive tests under various background noise, temperature, and
humidity conditions. The system was also validated in a real of-
ﬁce environment, demonstrating its potential to handle complex
scenarios. Furthermore, we discussed three potential defense
strategies to counteract RF-based eavesdropping attacks.
VIII. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we introduce a millimeter-wave-based acoustic
eavesdropping attack scheme that captures and reconstructs
audio through signal processing. This approach circumvents
the need for machine learning algorithms and pre-existing data
about the target. Our comprehensive evaluation demonstrates the
robustness of mmEcho in various real-life conditions, including
variations in distance, direction, reverberating material, sound
insulator, sound volume, and spoken language. With the aid of
multi-antenna technology, our system enables more distant and
more effective eavesdropping. Additionally, our technique is
capable of intercepting sounds from both stationary and moving
sources, enhancing the overall efﬁcacy of the eavesdropping
process.
