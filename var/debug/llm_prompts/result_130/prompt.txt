Ambient Light Reﬂection-Based Eavesdropping
Enhanced With cGAN
Guoming Zhang ,H e q i a n gF u, Zhijie Xiang , Xinyan Zhou , Member , IEEE, Pengfei Hu ,
Xiuzhen Cheng , Fellow, IEEE, and Yanni Yang
Abstract—Sound eavesdropping using light has been an area
of considerable interest and concern, as it can be achieved over
long distances. However, previous work has often lacked stealth
(e.g., active emission of laser beams) or been limited in the range
of realistic applications (e.g., using direct light from a device’s
indicator LED or a hanging light bulb). In this paper, we present
EchoLight, a non-intrusive, passive and long-range sound eaves-
dropping method that utilizes the extensive reﬂection of ambient
light from vibrating objects to reconstruct sound. We analyze the
relationship between reﬂection light signals and sound signals,
particularly in situations where the frequency response of reﬂective
objects and the efﬁciency of diffuse reﬂection are suboptimal.
Based on this analysis, we have introduced an algorithm based
on cGAN to address the issues of nonlinear distortion and spectral
absence in the frequency domain of sound. We extensively evaluate
EchoLight’s performance in a variety of real-world scenarios.
It demonstrates the ability to accurately reconstruct audio from
a variety of source distances, attack distances, sound levels, light
sources, and reﬂective materials. Our results reveal that the recon-
structed audio exhibits a high degree of similarity to the original
audio over 40 meters of attack distance.
Index Terms—Optical eavesdropping, ambient light, generative
adversarial network.

II. RELATED WORK
In this section, we extensively review state-of-the-art work
related to eavesdropping. Certain eavesdropping techniques
leverage the impact of speech on smartphone motion sensors
(gyroscope and accelerometer) to gather private user information
[10], [12], [24], [25].I n [13], multiple non-acoustic sensors
are fused for intelligible signal reconstruction. Maruri et al.
[26] explores the use of vibration sensors in the nose pads of
glasses for eavesdropping. Roy and Choudhury[27] employs a
vibra-motor, vibrating due to the movement of magnetic sub-
stances, as an acoustic sensor. Guri et al.[28] demonstrates how
earphones can be transformed into eavesdropping microphones
through malware, while[29] treats mechanical hard drives as
synthetic microphones. Additionally,[22], [23] transforms the
lidar sensing radar on a robotic vacuum cleaner into a laser
microphone, and RFID[30] is also utilized for eavesdropping.
It is worth noting that the aforementioned eavesdropping
attacks are considered invasive attacks, requiring the eaves-
dropper to either physically tamper with the victim’s device or
install malware on it. On the other hand, some eavesdropping
methods [10], [12], [13], [24], [25] rely on pre-established
vocabulary of hot words and are limited to tasks such as word
classiﬁcation, speaker identiﬁcation, or gender identiﬁcation. In
contrast to these methods, our proposed approach belongs to a
non-intrusive attack and does not rely on pre-established vocab-
ulary. This signiﬁcantly enhances its practicality in real-world
scenarios.
Non-intrusive eavesdropping attacks are continually being
developed. For instance,[8], [9] employ WiFi Received Signal
Strength (RSS) and Channel State Information (CSI) analysis,
respectively, to identify speciﬁc words. Another approach[31],
based on Impulse Radio Ultra-Wideband, recovers and separates
sound from multiple sources. However, these methods face
challenges such as limited vibration resolution due to longer
wavelengths and the need for large antenna setups, which in-
creases operational difﬁculty in real-world scenarios. Moreover,
mmWave-based eavesdropping attacks have been explored. For
instance, mmEve [4] captures mmWave signals emitted by
smartphones in earphone mode to recover speech. Wavesdropper
[32] uses millimeter waves to eavesdrop on human speech in
through-wall scenarios. Another method proposed in[5] em-
ploys piezoelectric ﬁlm and millimeter wave signals for voice
eavesdropping. mmSpy [6] also utilizes mmWave for eaves-
dropping on phone conversations but treats it as a classiﬁcation
problem, limiting its ability to recognize speciﬁc keywords or
numbers. Additionally,[7] can reconstruct high-quality sound
from the user’s throat using millimeter-wave radar, but it requires
the subject to remain motionless and maintain a short distance
from the radar probe.
Optical eavesdropping is the most relevant eavesdropping
technique to this article. The visual microphone[33] captures
subtle surface vibrations of objects using a high-speed camera
(2200 FPS). However, this method demands signiﬁcant com-
puting resources and high hardware requirements, resulting in
several hours of processing time to recover a few seconds of
audio. Laser microphones[20], [21], [22], [23], [34] use lidar
sensors to illuminate vibrating objects and collect reﬂected sig-
nals for audio reconstruction. However, these methods have sev-
eral drawbacks: 1) They are active, relying on active sensors that
require signal transmission and reception. 2) They lack sufﬁcient
concealment, as specialized optical sensors can detect directed
laser beams since the eavesdropper needs to inject the laser beam
into the victim’s room. 3) They are position-sensitive, as laser
transceivers require precise calibration during signal reception,
and even slight positional deviations can affect the signal quality.
4) Certain methods[22], [23] are considered intrusive attacks,
requiring malware installation in the victim’s device to extract
laser data. 5) They have equipment restrictions, as specialized
equipment with limited availability (such as specialized laser
transceivers) may be required, and these may only be accessible
to speciﬁc national departments.

III. THREAT MODEL
The scenario we consider is that the victims are in an ofﬁce or
conference room discussing conﬁdential business matters, and
the meeting may or may not use loudspeakers. Sunlight enters
the room through the window and shines on various everyday
objects (called reﬂective objects), or indoor light sources (such
as desk lamps) shine on reﬂective objects, and then the light is
reﬂected out the window.
The eavesdropper uses a telescope to capture the reﬂected
ambient light (either sunlight or indoor lighting) and this cap-
tured light is received by a photodetector. The photodetector
is connected to a Data Acquisition Card (DAQ) to convert the
analog signal to a digital format. After the digital signal is
processed, the high-frequency information is recovered using
a bandwidth extension algorithm. Finally, the eavesdropper re-
ceives a reconstructed, high-quality sound.
In this attack scenario, we assume that the eavesdropper has
the following conditions and capabilities when launching an
EchoLight attack:
r The attacker cannot access or damage the victim’s device,
nor can the attacker install any sensors in the victim’s room.
r The victim’s room contains common everyday items, such
as reﬂective objects like packing bag, vanity mirror, ﬁle
folder, etc. Sound waves can induce minute vibrations in
these reﬂective objects.
r There must be an unobstructed line of sight between the
telescope and the reﬂective object in the victim’s room,
with the reﬂective object receiving light.
r The attacker does not require knowledge of the speciﬁc
words or any prior information spoken by the victim. They
should be able to reconstruct audio from any complete
sentence.
r The attacker does not need expensive physical equipment;
instead, they only require passive sensors, telescopes, and
signal processing tools that are not categorized as spy
equipment.
IV . PRELIMINARIES

ping attack and introduce the Generative Adversarial Networks

A. Feasibility Analysis
To conduct a successful eavesdropping attack, we investigated
whether the surface vibrations resulting from the acoustic wave
are sufﬁcient to recover sound and whether the ambient light
(such as sunlight or indoor desk lamp light) reﬂected from the
surface carries enough information about the acoustic vibration.
We selected various materials as reﬂective objects (e.g., mirror,
photo frame, packing bag, ﬁle folder and gift box) and placed
the speaker approximately 5cm away from the reﬂective object.
During the experiment, we set the sound level of the loud-
speaker to 90 dB and played a chirp signal with a frequency
range from 100 Hz to 8 kHz, as well as speech audio. To capture
the reﬂected ambient light, we placed a telescope at a distance of
5 meters from the reﬂective object. A photodetector was placed
behind the eyepiece of the telescope to detect the changing light
signal received by the telescope. Subsequently, we used a DAQ
to convert the optical analog signal detected by the photodetector
into a digital signal for further analysis.
Fig. 2 displays the results of the feasibility analysis using
sunlight and indoor desk lamp light sources. It is evident that
each material’s maximum response frequency to the chirp signal
is quite high (around 4 - 7 kHz). The signal strength gradually
decreases as the frequency increases, eventually leading to no
response at certain high frequencies. On the other hand, each
material’s maximum response frequency to the speech audio
is relatively low (approximately 1000 Hz). This is attributed
to the weak high-frequency component of the speech audio,
which differs from the chirp signal that possesses a high am-
plitude across all frequency bands. Additionally, the response
of reﬂective objects to high frequencies is weaker than that to
low frequencies, making it challenging for them to generate any
substantial response at high frequencies. Moreover, we observed
that the audio recovered using sunlight exhibits a higher response
frequency compared to that obtained with a desk lamp light
source, and it also contains relatively less noise.
The results of our feasibility analysis show that there is a
certain correlation between light signals and sound signals,
both in the case of specular reﬂection (e.g., mirror) and diffuse
reﬂection (other materials). The vibration of the object’s surface
is capable of generating a signal that can be used for audio
recovery, with a frequency response of around 1000 Hz. In
addition, ambient light carries the information of the vibrations
caused by the sound when reﬂected from the reﬂective object.
Therefore, it is possible to use ambient light to recover sound
from the vibrations of reﬂective objects.

C. Generative Adversarial Network
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) were originally
proposed by Ian Goodfellow et al.[41]. The architecture of
GAN consists of a generator and a discriminator. The generator
takes a random noise sequence as input and produces an image
that appears realistic. On the other hand, the discriminator takes
either a real image or an image generated by the generator
as input and then determines whether the input image is real
or fake. Through multiple alternating training iterations of the
discriminator and the generator, the discriminator’s ability to
distinguish between true and fake images gradually improves,
while the generator’s capacity to create deceptive images to
fool the discriminator also enhances. This process resembles
a two-player minimax game, and it eventually reaches a Nash
equilibrium state, where the generator and the discriminator
cease to further improve their respective capabilities. As a result
of this adversarial training, the generated images become so
realistic that the discriminator discriminates the output of the
image by the generator as real images with a probability of 0.5.
Although GANs are capable of generating real-looking im-
ages, they may not generate the speciﬁc images we desire.
To address this limitation, conditional Generative Adversarial
Networks (cGANs)[42] were introduced. The architecture of a
cGAN is depicted in Fig.4. The generator takes both a noise
vector z and a condition vectory as input and outputs an image
G(z|y) that satisﬁes the given condition. On the other hand,
the discriminator takes both the image (G(z|y) or x) and the

V. EchoLight DESIGN
In the previous section, we have veriﬁed the feasibility of
ambient light eavesdropping and captured the changing light
signal caused by the vibration of the object’s surface. Next, we
proceed to reconstruct the optical signal acquired from the DAQ
into an audio signal. However, if the optical signal is directly
considered as an audio signal and written into a wav audio
ﬁle, it will be accompanied by strong noise, which hinders the
clear understanding of the audio. Therefore, we ﬁrst process the
audio for speech enhancement. Additionally, we noticed that
most of the collected optical signals only contain information
in the low frequency band (below 1000 Hz). To address this
limitation, we utilize a bandwidth extension method based on
the cGAN architecture to recover the missing high frequencies
from the audio, which initially contains only the low-frequency
component. In conclusion, we obtain high-quality reconstructed
audio through a two-step process involving speech enhancement
and bandwidth extension. In this section, we describe the design
details of each component inEchoLight.
A. Speech Enhancement
A direct transformation of the optical signal into audio intro-
duces considerable noise, making it unrecognizable. Thus, we
ﬁrst apply speech enhancement techniques to the optical signal.
Speciﬁcally, our speech enhancement process consists of the
following steps:
r Filtering: Typically, household AC power operates at a
frequency of 50Hz, and light bulbs function at this same
frequency. However, since the AC cycle has two peaks
(one positive and one negative), the brightness variation
frequency of the bulb is twice the AC frequency, usually
100Hz. As a result, the light signal captured by the photode-
tector contains 100Hz and its harmonics (200Hz, 300Hz,
etc.). We use a high-pass ﬁlter with a cutoff frequency of
100 Hz to remove components below 100 Hz, and band-
stop ﬁlters to remove the light frequency and its harmonics.
Fig. 5. The inﬂuence of the three stages of speech enhancement.
r Denoising: The captured optical signal exhibits pro-
nounced noise, similar to white noise, which is not a result
of the original audio. To deal with this, we leverage Sox
- Sound eXchange[43], a widely used command-line tool
for audio processing, to effectively diminish this type of
interference. Speciﬁcally, we select a short segment of
silent audio, extract its noise features, and then denoise
the entire audio segment based on the noise features.
r Peak Normalization: To adjust the dynamic range of the
denoised signal, we normalize the audio peak to 0 dB and
adjust other parts of the audio accordingly.
The implementation results of each stage are illustrated in
Fig. 5. The speech enhancement technology used in our study is
lightweight and straightforward in nature. This approach allows
us to obtain a relatively clean optical signal, which can then
be fed into the trained model for rapid reconstruction of high-
quality audio. This ensures a certain level of real-time capability
in the eavesdropping process, which is a critical requirement for
eavesdroppers.
B. Bandwidth Extension
Due to the poor frequency response of the reﬂective object
and the reﬂection efﬁciency, the collected optical signal always
lacks these high-frequency components. The goal of bandwidth
extension is to recreate the high-frequency components based
on the low-frequency components of the audio signal. This is
achieved by learning the mapping relationship between low-
frequency and high-frequency components in the sound. In our
method, we design the bandwidth extension method based on
cGAN.
1) Model Input and Output:The application ﬁeld of cGAN
primarily focuses on image translation and related tasks, where
both the input and output are in the form of images. In our case,
we apply the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) to convert
the input audio into a spectrogram, which is then used as the
input and output form for the model. The calculation formula

2) Model Architecture: Fig. 6 illustrates our model archi-
tecture, where the spectrograms of the original audiox and
the speech-enhanced light signal y are used as inputs to the
model. The generator adopts a Unet architecture, consisting of
8 Down-sampling and 8 Up-sampling processes. The Down-

Up-sampling process through skip connections. The output of
the ﬁnal Up-sampling layer has the same dimension as the input
spectrogram. On the other hand, the discriminator consists of 5
layers of 2D convolutional layers. The last layer of the general
discriminator is designed to output a scalar value representing
probability. However, in our discriminator, the last layer em-
ploys only 1 convolution kernel and outputs 30× 30 patches.
Subsequently, each patch is discriminated as either real or fake.

spectrogram and the spectrograms of the original audio, while
simultaneously making the discriminator output “True.” On the
other hand, the discriminator aims to accurately distinguish
between real and fake inputs. As a result, our objective function
can be formulated as follows:

4) Training Parameters: The training process involves 200
epochs. During the initial 100 epochs, the learning rate is set
to 0.0002, and then it gradually decays linearly to 0 over the
last 100 epochs. We utilize the Adam optimizer with a ﬁrst-
order momentum of 0.5 and a second-order momentum of 0.999
to update the parameters. Throughout the training process, the
discriminator and generator are trained alternately.
5) Spectrogram to Audio: The spectrogram generated by
the generator contains rich amplitude information for high-
frequency components but lacks any phase-related information,
making it challenging to directly reconstruct playable audio.
To address this, we employ the classic phase reconstruction
algorithm called Grifﬁn-Lim (GL) to recover the magnitude
spectrum back to audio with reasonable phase information.
The GL algorithm initiates with a random phase spectrum and
utilizes the phase spectrum along with the input magnitude spec-
trum to synthesize speech using iSTFT. Subsequently, STFT is
performed on the synthesized speech to obtain a new magnitude
spectrum and phase spectrum. The new magnitude spectrum is
then discarded, and the original magnitude spectrum is com-
bined with the new phase spectrum to synthesize speech again
using iSTFT. Through successive iterations, the estimated phase
spectrum converges to values close to the real phase spectrum,
resulting in an audio reconstruction with minimal distortion.

A. Evaluation Metrics
We utilize Mel Cepstral Distortion (MCD), Log Spectral
Distance (LSD), and Mean Opinion Score (MOS) as evaluation
metrics to assess the performance ofEchoLight.

between their logarithmic spectrograms. The combined use of
MCD and LSD provides a more comprehensive assessment
of the quality of synthesized speech. Lower values of MCD
and LSD indicate that the synthesized speech is closer to the
original speech. Generally, speech recognition systems consider
two sounds acceptable if their MCD values are less than 8.
MOS is obtained through human subjective evaluation. As
shown in TableII, scores range from 1 to 5, where 1 indicates
very poor reconstruction quality and 5 indicates very good
reconstruction quality. We recruited 16 volunteers (8 males and

VIII. DISCUSSION
A. Limitations of Proposed Attacks
EchoLight is able to perform eavesdropping attacks in a
variety of lighting conditions, including both sunlight and indoor
lighting scenarios. The high intensity of sunlight often produces
better results than indoor desk lamp light, with effectiveness
depending on the speciﬁc light intensity. While the sunlight
EchoLight attack can provide high-quality audio reconstruc-
tion, it presents certain challenges: (1) The movement of the sun,
which is beyond human control, results in constant changes in il-
lumination and reﬂection angles. As a result, attackers may need
to dynamically adjust attack angles during an eavesdropping
operation, especially if the time of eavesdropping is relatively
long. (2)EchoLight’s performance can be affected by changes
in weather conditions, with adverse weather or nighttime con-
ditions potentially resulting in a failed eavesdropping attempt.
Despite these limitations,EchoLight retains its effectiveness
when using indoor desk lamp lights or similar ambient light
sources, ensuring the recovery of intelligible audio. On clear
and sunny days, attackers can still choose sunlight to initiate
an EchoLight eavesdropping attack, achieving the highest
quality audio reconstruction.
In addition, the characteristics of reﬂective objects, such as
their ability to vibrate and their reﬂective properties, signiﬁ-
cantly affect the effectiveness ofEchoLight attacks. In fact,
the passive vibration capacity of everyday objects, such as pack-
ing bag, and ﬁle folder, is generally adequate. Another limitation
is the minimal sound pressure hitting on these reﬂective objects.
B. Inﬂuence of Breezy Conditions on Attack Performance
In addition to the factors mentioned earlier, in practical attack
scenarios, some relatively sturdy reﬂective objects (such as
mirrors, picture frames, etc.) may be less susceptible to the
inﬂuence of gentle breezes. However, other lightweight and thin
reﬂective objects (such as packaging bags, folders, gift boxes,
etc.) are often more prone to the effects of mild winds. To delve
deeper into the impact of ambient air currents onEchoLight
attacks, we conducted the following experiment. In this study, we
selected a packaging bag as a reﬂective object highly sensitive
to gentle winds. The speaker was positioned 5 cm behind the
packaging bag, the light bulb was approximately 0.5 m away
from the bag, and the telescope was situated 15 m from the bag
to capture reﬂected light. Around the packaging bag, we placed
a small fan with an approximate wind speed of 3 m/s, gener-
ating mild air disturbances that resulted in the packaging bag
experiencing gentle vibrations. We provide audio examples for
the following scenarios: original audio, original audio captured
in calm conditions, denoised audio in calm conditions, original
audio captured in windy conditions, and denoised audio in windy
conditions, as shown in the Fig.19.
It can be observed that the original audio signal captured under
windy conditions exhibits a relatively regular pattern of ﬂuctua-
tions. This is attributed to the fact that the vibrations caused by
the gentle breeze are predominantly low-frequency vibrations,
and the resulting movements in the packaging bag may alter the
angles of light reﬂection. If the vibrations are too intense, it could
potentially lead to the failure of eavesdropping attacks. However,
when the vibrations are within a certain range–allowing the

C. Suggestions for Improving Attack Strategies
For the proposed attack method in this article, we have
provided a feasible solution, but there is signiﬁcant room for
improvement in practical applications. The following are some
suggestions for optimizing the system components:
1) Finer Experimental Equipment:In the pursuit of reﬁning
the proposed attack methodology, attackers can enhance their
experimental setup by incorporating more advanced optical
instruments. Speciﬁcally, the utilization of professional-grade
telescopes is beneﬁcial for focusing on the precise collection of
modulated light signals from reﬂective objects. The effective-
ness of telescopes is intricately linked to the size of their lenses,
suggesting that choosing a telescope with a larger diameter
contributes to the comprehensive capture of light signals, thereby
optimizing the overall quality of the recovered audio. More-
over, attackers can elevate the performance of their light-based
eavesdropping systems by integrating light sensors endowed
with higher internal gain. The internal gain of these sensors
directly inﬂuences their sensitivity, rendering them more attuned
to subtle variations in light signals. This heightened sensitivity
enhances the system’s responsiveness to changes in ambient
light, consequently bolstering the quality of audio recovery.
To further reﬁne their experimental apparatus, attackers can
deploy a DAQ with a higher sampling rate tailored for the light
sensor. A higher sampling rate facilitates more frequent and
precise sampling of the modulated light signals, ensuring a more
accurate representation of the encoded audio information.

IX. CONCLUSION
In this work, we have introducedEchoLight, an innovative
method for ambient light eavesdropping that is capable of ef-
fectively recovering sounds occurring in the vicinity of a target.
EchoLight leverage ambient light, which is either reﬂected
or diffused by a range of reﬂective objects commonly found
in everyday life, to recover sound. To enhance the audio quality
and reconstruct the missing high-frequency components present
in the optical signal, we implemented a bandwidth extension
model based on the cGAN architecture. Our evaluation results
demonstrate the effectiveness ofEchoLight in reconstruct-
ing intelligible sounds under various settings, such as different
sound source distances, different reﬂective materials, and mov-
ing audio sources. Additionally,EchoLight has the ability to
launch attacks up to 40 meters away from the victim.
