Abstract—The popularity of smartphones presents a
potential risk to personal privacy, particularly through covert
eavesdropping by external nonacoustic sensors (e.g., high-
speed camera and radio frequency (RF) sensor), where
RF-based voice perception technology shows great potential
due to its insensitivity to the environment and its ability to
traverse occlusions. In this article, we introduce Vib2audio,
a high-quality audio recovery system that uses RF signals
to recover audio from the tiny vibration of sound source.
We discover that the extracted phase spectrum lacks compo-
nents in the high-frequency band. Therefore, we investigate
the frequency detectable range of mmWave radar used in our scenario and develop an improved spectrum reconstruction
network based on the pix2pix structure to accurately recover the time–frequency spectrum of audio from the limited
frequency band of the vibration signal. In addition, we synthesize the audio waveform of voice from the time–frequency
spectrum using a Griffin–Lim vocoder. Our extensive experiments indicate that Vib2audio can robustly recover tiny
sound source vibration to recognizable high-quality human voice audio from tiny vibration. In our overall objective and
subjective performance evaluation, the reconstructed audio by Vib2audio had a mean absolute error (MAE) of 3.6% in
the non-intrusive speech quality assessment (NISQA) and achieved a subjective identifiability assessment score of 3.48.
Furthermore, the quantitative evaluation results show that Vib2audio is effective and robust to background noise and
multisource interference.
Index Terms— Generative adversarial network (GAN), microvibration sensing, mmWave, sound recovery.

External speakers usually have large sound-generation units

case of built-in tiny speaker, vibration sensing based on RF
signal encounters a number of challenges as follows.
1) Microvibration Acquisition: The area of phone speaker
is usually only 20 × 14 mm, the vibration amplitude
is extremely weak (≈7 µm). Moreover, the speaker is
assembled inside the phone, the RF reflection signal of
vibration will be further attenuated under the cover of
the phone case. Such weak vibration is susceptible to
external disturbances of uncertain noise. How to extract
the weak vibration signal from the complex environment
is a challenging technical task.
2) Audio Reconstruction: The detectable frequency range
of RF sensing is narrower than the voice frequency
band. According to our experiment in Section III-C, the
detectable frequency range of mmWave radar is about
[0, 1300] Hz. Therefore, the high-frequency band of the
vibration signal by RF sensing will be missing, which
directly impacts the quality of audio recovery. It is
a big challenge to reconstruct accurate high-frequency
information based on low-frequency information.

the microvibration features of a microspeaker from the raw RF

spectrum reconstruction network and a vocoder. The extensive
evaluation results show that the Vib2audio reconstructed audio
has high similarity with the original audio. Our contributions
can be summarized as follows.

which utilizes an off-the-shelf mmWave frequency

and reconstruct the tiny vibration to recognizable
high-quality human voice audio.

alleviating the noise.

effectively learn the contextual relationship between
the detected low-frequency vibration features and the

B. Active Sensing
Active sensing method directly measures the microvibration
of vibration sources. Izzo et al. [15] used 24 GHz continu-
ous wave radar to analyze the micro-Doppler signatures of
speaker and demonstrated that the radar vibration detection
results were highly similar to the real speaker’s vibration.
But the system still suffers from multiple vibration sources
interference. To address multiple vibration sources interference
issue, Wang et al. [8] proposed an IR-UWB radar system
operating at 10 GHz that can recover and separate sound
from multiple sources simultaneously. The system utilizes the
ranging capability of IR-UWB to distinguish multiple vibra-
tion sources. Due to the limitation of its operating frequency
(3.1–10.6 GHz), IR-UWB is not sensitive to weak vibration
amplitudes (micrometer-level). mmWave is RFs in the range of
30–300 GHz, with corresponding wavelengths between 1 and
10 mm. The characteristic of high-frequency and short wave-
length makes mmWave technology have the ability to capture
fainter vibrations [16], [17]. Hu et al. [18] presented MIL-
LIEAR, an mmWave-based acoustic eavesdropping system
that utilized the high-resolution ranging capability of mmWave
FMCW radar to extract speaker’s vibration and reconstruct
the audio using a generative machine learning model. The
experiment showed the feasibility of mmWave-based vibration
sensing technology in reconstructing audio. mmWave radar not
only performs excellently in vibration detection, but also in
the distinguishment of multiple vibration sources. Xiong et al.
[19] proposed a full-field phase evolution tracking algorithm
based on MIMO FMCW mmWave radar system, the system
was capable of measuring and tracking the tiny motions of
all targets within the radar field of view simultaneously. This
algorithm has demonstrated significant performance in multi-
target scenarios, including noncontact vital sign monitoring of
multiple people, full-field mechanical vibration measurement,
and multiple sound source localization and reconstruction.
The previously mentioned loudspeakers are external loud-
speakers of the equipment (such as stereos), which have a
larger sound generator and vibration amplitude. Can mmWave
measure even weaker vibration? Researchers focused the
investigation on two micrometer-level vibration situations,
namely vocal fold vibration and smartphone speaker vibration.
Ozturk et al. [10] successfully used mmWave radar to measure
vocal fold vibration, and adopted multimodal deep learning
techniques to fuse the vocal fold vibration with audio signals
to improve voice enhancement and voice separation. This
demonstrated that the vibration of the vocal fold extracted
by the mmWave radar is highly correlated with the acoustic
signal. Wang et al. [9] and Zahid Ozturk et al. [20] further
extracted human vocal fold vibration in a soundproof sce-
nario and recovered voice contents. Basak and Gowda [21]
exploited mmWave radar to reconstruct and recover words on
predefined vocabularies by detecting the vibration of the phone
earpiece.
In our work, the detection target is the built-in microspeaker
of a smartphone. The microspeaker operates at the micrometer
level of vibration, which makes our work more challenging
than research in external speakers (see [18], [19]). The most
relevant to our work is [21] and [22], which have many
similarities to our work in terms of application scenarios and
audio data source. However, our work still has significant
differences. mmspy [21] used artificially synthesized radar
spectrum data to guide the training process of the machine
learning model, with only 5% real radar samples in the training
dataset. The dataset used by mmEve [22] is synthetic data,
with its training samples generated by superimposing radar
noise on the audio. Only 100 real radar data were collected
during the testing phase. In contrast, our work meticulously
collected 20 000 real radar audio samples as the training
foundation for machine learning model, which will further
improve the quality of the reconstructed audio. In regard to
the evaluation of audio quality, Basak and Gowda [21] used
the mean square error (mse) of the spectrum as the primary
metric, which is not a mainstream method in audio quality
evaluation. In order to verify the quality of reconstructed audio
more comprehensively, we use a variety of objective metrics
as well as subjective evaluation methods, all of which are
designed specifically for speech quality evaluation.

A loudspeaker is an electroacoustic transducer. The loud-
speaker first converts the electrical signal into mechanical
vibration, and then further into sound pressure waves. The
sound pressure waves reach our eardrums and cause them to
vibrate. The vibration of the eardrum is transmitted as an

The magnetic system generates a fixed magnetic field.

electrical signal. This alternating magnetic field interacts with

generate sound pressure waves and output them. The back-

magnetic field drives the displacement of the diaphragm,
resulting in the loudspeaker vibration. Therefore, the vibration
of the loudspeaker can be sensed by detecting the back-and-
forth displacement of the diaphragm.
A microspeaker is a small-sized loudspeaker commonly
used in portable devices such as smartphones, tablets, laptops,
and personal audio devices. The sound-generation principle of
the microspeaker is the same as the loudspeaker. Compared
with the loudspeaker, the vibration amplitude of the micros-
peaker is much smaller, generally ranging between a few
micrometers and tens of micrometers. The operation frequency
range of the microspeaker is between 20 Hz and 20 kHz which
is the full human hearing frequency. There are many types of
microspeakers, among which the dynamic microspeaker (also
known as moving coil microspeaker) is the most widely used
type of smartphone speaker due to its simple structure, low
cost, low power consumption, and high sensitivity, as well as
reasonably good sound quality. Therefore, we use the moving
coil microspeaker as the sensing object in this article.

A radar chirp can be considered as a single sampling of

many factors in practice, such as the limitations of the radar’s
hardware and the amplitude response of the detected object.
These factors resulted in the frequency band missing in the
vibration detection of tiny smartphone speaker.
C. Detectable Frequency Range
The issue of frequency band missing of the vibration
signal arises due to the commercial mmWave radar utilized in
this study, which was not designed specifically for vibration
sensing. The main reasons for the missing frequency band are
the limitation of the hardware and microdisplacement sensing
capability of the mmWave radar.
Section III-B outlines the operation and vibration sensing
method of a standard commercial FMCW radar, such as the
TI IWR1843 used in this article. To sense the full frequency
range of a loudspeaker from 20 Hz to 20 kHz, the chirp
duration T should be less than 22 µs. However, this setup is
not supported by the commercial radar hardware (e.g., analog
to digital converter (ADC) sampling rate and FM slope).
As a result, IWR1843 is considered unable to detect the full
frequency range of the loudspeaker. The frequency detection
range of the IWR1843 is also limited by its displacement
sensing capability.
What is the detectable frequency range of an IWR1843
radar? Hu et al. [18] employed the TI IWR1642, which
has close hardware specifications to the IWR1843, to detect
the vibration of a loudspeaker (Philips SPA33) at a distance
of 1 m. The results indicate that the maximum detectable
frequency is about 2000 Hz. However, our detection target is
a tiny smartphone speaker with lower amplitude. Therefore,
we believe that the maximum detectable frequency of the
IWR1843 in our scenario is lower.
To measure and estimate the maximum effective frequency
for the IWR1843 in our scenario, we utilized a smartphone
(HUAWEI MATE 30) to play the test audio [as shown in
Fig. 4(a)] while the IWR1843 is placed in front of the speaker
at a 1 m distance without any blockage. The frequency of the
test audio is from 100 Hz to 2 kHz to measure the IWR1843’s
frequency response. The radar detection spectrum is shown
in Fig. 4(b), we observe that radar suffers from white noise

E. Reconstruction Network
Since the detectable frequency range of RF sensing is
narrower than the voice frequency band, the radar vibration
spectrum is mainly correlated with the low-frequency band of
the real audio spectrum, while the high-frequency component
of the sound is missed by the limitation of RF sensing,
as shown in Fig. 4. The sampling frequency of the radar
is 14 kHz in this article, so the theoretically recognizable
vibration frequency is around 7 kHz, and the higher audio
frequencies cannot be captured under limited radar resolution.
So the spectrum reconstruction method should have the ability
to predict the missing high-frequency components from the
limited-band radar vibration spectrum. The key to spectrum
reconstruction is establishing the correlation between low and
high frequencies in the audio spectrum.
Deep generative models, such as variational autoencoder
(V AE), generative adversarial networks (GANs), and diffusion
model [24], have been widely utilized in mmWave-related
research work. The V AE [21], [25] and GAN [6], [18], [22]
have shown excellent ability to reconstruct the high-frequency
bands that are missing due to limited frequency sensing range
and low sampling rate. These models are popularly developed
for the generation of high-fidelity images, including human
faces and landscapes. The distinction between these models
lies in the quality and time-consuming nature of the generated
images. The images generated by the GAN are typically
superior to those generated by the V AE in terms of detail. The
diffusion model is comparable to the GAN in the details of the
generated images. However, it requires multiple iterations dur-
ing the generation process, resulting in larger time-consuming
costs. In a comprehensive comparison, this article employs
the GAN structure, which generates high-quality samples
efficiently.
In this article, we propose an improved spectrum reconstruc-
tion network based on pix2pix network structure [26] to solve
the transformation issue. The pix2pix network structure is a
GAN variant, and the network structure is illustrated in Fig. 6.
The discriminator uses a full convolutional neural network.
The generator consists of four downsampling blocks, a middle
block, and four upsampling blocks. These blocks all have the
same network structure. To improve the readability of restored
audio, we further consider that the frequency components of
the spectrum are correlated on the time axis. We interpret
this correlation as the contextual relationship of frequencies
on the time axis and learn it through a feature extraction
block. Fig. 7 is the internal structure of the proposed feature
extraction block. The feature extraction block is characterized
by its residual structure and MSA. The MSA [27] allows the
model to consider the relationship between input elements in
different perspectives, thereby extracting effective information
about the contextual relationship. In addition, the residual
structure is helpful to avoid gradient disappearance or gra-
dient explosion. The structure learns the temporal correlations
between low-frequency and high-frequency components, and
we substantiate the effectiveness of this structure on an audio
reconstruction task in ablation experiments. We use the feature
extraction block as the base structure for the upsampling
block, the middle block, and the downsampling block. When
the feature extraction block turns into the upsampling block,
the upsampling layer uses the nearest interpolation algorithm
to expand the input features ( h, w− →2h, 2w). When the
feature extraction block turns into the downsampling block,
the downsampling layer is the average pooling layer to shrink
the input features ( h, w− →(h/2), (w/2)). And when the
feature extraction block becomes the middle block, the upsam-
pling/downsampling layer is not used.
1) Network Structure: The generator uses U-Net as the
network structure [see Fig. 6(a)]. First, a convolutional layer
is used to extend the input data channel to 64, then the
high-dimensional features are extracted and reconstructed by
four upsampling blocks, one middle block, and four down-
sampling blocks, and finally the high-dimensional features
are compressed to a single channel by a convolutional layer
as the output of the reconstructed spectrum. The generator
predicts the high-frequency components, enhances the low-
frequency components, and outputs the complete and clean
audio spectrum. The upsampling block, the middle block, and
the downsampling block are composed of feature extraction
blocks, with the difference lying in the variation of network
layer parameters. The discriminator follows the design of
PatchGAN [see Fig. 6(b)], which is a fully convolutional
network with a kernel size of 4 × 4 and a stride size of

C. Data Resampling for Data Augmentation
In practice, the detected vibration signal may suffer from dc
offset, which makes the center of the circle deviate from the
coordinate origin. As shown in Fig. 10(a), an ideal vibration
signal in the in-phase and quadrature (IQ) domain is an arc
with the origin as the center of the circle, and the central angles
of the arc are proportional to their amplitudes.
The dc offset distorts the true phase change θ into θ′.
A widely used method for correcting dc offset is to transform
the dc offset estimation problem as a circle fitting problem,
where a geometry-based circle fitting algorithm is first applied
to estimate the center of the circular arc, and then translating
the center to the origin of the coordinates. However, the
accuracy of this simple circle fitting method correlated with
the distribution of the samples. The fitting accuracy is higher
with the more discrete distribution of samples.
The smartphone body will generate a tiny resonance (about
7 µm) under the vibration of the speaker, and the vibration
signal is a small arc (samples are highly concentrated) in
the IQ domain. In this case, the estimation results of the
geometry-based circle fitting method are unstable and inac-
curate. Similar to mmVib [16], a data resampling method is
exploited to augment the data by multiple observations of
the simultaneous vibration for robust dc offset compensation.
As shown in Fig. 10(b), a fixed size window is sliding over the
radar raw data for new sampling mode, and each resampled
result is a subchirp. Suppose the size of the sliding window
is L, the step size is S, and G is sliding time. In light of the
above, the resampled data is depicted as follows:

B. Data Collection
Digits in speech contain a large amount of private informa-
tion, such as phone numbers and voice authentication codes.
To verify the feasibility of Vib2audio for eavesdropping on
digital information, we use the AudioMNIST as the audio
data source. The AudioMNIST [39] consists of 30 000 audio
samples of English digits (0–9) from 60 different speakers.
Each speaker provides 50 audio clips in different tones of
voice. The samples from AudioMNIST dataset were played
on a smartphone, and the reflected signals from the sound
source were captured by an mmWave radar. We collected
approximately 20 000 pairs of data. Each pair of data contains
a sound source reflected radar data and a corresponding audio
data from human voice.
C. Training
1) Data Augmentation: During the model training,
we employed the noise suppression method and random
horizontal shift to augment the data. We use a noise
suppression method called spectral gating [40], [41] to
alleviate radar noise, which works by calculating the
spectrogram of a signal (reference noise) and estimating a
noise threshold for each frequency band. This noise threshold
is used to gate noise below the frequency-varying threshold.
The random horizontal shift augments the diversity of the
data and makes the model insensitive to positional variations.
2) Training Details: The training process of the spectrum
reconstruction network is shown in Algorithm 1. We use
the Adam algorithm [42] to optimize the generator and the
discriminator, both with a learning rate of 0.0002. We set batch
size to 1 and trained 150 epochs on a server with a NVIDIA
RTX 3080. We utilize cosine annealing schedule during the
network training, where the learning rate decays to 0.0001 in
150 epochs.

VII. D ISCUSSION
A. Performance Discussion
The overall performance evaluation in Section VI-A demon-
strates that Vib2audio has the outstanding ability to reconstruct
recognizable human audio from microvibration by mmWave
sensing, not only under the evaluation of reference-based
traditional metrics but also unreferenced deep learning tech-
niques. However, there are differences in the performance
of these two Vib2audio objective evaluation methods. The
quality evaluation results of the recovered sound under NISQA
model outperform the evaluation of referenced-based tradi-
tional metrics (e.g., the MAE of NISQA is 3.6%, MCD score
is 7.6, still within the recognizable and acceptable range of
metrics). There are two main explanations to this phenomenon:
1) the reconstructed audio is the same digit content but maybe
different speakers or tone with origin and 2) reference-based
traditional metrics focus on assessing the similarity between
the reference and test signals, whereas unreferenced speech
quality evaluation models concentrate only on the quality of
the test signal. Therefore, two different speakers or tones
of audio have close evaluation values on an unreferenced
speech quality evaluation model like NISQA and more
mediocre evaluation values on traditional reference-based
metrics.
B. Performance Improvement
In our Vib2audio system, we focus the experiment on
the reconstruction of English digital audio. Since our final
goal is to build a voice-aware system using mmWave radar
and GAN technologies, we will extend the database to a
wider vocabulary in our future work. Nonetheless, Vib2audio’s
reconstruction results for digital audio have achieved exciting
overall performance, strongly supporting that the Vib2audio
system of sound recovery with microvibration sensing via
mmWave radar is efficient.
C. Hardware Limitations
While Vib2audio system demonstrates promising perfor-
mance in audio reconstruction, its effectiveness is inherently
constrained by the limitations of commercial mmWave radar
hardware, which was not specifically designed for audio vibra-
tion sensing. The system’s upper frequency detection limit
is about 1300 Hz, which is far below the human auditory
range of 20 Hz to 20 kHz. This results in a loss of the high-
frequency component of the radar detected vibration spectrum,
which directly affects the fidelity of the reconstructed audio.
Applications requiring full-bandwidth recovery (e.g., emotion
recognition) may thus face quality degradation. Besides, the
limited number of radar antennas has a detrimental effect
on the system’s ability to detect targets at extreme angles.
Despite the hardware limitations, the success of Vib2audio on
constrained hardware demonstrates the enormous potential of
mmWave radar in cross-domain applications. In the future, the
range of application scenarios can be expanded further through
customized hardware design and lightweight deployment of
Vib2audio system.
VIII. C ONCLUSION
In this work, we propose a robust mmWave-based sound
sensing system that combines the mmWave-based vibration
sensing technique and deep generative models to reconstruct
the vibration signal to human audio. Due to limited microdis-
placement sensing capability and radar hardware constraints,
we encounter a significant frequency band missing ([1300 Hz,
20 kHz]) in audio reconstruction. To settle the audio recon-
struction issue, we deploy a proposed feature extraction block
structure to learn the contextual relationships of frequencies in
the time dimension. Our ablation experiment shows that this
feature extraction block structure improves the audio recon-
struction performance by 6%. Our objective and subjective
evaluation of extensive experimental results shows that the
reconstructed audio by Vib2audio had an MAE of 3.6% in the
NISQA and achieved a subjective identifiability assessment
score of 3.48. We also perform the robustness of Vib2audio
with the impact of source localization, background noise, and
multisource. All the experiments demonstrate that Vib2audio
can robustly reconstruct recognizable human voice audio from
microvibration. In light of the continued expansion of the
