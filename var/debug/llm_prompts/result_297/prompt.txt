A Vibration Signal Enhancement Scheme for
mmWave-based Sound Eavesdropping
Shaoying Wang, Xiaonan Zhang
Department of Computer Science, Florida State University, USA
Email: sw22bh@fsu.edu, xzhang@cs.fsu.edu
Abstract—The emergence of millimeter wave (mmWave) sens-
ing in sound eavesdropping poses a signiﬁcant threat to speech
privacy. It exploits the fact that speech can induce vibrations in
objects, which in turn changes the phase of the reﬂected mmWave
signal. Through extracting the phase change, the attacker is able
to rebuild the speech. Conventional methods of phase change
extraction, mainly based on circle ﬁtting, often fail to consider the
phase distortion caused by speech, which decreases the quality
of reconstructed speech. T o ﬁll this gap, this paper proposes
an innovative mmWave-based sound eavesdropping framework.
The novelty lies in a vibration signal enhancement scheme to
calibrate the phase distortion, which consists of the speech
activity detection and the constrained circle ﬁtting. In particular ,
speech activity detection divides the signal into speech segment
and non-speech segment. Subsequently, the constrained circle
ﬁtting utilizes the ﬁtted circle radius of the non-speech segment
to determine the circle center of the speech segment, enabling
precise phase change extraction. Our experimental results reveal
that our proposed framework is able to recover speech with a
similarity of 0.44 to the original speech and boost the signal-
to-noise ratio (SNR) for sound-induced vibration signal by 13%.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Sound eavesdropping is considered a very serious problem
in real life because of the unencrypted nature of sound, leading
it to be one of the main sources of information leakage.
With the development of smart homes, voice assistants have
been popularized in many users’ homes. When users leverage
voice assistants for authentication or conversation, private
information such as personal information and passwords are
highly likely to be leaked. In addition, the convenience of
online meetings, i.e., meetings that are not restricted by
location, has led to an increasing number of people choosing
to meet on online meeting applications such as Zoom, Skype,
etc. Conversations in a meeting will be transmitted over a
loudspeaker. They may contain some commercially sensitive
information, such as bid prices or important information about
the type of decisions that the company is making. All the above
information leakage causes indelible damage to individuals
and businesses. To preserve speech privacy, a soundproof room
is a promising solution for speakers [1].
Nevertheless, sound waves cause vibrations in the speaker
itself or surrounding objects. By detecting those sound-induced
vibrations, attackers are able to reconstruct the sound. One
type of sound eavesdropping method [2]–[6] uses motion sen-
sors, e.g., gyroscope and accelerometer, to sense the vibration.
These techniques typically require either direct attachment to
the victim’s device or have to be placed in the same room
as the sound source. As the second type of method, vision-
based approaches [7] employ high-speed cameras to detect
sound-induced vibration. However, it is highly susceptible to
light conditions and, thus, can be easily obscured. Recently,
wireless-based methods have gained signiﬁcant attention ow-
ing to their ability to penetrate barriers and operate indepen-
dently of light conditions. These techniques leverage changes
in reﬂected wireless signals, such as WiFi [8], RFID [9], and
mmWave [10], to infer sound-induced vibrations, enabling the
recreation of sound information.
Nowadays, leveraging a millimeter wave (mmWave) sensor
for sound eavesdropping has been popularized due to its
capabilities of measuring vibrations at the micrometer level.
The mmWave-based sound eavesdropping generally begins
with a range FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) of the reﬂected
signals to locate the vibrating target and obtain its reﬂected
signal. Subsequently, the phase change of the reﬂected signal
from the vibrating object is extracted to reconstruct the sound
information. Accurately extracting the phase change of the
reﬂected signal plays a critical role in sound restoration.
AmbiEar [11] and mmEve [12] both employ a technique of
ﬁtting circles to all data samples in the IQ plane to ﬁnd
the center of the circle and then transform the coordinates
to remove static clutter, enabling the accurate extraction of
phase changes. Nevertheless, the above circle ﬁtting approach
fails to account for phase distortion, causing inaccurate phase
change extraction. Upon analyzing phase distortion in the IQ
plane, mmEavesdropper [10] explores a segmentation-based
ﬁtting method to calibrate the vibration signal. Despite this
innovative approach, mmEavesdropper supposes that the phase
distortion is caused mainly by FFT calculation but ignores the
environmental factors, such as speech activity.
In this paper, we propose a novel mmWave-based speech
eavesdropping framework. Our main novelty lies in designing
a vibration signal enhancement scheme to correct phase dis-
tortions resulting from the speech activity. The enhancement
scheme includes speech activity detection and constrained
circle ﬁtting. The former method divides the signal into speech
and non-speech segments, whereas the latter method calibrates
the phase distortions in the IQ phase. In particular, based on
our ﬁndings that the phase changes of the speech and non-
speech segments share the same radius but have different circle
centers, we ﬁrst apply the conventional circle ﬁtting to ﬁnd the

V. VIBRA TION SIGNAL ENHANCEMENT SCHEME
In this paper, we propose a sound eavesdropping framework
with the main focus on the design of the signal enhancement
scheme. The attacker utilizes an FMCW radar to emit a
mmWave signal. By analyzing the signals reﬂected from a
target object within the room, the attacker can reconstruct the
speech content. Our framework is shown in Fig. 1. Initially,
a range FFT is applied to detect the target object. Once the
target object is localized, the attacker extracts the reﬂected
signal and deploys the speech activity detection to differentiate
between speech segment and non-speech segment signals. A
circle ﬁtting method is then applied to the non-speech segment
signal to determine a reference radius. This reference radius
serves as a crucial constraint for subsequent circle ﬁtting on
the speech segment signal. Finally, we adopt the U-Net neural
network on the extracted signals to denoise the audio ﬁle.
A. Target Localization
The mmWave sensor captures reﬂected signals from var-
ious objects in the conference room. To accurately localize
the target object, we conduct a 256-point range FFT on
the collected signals. FFT aids in identifying the frequency
domain’s peak, which corresponds to the range bin of the target
object. Subsequently, we extract and concatenate signals from
this speciﬁc range bin across consecutive chirps, forming the
vibration signal SIF .
B. Speech Activity Detection
The extracted vibration signal SIF contains the signals
captured with and without the speech. According to the
observation in Section IV-B, speech activity causes phase
distortion. In order to accurately extract the phase of the
speech segment, it becomes critical to ﬁrst separate the
speech segment from the non-speech segment and then process
them respectively. Theoretically, the speech segment exhibits
signiﬁcant phase changes due to sound-induced vibrations.
However, it is difﬁcult to distinguish speech segments in the
time domain since phase changes due to the mmWave sensor
hardware itself always being present throughout the signal.
To address this issue, we distinguish speech segments from
the perspective of the spectrogram. We perform a short-time
Fourier transform (STFT) on the phase change. From Fig. 7a,
the speech components concentrate on the low frequency of the
STFT spectrogram. Hence, we apply a band-pass ﬁlter within
50-1000 Hz to preserve the speech component and ﬁlter out
most of the high-frequency noise. After the bandpass ﬁlter,
the phase changes of the speech segments in the time domain
become noticeable. Given that the envelope is an important
parameter in describing the ﬂuctuations of a signal, we employ
it to detect the speech segment. The effectiveness of this
speech activity detection approach is depicted in Fig. 7b.

D. U-Net Denoising Network
After extracting the vibration signal, we convert it directly
to an audio ﬁle. However, the mixture of self-noise from
mmWave hardware with the vibration signal makes it difﬁcult
to understand the speech content in the audio ﬁle. Traditional
denoising methods can’t effectively remove the self-noise, so
we turn to deep learning for a solution. U-Net [13], as a
deep convolutional autoencoder, was originally designed for
biomedical image segmentation because it can accurately cap-
ture and reconstruct complex image details. This characteristic
makes it suitable for processing sound spectrograms with ﬁne
features. Therefore, we apply U-Net to estimate and remove
the noise component from the sound spectrogram in order to
enhance the speech recovery quality further.
VI. EXPERIMENTAL EV ALUA TION
A. Experiment Setting
Implementation: We implement our system using the
mmWave sensor AWR1843 and a data-capture adapter
DCA1000EVM. We enable one transmitter and four receiver
antennas of the mmWave sensor. Our experimental setup is
shown in Fig. 5, where we use tinfoil as the reﬂector to
capture sound-induced vibrations caused by the loudspeaker.
The captured data are transmitted to the laptop, and Matlab
is used for signal processing. The U-Net model is trained on
an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 GPU with TensorFlow. The
γ for the constrained circle ﬁtting method is set to 0.1.
Training Dataset: We use synthetic data to train the U-Net
network. To generate the synthetic training data, we select the
LibriSpeech corpus [17] as the clean audio and mix them with
the mmWave signal in the absence of the speech every second.
The mixed data is then converted into a spectrogram matrix of
the size128×128. In total, we generated 40,000 spectrograms
of mixed data for training purposes.
T est Dataset:We select the audio clips in the LibriSpeech
corpus as the test data (non-overlapping with the training data).
These selected audio clips were played using a commercial
loudspeaker at varying volume levels (75 dB, 65 dB, and
55 dB, respectively). To capture the corresponding vibration
signals, we deploy an mmWave sensor to receive signals
reﬂected from the reﬂector mentioned in Section IV-A. We
collect signals with a duration exceeding 2000 seconds, which
will be made publicly available to support further research in
mmWave-based eavesdropping.

C. Overall Performance
We ﬁrst evaluate the performance of our designed con-
strained circle ﬁtting method. Fig. 8 compares the SNRs of the
extracted vibration-related signals before the U-Net denoising,
where the speaker volume is set to 55 dB, 65 dB, and 75
dB, respectively. With the conventional circle ﬁtting method,
the corresponding SNRs are 2.6721dB, 3.1213 dB, and 3.6347
dB. In contrast, our method yields SNRs of 3.1132 dB, 3.4763
dB, and 4.0906 dB, respectively, which is an average increase
of 13%. These results imply the precision of our proposed
method in extracting phase changes, demonstrating its efﬁcacy
in enhancing signal quality.

VII. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we achieved sound eavesdropping on speech
in a soundproof room using a commercial mmWave sensor.
We enhanced the extracted vibration signal quality by cali-
brating the phase change distortion caused by the speech. The
results of our experiments show that our scheme dramatically
improves the SNR of the speech-induced vibration signal.
Furthermore, the reconstructed speech exhibits a signiﬁcant
similarity when compared to the original speech, demonstrat-
ing the effectiveness of our attack in real life.
