Abstract—In this paper, we introduce mmOrbit, a mmWave-
based rotor orbit measurement system that can estimate rotor orbit
by analyzing machinery surface vibration. To measure the 2D rotor
orbit, two synchronized mmWave radars are deployed to build the
orbit from different viewpoints. The existing literature shows that
the micro-displacement measurement accuracy of mmWave radar
is not enough to meet the application requirements of micron-level
resolution without appropriate ﬁne-gained processing methods.
Therefore, we propose a three-step vibration displacement extrac-
tion algorithm with sliding table to extract mechanical vibration
micro-displacement and increase measurement precision. Accurate
mechanical vibration displacements in two vertical directions are
used to estimate the 2D rotor orbit. Our extensive experiments
indicate that mmOrbit can accurately measure mechanical vibra-
tion micro-displacement with an error of 4.4 µmf o rt h e 80th
percentile. Furthermore, mmOrbit can estimate 2D rotor orbit with
high precision, showing an orbit eccentricity error of 7%, an orbit
direction error of 7◦, and a disjoint area proportion of 17% for the
80th percentile. The imbalance detection experiment veriﬁes the
accuracy and dependability of our measured rotor orbit.
Index Terms—mmWave, micro displacement sensing, rotor orbit
measurement, DC offset.

is detrimental to stability, operational efﬁciency, and lifespan.
Therefore, rotor imbalance diagnosis is important to improve
the efﬁciency and extend the service life of the machinery. Rotor
imbalance is attributed to various factors, such as uneven density

The primary step in detecting rotor imbalance faults is to

motion, constructed by synthesizing the multi-directional radial
displacement of the rotor. The rotor orbit provides a snapshot
of the dynamic motion of the rotor center, which contains both
critical phase and kinematic information, making it a powerful
feature for identifying imbalance faults[5], [6].

II. RELATED WORK
In this section, we review the outstanding work related to
RF-based vibration sensing in terms of mechanical vibration
sensing and micro-motion measurement.
A. Mechanical Vibration Sensing
Recently, several works have been proposed to utilize mature
RF technology for industrial machinery vibration sensing, such
as RFID, WiFi, and radar. TagSound[17] is a vibration sensing
system based on RFID that detected harmonic backscatter from
tags to recover high-frequency (2500 Hz) and tiny mechanical
vibration (2 mm) accurately. However, the tags used in RFID
need to be close to the vibration source (less than 0.5 m), making
them challenging to deploy and potentially unsafe for vibrating
equipment. WiFi is a pervasive networking device in life, and
its ease of deployment and low cost have led researchers to
investigate its feasibility for vibration sensing. Wei et al.[18]
utilized an acoustic-radio transformation (ART) algorithm to
recover the speaker’s sound by inspecting the subtle disturbance
caused by their vibrations on the WiFi radio signals. The ART
algorithm has limited anti-interference capability and is ineffec-
tive in eliminating the disturbances when moving object is close
to the speaker or WiFi device (within 40 cm).

C. DC Offset
DC offset presents a frequent issue in the processing of radar
signals. An ideal vibration signal in the IQ domain is an arc
centered at the origin, and the arc angles are proportional to their
amplitudes. In practice, the detected vibration signal contains a
static component (i.e. DC offset) that makes the center deviate
from the origin illustrated in Fig.4(a). The impact of DC offset
Fig. 4. The impact of DC offset on IQ signal (a) and phase signal (b).
Fig. 5. Circle ﬁtting results (a) and corresponding phase signal (b) when
moving the radar front-end.
on phase detection accuracy can be subdivided into two distinct
categories: 1) the imprecise radius of the circle center, resulting
in wrong amplitude; 2) the deviation of the circle center direction
distorts the phase signal. A widely used method for correcting
DC offset is to transform the problem into an estimation of the
circle center. The circle center is estimated using a circle ﬁtting
algorithm, and the DC offset is removed by translating the center
to the origin of the coordinates. However, when the displacement
variation is minimal (around a few dozen microns), the phase
change of the IF signal is insufﬁcient to ﬁt a complete arc,
which results in a signiﬁcant deviation in the center estimation.
In this case, the estimation results of the circle ﬁtting method
are unstable and inaccurate. A practical solution to obtain a
sufﬁcient arc is to increase the relative motion by moving the
radar front end. To move the radar front end, we mounted the
radar on a sliding table. The sliding table moves back and forth
at a constant speed while the radar collects data.
The Fig. 5(a) depicts the results of circle ﬁtting when the
radar front end is moving. The movement of the radar front
end achieves a more accurate circle ﬁt result for the DC offset.
Additionally, Fig. 5(b) shows the corresponding phase signal,
where the micro-displacement (mechanical vibration) is super-
imposed on the large-scale sliding table movement. The sliding
table is an effective solution for the DC offset problem in the
micro-displacement scenario. We have applied the sliding table
in mmOrbit.

A. Synchronized Radar Raw Data Acquisition
Fig. 7 shows a diagram of our data collection scenario. During
the data collection process, two mmWave radars are positioned
at a perpendicular angle to one another and oriented directly
towards the vibrating machinery. Radar uses electromagnetic
waves to sense the displacement state of a target. However,
signals cannot reach the radar receiver on the sides or back of an
object, so it cannot directly sense vibrations in these areas. To
measure rotor orbit, we utilize two mmWave radar sensors and
control them using a radar synchronisation device, each sensor
provides a 1D view of the vibration source. When the two radars
are positioned at a 90◦ to one another, their combined ﬁeld
of view encompasses a monitoring area exceeding180◦.T h e
vibration information within this monitoring area is sufﬁcient
to construct a 2D coordinate plane that accurately describes
the rotor orbit. In addition, both radars are ﬁrmly attached to
the sliding table, which improves the accuracy of displacement
detection.
A single sensor collects information about the motion of the
rotor in one direction (e.g., axial or radial), which is limited
in characterizing the rotor’s motion in 3D space. Two or more
sensors deployed at different locations can capture the multi-
dimensional motion information of the rotor in 3D space. The
traditional sensor-based rotor orbit measurement method shows
that it is sufﬁcient to analyze the dynamic 2D rotor orbit with two
radial displacement sensors positioned perpendicularly to each
other. Although, in speciﬁc application scenarios, additional
sensor can enhance the analysis of rotor state. For instance, the
axial displacement sensor [29] (deployed along the direction
of the rotor axis) is essential for monitoring key parameters
such as axial thrust[30] and axial thermal expansion[31].I n
addition, more radial displacement sensors improve the ability
to monitor the global state of axes in the case of complex systems
with long or multi-axis rotor system. In our scenario, two radial
displacement sensors are adequate to fulﬁll our requirements for
rotor orbit monitoring.

A. Implementation and Methodology
Synchronized Data Acquisition System:Fig. 16 shows our
synchronized data acquisition system. We use two radar sensors,
the IWR1843BOOST and the AWR1843BOOST [35] from
Texas Instruments. These two sensors are almost identical in
hardware speciﬁcations. The Arduino Uno Rev3 serves as a
radar synchronisation device, coordinating the acquisition of
two radars by simultaneously transmitting high-level signals.
The sliding table system includes the sliding table, power mod-
ule, and drive module. The power supply module provides
power to the sliding table, while the driver module controls
its movement. The radar and sliding table are tightly secure
to prevent excessive vibration of the radar panel while in
motion.The data collection scenario of our synchronized data
acquisition system is illustrated in Fig.17. Both sensors are
ﬁrmly attached to the sliding table at a90◦ to the vibration
source.
The synchronization technique we used is based on the
hardware synchronisation principle of the TI radar. Due to the
high-level of integration of the TI radar, measuring synchro-
nization times with reference values is difﬁcult. According to
TI’s technical document[36], the actual delay value will be
accurate to +−1 ms for software-based synchronization, and
the hardware-triggered synchronization method (the one we
used) has a synchronization delay of much less than 1 ms.
In addition, the prevailing view on TI’s technical forums is
that hardware-triggered pulses via the SYNC_in pin are by far
the most reliable radar synchronization method. Therefore, we
believe that the synchronization of our system is sufﬁcient to
support our experiments.
Radar Setup:In our experiments, we used the COTS mmWave
radars IWR1843BOOST and AWR1843BOOST, which have a
4 GHz bandwidth (76∼81 GHz) and integrate an antenna array
with 2 transmit (Tx) and 4 receive (Rx) antenna arrays. We
partitioned the 4 GHz bandwidth into two equal segments to
prevent interference between the radars, each utilizing 2 GHz
bandwidth. Table I provides a more detailed conﬁguration of
the radar parameters. The raw samples were acquired using a TI
DCA1000EVM.
Sliding Table Setup:On our sliding table device, we conﬁg-
ured a stepper motor. We set the stepper motor to move 2 mm at a
uniform speed within 1s We suggest against setting a high speed,
as it could exacerbate and prolong the vibration of the radar
panel. Conversely, setting the speed too low isn’t recommended
either, as it would necessitate more data frames to guarantee an
adequate arc.

VI. DISCUSS
The overall performance evaluation in SectionV-B1 demon-
strated the excellent capability of mmOrbit for both micro-
displacement measurement and rotor orbit estimation. However,
We have discovered that the mmOrbit has some limitations. This
chapter analyzes the advantages and limitations of mmOrbit.
A. Advantages
The accuracy of DC offset estimation is low in micro-
displacement scenarios. mmOrbit introduced a sliding table to
compensate for the DC offset by increasing the relative mo-
tion between the radar and the machine. Faced with the same
problem, GWaltz [22] estimates a more accurate DC offset
by restricting the circle radius to a speciﬁc range based on a
priori knowledge of the range of vibration displacement. This
method is only effective when the vibration displacement is
within a set range. When the mechanical vibration displacement
exceeds the preset radius limit, a signiﬁcant displacement error
occurs, which is the reason why GWaltz is underperformed in
extracting large-displacement vibrations. mmVib[21] addresses
the challenge of DC offset with a different solution, which
employs a multi-frequency property of the MSC to generate
chirp signals with varying initial frequencies. These chirp sig-
nals form longer but discontinuous arcs in the IQ plane by
creating multiple observations on the same vibration. In con-
trast, mmOrbit utilized a sliding table to fundamentally solve
the problem of the inferior accuracy of DC offset estimation
in micro-displacement scenarios, thus achieving high-precision
micro-displacement measurement. In addition, the sliding table
provided a controllable uniform motion so that the vibration
signal presented a standard circular arc shape in the IQ do-
main (as shown in Fig.9), which was conducive to improving
the accuracy of DC offset estimation. Although the sliding
table causes additional interference (dr(t)), this interference
can be effectively suppressed by our proposed three-step vi-
bration displacement extraction algorithm. Experiments show
that the micro-displacement measurement error of mmOrbit is
only 4.4 μma tt h e80th percentile, verifying that the beneﬁt
of accuracy improvement from the sliding table outweighs the
effect of interference.
B. Limitations
The principal constraints on our work lie in the mmOrbit
prototype system itself and in the imperfections of the imbal-
ance detection experiments. We have developed an experimental
prototype of the mmOrbit that utilizes two synchronous radars
to acquire 2D vibration data and detects high-precision rotor
orbit with the help of a sliding table. However, the proto-
type utilized a universal mechanical sliding table that was not
speciﬁcally designed for the mmOrbit, leading to the system
becoming cumbersome. Developing a customized mini-slider
could greatly simplify the system. Furthermore, the movement
of the radar panel unavoidably introduced additional interfer-
ences (e.g.,dr(t)). Although we proposed a three-step vibration
displacement extraction algorithm to mitigate interferences, it
would eliminate mechanical fault features when both fault fea-
ture and interference were present in the observation window.
In our imbalance detection experiment, our purpose is verifying
the accuracy and reliability of the orbit detected by the mmOrbit
system, rather than to classify mechanical fault. And due to
the limitations of the experimental conditions, we regret that
we are unable to safely and controllably simulate various types
of imbalanced states, including parallel misalignment, angular
misalignment, and loosening. Considering the above reasons, a
more comprehensive identiﬁcation of those different types of
imbalanced states was not sufﬁciently perfect.
Therefore, the future work involves the following aspects:
r Simplifying the sliding table device to reduce its complex-
ity and improve its feasibility for practical applications;
r Designing more intelligent and adaptive algorithms to ef-
fectively solve the problem when fault feature and inter-
ference coexist;
r Using professional equipment such as a bearing failure
simulation test bench to simulate various types of imbal-
anced states, and explore more detailed classiﬁcation under
the imbalanced state.

estimation challenge, but this increases the complexity of the
system. The recent ﬁndings of Gu [39] are fascinating, and
the proposed frequency-reconﬁgurable technique offer novel

simplify the mmOrbit system.
VII. CONCLUSION
In this work, we presented a rotor orbit estimation system
based on mmWave technology. The system utilized mmWave-
based vibration sensing techniques to estimate the rotor orbit by
analyzing the vibration of the machinery surface. We constructed
a synchronized data acquisition system based on two mmWave
radars and a sliding table device to collect 2D machinery surface
vibration in two vertical directions. To address the challenge of
DC offset in micro-displacement scenarios, we ﬁxed the radar on
sliding table to increase relative displacement. The introduction
of the sliding table resulted in two additional displacement
component in the displacement signal, i.e., the sliding table
displacement and the radar panel vibration displacement. There-
fore, we propose a three-step vibration displacement extraction
algorithm to extract mechanical vibration displacement.
Our experimental results demonstrate that mmOrbit accu-
rately measures mechanical vibration micro-displacement with
an 80th percentile micro-displacement error of 4.4μm. Addi-
tionally, mmOrbit was able to estimate rotor orbit comparable to
high-precision laser sensors, where the80th percentile rotor ec-
centricity error, rotor direction error, and disjoint area proportion
are 7%,7◦, and 17%, respectively. In quantitative distance and
rotational speed experiments, we have validated the robustness
of mmOrbit to micro-displacement measurements of distance
and rotational speed, as well as to orbit estimation.
