Recent advancements in headset technology, coupled with the rising demand for secure, high-quality audio, have made
headsets essential in both professional and personal contexts. However, emerging research highlights the vulnerability
of these devices to sophisticated eavesdropping attacks using mmWave signals, where subtle diaphragm vibrations can
unintentionally reveal playback speech content. Current eavesdropping methods face challenges, including low accuracy
in modeling vibration signals, insufficient resolution in capturing diaphragm vibrations, and audio artifacts from nonlinear
headset interactions, with FFT-based techniques further limited by resolution. To overcome these limitations, we propose the
prototype system, namly mmHSE, which features two key innovations. First, we propose the Spatio-Temporal Segmented
Fitting (STSF) method, which enhances signal clarity by isolating primary diaphragm vibrations through spatiotemporal signal
segmentation. Second, we devise a two-stage integrated approach combining Minimum Variance Distortionless Response
(MVDR) beamforming with the Chirp-Z Transform (CZT), achieving a resolution of 0.04 cm and significantly enhancing
vibration capture precision. Additionally, mmHSE incorporates a conditional Generative Adversarial Network (cGAN) to
refine Mel Spectrograms, enabling effective speech recovery. Experimental results demonstrate that mmHSE outperforms
existing methods, with a 15.4% increase in Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), an 11.4% reduction in Mel Cepstral Distortion
(MCD), a 14.5% improvement in Word Error Rate (WER), and approximately a 15.1% increase in Likert Score, confirming its
effectiveness in enhancing eavesdropping accuracy on headsets.

1 Introduction
The rising demand for high-quality, adaptable audio solutions, coupled with advancements in headset technology, 
has made headsets indispensable across a broad spectrum of personal and professional contexts in today’s digital 
landscape. For instance, the shift toward remote and hybrid work models has particularly underscored the 
necessity for clear audio, positioning headsets as essential tools for effective virtual meetings and collaboration. 
Additionally, headsets enhance immersive experiences in gaming, media streaming, and everyday use, with the 
growing adoption of wireless Bluetooth connectivity further expanding their versatility. According to a 2024 
Statista report, the global headsets market is projected to grow by 2.39% annually from 2024 to 2029, reaching an 
estimated USD 20.2 billion [31].
Unlike conventional loudspeakers that project sound into the environment without privacy safeguards, headsets 
are specifically designed for individual use, prioritizing portability and secure sound transmission. While side-
channel eavesdropping attacks on loudspeakers exploit the acoustic emissions and sound-induced vibrations 
generated during playback [11, 20], enabling eavesdroppers to reconstruct audio by analyzing indirect vibrations 
from the loudspeaker and its surroundings, headsets deliver sound directly to the user’s ears, significantly 
reducing sound leakage. This design minimizes environmental sound interception, making headsets ideal for 
confidential voice communication and offering a significant privacy advantage over loudspeakers by reducing 
eavesdropping risk [7].

mmHSE: Enhanced Eavesdropping Attack on Headsets Leveraging COTS mmWave Radar • 51:3
Implementing side-channel eavesdropping attacks on headsets using mmWave signals is challenging due to
the faint, often distorted vibrations caused by sound, which are further obscured by the headset’s structure. These
diaphragm vibrations typically range from tens to hundreds of micrometers, rarely exceeding 1 𝑚𝑚, making
precise detection and speech recovery difficult. Upon analyzing the existing approach, we identify opportunities
for further enhancing the performance of side-channel eavesdropping attack on headsets.
1. Refined Model of Vibration Signals : The current approach directly captures and analyzes vibration
signals from the outer shell of a headset to extract encoded speech information. However, as shown on the right
side of Fig. 1, nonlinear interactions between the shell and diaphragm can lead to the amplification, attenuation,
or generation of new resonant frequencies, resulting in audio artifacts. Moreover, the system is susceptible to
environmental noise, wear, and other factors, all of which diminish signal reliability. Variations in the shell’s
material and structure lead to inconsistent vibration frequencies and amplitudes in response to external forces
and audio signals, further complicating accurate analysis. These challenges underscore the need for an advanced
method to analyze and process vibration signals.
2. Enhanced Resolution in Vibration Capture : The current approach uses Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) for
headset localization, locking onto its corresponding range bin and accurately tracking phase variations to capture
continuous vibrations induced by audio signals. However, FFT’s performance is constrained by sampling rate
limitations, resulting in a uniform spectrum across the frequency band. In practical applications, COTS mmWave
radar with a 4 𝐺𝐻𝑧 bandwidth achieves only 4 𝑐𝑚resolution, which is insufficient for precise vibration distance
measurement and accurate signal extraction.
To enhance system performance, we propose targeted approaches that address these two aforementioned key
issues. First, we analyze the complex vibration dynamics in headsets, where diaphragm vibrations transmit to the
outer shell, creating secondary vibrations that introduce interference into the captured signal . Through preliminary
experiments, we validate our approach and draw relevant conclusions. To isolate and extract the primary vibrations
generated by the diaphragm, we propose Spatio-Temporal Segmented Fitting (STSF) method. This technique
segments the vibration signal based on spatiotemporal characteristics, allowing for more effective separation of
primary diaphragm components and improved alignment with speech segments, thereby significantly enhancing
both signal quality and audio clarity. Regarding the second issue, we focus on accurately capturing the subtle
vibrations of the headset diaphragm, and design a two-stage integrated approach. Specifically, we first apply the
Minimum Variance Distortionless Response (MVDR) adaptive beamforming method [40] to focus energy on the
target headset while suppressing noise. Then, the Chirp-Z Transform (CZT) achieves a distance resolution of
0.04 𝑐𝑚, 100 times higher than FFT, without significantly increasing computational complexity. This enhancement
enables precise detection of minute vibrations, improving signal extraction and speech recovery.
Building on this foundation, we develop a novel side-channel eavesdropping attack system mmHSE, which
combines MVDR adaptive beamforming with the CZT technique to achieve 0.04 𝑐𝑚resolution, capturing subtle
diaphragm vibrations. The system also utilizes our proposed STSF method to segment vibration signals, enhancing
signal quality and clarity. Finally,mmHSE incorporates a conditional Generative Adversarial Network (cGAN)
with a self-attention mechanism, utilizing Mel Spectrograms as conditional input for data augmentation. This
approach mitigates high-frequency loss, facilitating accurate reconstruction of speech content.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
(1) We identify two key areas for improvement in the existing system: a refined vibration signal model and
enhanced resolution in vibration capture. By differentiating diaphragm and shell vibrations, we propose
the STSF method for isolating and extracting diaphragm vibrations through spatiotemporal segmentation,
improving signal quality and clarity. Additionally, we develop a two-stage approach combining MVDR
adaptive beamforming and CZT to accurately capture subtle diaphragm vibrations.

2 RELATED WORK
2.1 Audio Eavesdropping Using Millimeter Waves Signals
Direct Millimeter Wave-Based Audio Eavesdropping: Recent advances in audio eavesdropping leverage
mmWave signals for high-precision audio recovery [5, 38]. Basak et al. propose mmWave-Whisper, which achieves
full-corpus automated speech recognition (ASR) on phone calls eavesdropped at a distance using commercial
mmWave radars [3]. Lin et al. develop a probe near the speaker’s throat to capture mixed vibrations, enhancing
vocal vibration extraction with wavelet techniques [34]. Their wavelet-based approach enhances vocal vibrations
for monitoring multiple speakers, while Wang et al. detect smartphone-induced vibrations in earpiece mode,
extending eavesdropping range with mmWave and software optimizations [36]. Liu et al. propose a multimodal
system combining mmWave signals and microphone data to improve audio reconstruction accuracy [21], while
Feng et al. reconstruct numeric and alphabetic characters from speaker systems using mmWave radar, though
their method doesn’t support full text reconstruction [ 10]. Hu et al. present MILLIEAR, combining speaker
vibration estimation with cGANs for audio reconstruction, but it struggles with high-frequency sounds in noisy,
multi-directional environments due to coarse diaphragm localization [13]. Xu et al. develop mmEar, an audio
eavesdropping system for headsets that captures faint vibrations with a faint vibration emphasis method and deep
denoising network [41]. Despite its advances, it struggles with insufficient vibration signals from the headset
shell and resolution limits of FFT, hindering accurate speech characterization.
Indirect Audio Eavesdropping via Ambient Environmental Vibrations: Recent research has shifted from
direct vibration capture at the sound source to indirect audio eavesdropping through vibrations in nearby objects.
Yang et al. developed RF-Parrot, which captures audio transmitted through earphone wires using a miniature
field-effect transistor as a retroreflector, with efficiency linked to the audio signal’s amplitude [42]. Chen et al.
introduced Periscope, which exploits leaked electromagnetic radiation from audio amplifiers as a side-channel
for audio recovery [6]. Zhang et al. demonstrated that sound waves induce subtle vibrations in surrounding
objects, and by directing mmWave radar signals at these objects, they reconstruct audio from the vibrations [44].
Hu et al. expanded detectable targets to lightweight, thin objects like potato chip bags near the sound source,
using digital signal processing for effective audio reconstruction in complex environments [12]. Accurate audio
reconstruction requires target objects that are sensitive, lightweight, and thin, due to the limited amplitude and
attenuation of vibrations in air. These material characteristics are essential for optimizing audio recovery in
indirect eavesdropping scenarios.

3 EAVESDROPPING ATTACK OVERVIEW
3.1 Eavesdropping Attack Scenario
We consider an eavesdropping attack scenario where a victim engages in private voice communication or listens
to confidential messages in public or semi-public spaces, such as offices or cafes. To protect audio privacy, the
victim wears headsets designed to prevent external surveillance. In this context, the victim is assumed to remain
in a stationary or quasi-stationary state, such as sitting, standing, or making minor posture adjustments, while
avoiding dynamic activities like rapid walking or running. This ensures the secure and stable transmission of
audio signals.
The attacker operates under non-invasive conditions, with no control over the victim’s devices, including
the headsets, or the surrounding environment. The attack occurs within a typical 2 𝑚𝑒𝑡𝑒𝑟 range and an angular
range of 20°, reflecting common indoor settings, such as the space between desks or seats in public or semi-public
areas. This setup effectively simulates realistic eavesdropping threats while maintaining both practicality and
concealment.
3.2 Threat Model
The attacker employs commercial mmWave radar devices in conjunction with pre-trained attack models to
passively capture the subtle vibrations of the headset diaphragm caused by audio playback. This non-invasive
process relies solely on the reflection of mmWave signals, enabling the attacker to recover the speech content
transmitted through the headsets in real-time. Notably, the attack is entirely passive and requires no active
participation from the victim. The primary aim of the attacker is to extract sensitive information from the
recovered audio, such as credit card numbers, and confidential commercial data.

method using eighth-degree polynomial regression on 200-point segments to subtract underlying trends. A

low-frequency components for speech reconstruction.
Human speech typically spans a frequency range from 300 𝐻𝑧 to 3.4 𝑘𝐻𝑧 [2], with harmonic components
shaping its spectral structure. In non-tonal languages like English, vowel and consonant resonances occur
primarily between 80 𝐻𝑧 and 1.7 𝑘𝐻𝑧, encompassing most vowel sounds and approximately 62.5% of consonant
resonances. This frequency range is crucial for conveying speech information [ 28]. While traditional speech
processing methods capture frequencies up to 4975 𝐻𝑧 [30], vital for speech clarity, headset applications often
face low SNR, hindering accurate reconstruction of high-frequency components. As shown in Fig.12, testing
with a 25-second sweep tone from 20 𝐻𝑧 to 10 𝑘𝐻𝑧 reveals that, after phase extraction, vibrational signals above
800 𝐻𝑧 are nearly lost. This results in significant degradation of reconstructed speech quality, with high-frequency
content masked by noise, diminishing intelligibility and detail.

To address the limitations, we employed a Conditional Generative Adversarial Network (cGAN) with a UNet
architecture to enhance audio quality. The cGAN generates samples conditioned on specific inputs to exhibit
desired characteristics [8]. As shown in Fig. 13, the cGAN’s generator uses UNet’s skip connections to merge
low-level feature maps from the downsampling path with high-level maps from the upsampling path, capturing
both global context and fine details. A multi-head self-attention mechanism is applied before each skip connection
to improve vocal feature extraction and preserve speech information. We used a 4-head self-attention mechanism
with linear transformations and layer normalization [33]. To improve convergence and address gradient issues,
layer normalization was added after the second convolutional layer of the discriminator. The model was trained
with the Adam optimizer, a learning rate of 0.0002, for 300 epochs.

In the cGAN framework, the generator transforms input feature maps into high-quality speech signals. These
inputs, extracted from the MVDR, CZT, and STSF modules, are normalized and represented as Mel spectrograms.
The generator uses a CNN-based encoder-decoder structure, where the encoder extracts high-dimensional features
and the decoder reconstructs them into speech. Skip connections preserve local spectral details. The generator’s
optimization objective combines adversarial and reconstruction losses, defined as:
L𝐺 = L𝐷 +𝜆L𝐿1. (17)
where L𝐺 is the binary cross-entropy loss, evaluating the adversarial loss by comparing the discriminator’s output
with target labels. L𝐿1 is the L1 loss (mean absolute error), measuring the difference between generated and
input spectrograms, with 𝜆balancing the losses. L𝐷 is the discriminator’s loss, assessing its ability to distinguish
real from generated samples.
The discriminator classifies the authenticity of input signals, including (1) the Mel spectrogram of enhanced
audio from the generator and (2) the Mel spectrogram of real audio. It uses a convolutional neural network to ex-
tract time-frequency features and outputs a probability indicating whether the signal is “real”. The discriminator’s
optimization goal is to minimize the loss function, improving classification accuracy:
L𝐷 = −𝐸𝑥∼𝑝true [log 𝐷(𝑥)]− 𝐸𝑥∼𝑝fake [log(1 −𝐷(𝑥))], (18)
where 𝐷(𝑥)is the discriminator’s probability that the input signal is “real”. and 𝑝true and 𝑝fake represent the
distributions of real and generated samples, respectively.
During training, the generator and discriminator improve through adversarial updates: the discriminator
enhances classification accuracy, while the generator produces more realistic signals. Once trained, the model
processes the Mel spectrogram of vibration data to generate an enhanced version with better acoustic features,
which is then converted into audio using the Griffin-Lim algorithm [25] for efficient phase reconstruction.

In addition, we employed three objective metrics: Mel Cepstral Distortion (MCD) [16], Peak Signal-

recognition by standard speech recognition systems. PSNR measures speech recovery fidelity, with higher values
indicating greater similarity to the original. PSNR values between 20 and 30 indicate low quality, 30 to 40 indicate
medium quality, and above 40 indicate high quality. WER evaluates speech recovery accuracy by comparing the
predicted transcription to the reference, accounting for substitutions, deletions, and insertions. Lower WER values
indicate better recovery, with 0%∼20% reflecting high quality, 20%∼30% medium quality, and above 30% poor
quality. The open-source Google Speech-to-Text API transcribes the recovered speech, and WER is calculated
based on these transcriptions.

6.5.2.12 Impact of Fitting Threshold on Speech Recovery Performance. To evaluate the impact of the fitting
threshold on speech recovery quality, we use both PSNR and MCD as complementary metrics. PSNR increases,
while both MCD and WER decrease, as speech recovery quality improves, reflecting consistent trends across
these metrics. Since PSNR and MCD capture both positive and negative changes in recovery quality, they serve
as reliable indicators for identifying the optimal fitting threshold . Here, we systematically adjust the threshold
from 0° to 90° and conduct multiple experiments, analyzing the resulting variations in speech quality. As shown
in Fig. 28, a fitting threshold of 0° results in clustering failure, preventing differentiation between the two clusters
of data points and leading to suboptimal recovery performance. Notably, within the range of 60° to 90°, PSNR
shows a linear decline, while MCD plateaus; both metrics converge to the values observed at the 0° threshold.
This decline in performance can be attributed to reduced clustering resolution at higher thresholds, hindering
the system’s ability to capture subtle variations in the speech data. The optimal performance for both PSNR and
MCD occurs at a fitting threshold between 35° and 40°, with the best performance specifically at 35°. Therefore,
35° is designated as the optimal threshold for distinguishing between the two clusters in the speech segments.

7 DISCUSSION
Influence of Eavesdropping Distance and Angle : mmWave eavesdropping effectiveness is strongly influ-
enced by both distance and target orientation. Experimental results show that as the distance between the radar
and headset increases, speech recovery quality declines. Beyond a critical distance (e.g., 3 meters), the radar’s
ability to isolate faint vibrations from environmental noise diminishes, leading to degraded speech reconstruc-
tion. Additionally, target orientation affects signal quality: diaphragms facing the radar produce stronger, more
detectable signals, while side or rear orientations result in weaker signals prone to interference.

High-Frequency Information Loss : The loss of high-frequency information in mmWave speech recovery
stems from the limited frequency response of current radar systems and signal processing algorithms. This
limitation reduces audio quality, affecting both speech clarity and musical detail. Advancing radar technology and
developing more advanced signal processing techniques will improve the capture of high-frequency vibrations
and minimize information loss, enhancing audio fidelity in recovered speech and sound.
Simultaneous Eavesdropping on Multiple Targets : The mmHSE system isolates headset vibration signals
using target localization and advanced signal separation techniques, such as beamforming and Chirp-Z transform.
However, in environments like conference rooms or public transit, where multiple headset signals overlap with
ambient noise, speech recovery quality can degrade. In close proximity scenarios, radar waveform confusion
may occur, hindering accurate signal differentiation and reducing the system’s ability to capture distinct audio
sources.
Defenses Against mmWave Eavesdropping : While mmWave technology enables effective audio reconstruc-
tion, it also poses privacy risks. Defenses against mmWave eavesdropping include advanced signal encryption
and anonymization, as well as physical shielding materials in headset shells to prevent vibration detection. These
measures must be optimized to avoid compromising user experience or device performance.
Future Directions: While experiments with the mmHSE system have been conducted in controlled real-world
environments, its practical deployment introduces additional challenges, particularly in dynamic physical en-
vironments. Future research will focus on enhancing the system’s adaptability to such conditions, specifically
by quantifying the impact of weather factors (e.g., rain, fog attenuation, and wind disturbances) and urban
obstacles (e.g., buildings and vehicle-induced multipath effects) on system performance. Addressing these chal-
lenges will contribute to the development of more sophisticated eavesdropping techniques and more effective
countermeasures.
8 CONCLUSION
This study presents an advanced eavesdropping attack systemmmHSE for headsets using COTS mmWave devices,
addressing two key challenges: refining the vibration signal model and improving capture resolution. To tackle
these issues, we propose the STSF method, which isolates diaphragm vibrations from shell vibrations through a
two-stage process combining MVDR adaptive beamforming and the Chirp-Z Transform, enhancing signal quality
and clarity. Additionally, we integrate a self-attention mechanism and a conditional GAN-based mel-spectrogram
generation model to reconstruct missing high-frequency speech components. The mmHSE system outperforms
existing solutions, as validated by both subjective and objective evaluation metrics, demonstrating its effectiveness
in improving the accuracy of headset side-channel eavesdropping.
