Abstract—With the increasing popularity of voice-centric
applications, acoustic eavesdropping attacks pose a signiﬁcant
threat to user privacy. Although smartphones require explicit
user permission to access the microphone, such attacks can
bypass this restriction by exploiting power consumption data
through compromised power supplies, such as USB adapters,
public charging stations, and power banks. However, previous
attempts can only recognize a limited set of hotwords or digits.
To address this limitation, we introduce PowerEar, an acoustic
eavesdropping attack that leverages the power side channel to
reconstruct any audio reproduced by the built-in loudspeaker of
a mobile device with an unconstrained vocabulary. Our approach
relies on a combination of signal processing and generative
techniques to learn the mapping between power consumption
and audio playback, enabling the reconstruction of such audio
through spectrogram enhancement. To validate the eﬀectiveness
of PowerEar attack, we carry out a comprehensive set of exper-
iments using audio samples from various public personalities.
Our results obtained through objective and subjective evaluations
clearly demonstrate that PowerEar can successfully recover user
speeches from power consumption data in comprehensive realistic
settings, including speech utterances of individuals and diﬀerent
devices, mobile operating systems, activities, charging technology,
battery, and volume levels.

II. RELATED WORK
Researchers have demonstrated the feasibility of side-
channel analysis to achieve acoustic eavesdropping and infer
private information from mobile devices. In what follows,
we examine the state-of-the-art of acoustic eavesdropping via
physical channels in Section II-A and power channel-based
attacks on mobile devices in Section II-B. We then describe
previous attempts to infer audio content using the power
channel in Section II-C.
A. Audio Eavesdropping Using Physical Channels
In this section, we review audio eavesdropping methods
based on motion sensors and radio frequency-based analysis.
1) Motion Sensor-Based Methods: Several works analyze
data from motion sensors on mobile devices (e.g., accelerome-
ter, gyroscope) [1], [2], [30], [59] to recognize speciﬁc spoken
words or sentences and infer user-sensitive information, such
as speaker’s identity or gender. Reference [10] fuse sensor
data to eavesdrop on sound in a room, and AccEar [16]
relies on accelerometer data to eavesdrop on the built-in
loudspeaker of a mobile device. Other work eavesdrops via
magnetic hard drive readings [22] and vibrating motors [41].
The main weakness of the above motion sensor-based methods
is their intrusive nature, since they typically assume that a
complicit/spyware app is installed on the target device to
collect/send sensor data to the adversary.
2) Radio Frequency-Based Methods: In recent years,
researchers have exploited RF technologies (e.g., WiFi, RFID,
mmWave) to perform eavesdropping attacks. In particular,
[51], [54] rely on WiFi Received Signal Strength (RSS)
and Channel State Information (CSI), respectively, to identify
speciﬁc spoken words. Using Impulse Radio Ultra-Wideband,
[53] can separate multiple audio sources (household objects)
by vibration detection. Reference [50] proposes an RFID-
based acoustic eavesdropping method that utilizes RFID tags
near the target user. These methods [50], [51], [53], [54]
suﬀer from insuﬃcient vibration resolution due to the long
wavelength and low packet rate. Regarding mmWave tech-
nology, mmSpy [4] can identify speciﬁc keywords or digits
(i.e., closed-world) during phone calls from vibrations induced
by the earpiece. Furthermore, [14], [15], [25] reconstruct the
audio played by a PC loudspeaker and the sound-induced
vibrations on surrounding objects.
B. Power Channel on Mobile Devices
In recent years, researchers have investigated possible secu-
rity threats from physical channel-based attacks on mobile
devices [47]. Among those channels, the power (side) channel
considers the electrical quantities (e.g., voltage, current drawn,
power, impedance) of a mobile device measured at its internal
(battery) or external power source (powerbank or adapter).
Charger-Surfer [6] analyzes the current drawn by a smart-
phone from the USB cable to perform keystroke inference
to guess PINs and passwords. Similarly, [58] measures power
consumption during the loading phase of websites to extract
their power-based ﬁngerprints, which are later used for iden-
tiﬁcation. PowerID [45] is a power channel-based protection

C. Power Channel-Based Acoustic Leakages
Three works investigate potential audio leakage by mobile
devices over the power side channel. GhostTalk [52] attacks
voice assistants over a charging cable, which can inject and
eavesdrop on audio after modifying a shared power bank.
Speciﬁcally, GhostTalk-SC analyzes power traces while play-
ing audio to classify digits from zero to nine in a closed-world
scenario. In wireless charging settings, [27] can achieve 97%
accuracy in classifying digits (0-(9) and a limited set of
hotwords. In addition, [33] only brieﬂy mentions the presence
of voltage leaks from the loudspeaker of a charging phone on
a multiport charger.
In summary, [27], [52] can only classify digits or hotwords
in closed-world settings, i.e., they do not recognize any
words out of those already considered at the training time.
Therefore, none of them can actually achieve eavesdropping,
as they only output labels (i.e., digits or hotwords) rather than
audio content. Our PowerEar is not limited by close-world
settings, but it fully reconstructs high-ﬁdelity and intelligible
audio from power traces. We are the ﬁrst to design a power
channel-based framework that eavesdrops on the audio played
by a mobile device’s built-in loudspeaker by reconstruct-
ing comprehensible audio and an unconstrained vocabulary.
Furthermore, we demonstrate the robustness of our approach in
cross-individual, cross-device, and other challenging scenarios.
III. P RELIMINARIES
In this section, we introduce the principles of power supply
and loudspeakers in mobile devices, and the properties of
human speech.
A. Powering Mobile Devices
The USB interface is the de facto standard for data transmis-
sion and charging mobile devices [42], such as smartphones
and tablets. A common USB 2.0 cable consists of a pair of
wires for serial data transfer ( D+ and D−), and a pair for
power (+5V and GND). The newer USB 3.x and C include
additional data transfer pairs, but the power transfer wires
remain unchanged. While a device is connected to a power
supply (e.g., adapter), the electrical current necessary to power
the device components (e.g., CPU, loudspeaker, display) is
drawn directly from the power supplier instead of the battery,
which is in charging state [13]. During the charging state,
most of the current provided goes to charge the battery, i.e.,
constant current (CC) phase. When the battery reaches the
constant voltage (CV) phase around 80% of charge, the current
absorbed by the battery gradually decreases and reaches zero
at full charge capacity (FCC). In the CV and FCC phases, we
can measure current ﬂuctuations due to activities of the device
components.
B. Built-in Loudspeaker in Mobile Devices
In general, a smartphone has two built-in loudspeakers.
The upper front earpiece is used mainly for voice calls. The
loudspeaker is more powerful than the earpiece and is used
in a wider range of applications, such as a video call, playing
music/podcast, voice assistant, and navigation. In a nutshell,
a loudspeaker is composed of four main components: (i) a
diaphragm is a rigid component whose movement produces
sound waves; (ii) a voice coil is an insulated copper wire coil
mounted to the diaphragm; (iii) a magnet is often made of
neodymium and produces a permanent magnetic ﬁeld; and
(iv) a spider stabilizes the diaphragm by preventing side
movements. An electric current passing through the voice
coil causes the diaphragm to oscillate, producing high- and
low-pressure waves. The current amplitude and frequency
determine the dislocation and speed of the diaphragm, thus
the shape of the sound waves produced. As playing sound
on a built-in loudspeaker requires an electric current, we can
measure ﬂuctuations in the current drawn by a loudspeaker
when a device is connected to a power supply.

IV. POWER EAR: OUR EAVESDROPPING ATTACK
Our attack aims to eavesdrop on the audio reproduced by
the built-in loudspeaker on the victim’s mobile device. We
provide an overview of the components of our PowerEar attack
in Figure 2. In this section, we deﬁne the threat model and
describe in detail the components of our PowerEar attack.
A. Threat Model
We consider a threat model where the victim is charging
the battery of its mobile device by connecting it to a publicly
available USB power supply (e.g., outlet, adapter, power bank),
like in the following realistic scenarios: (i) a charging station in
a public area (e.g., airport, shopping mall); (ii) a USB outlet
in a hotel room, train, or rental car; (iii) a rental or shared
powerbank; and (iv) a USB power adapter not owned or led
by a third party. The USB power supply appears normal and
provides the expected charging capability. When charging its
mobile device, the victim can perform daily activities, such as
Fig. 2. Overview of the PowerEar attack.
listening to music or podcasts, watching a video, or calling.
However, the victim is unaware that an adversary concealed
a sensor that passively monitors the current drawn by the
connected mobile device. In particular, such a sensor can be
hidden inside the power supply chassis and does not aﬀect the
ordinary charging speed.
Our attack aims to reconstruct the audio reproduced by
such a loudspeaker by analyzing the power traces of the
mobile device under charge. To successfully perform this
attack, we make the following assumptions. We assume that
the mobile device is in the charging state and has reached a
suﬃciently high battery level to enter the CV or FCC phases
(see Section III-A). However, the adversary does not have
prior knowledge about the model of the connected mobile
device or the opportunity to collect the power proﬁle of such
a device beforehand. We also assume that the victim’s audio-
related activities utilize the built-in loudspeaker. However, the
adversary has no prior knowledge about the audio content nor
the utterances of individuals whose voices are reproduced by
the loudspeaker. In addition, the victim can use a power-only
cable or apply a data blocking device (e.g., USB condom)
since the adversary does not rely on the USB data line.
B. Power Trace Acquisition 1O
The ﬁrst component of our PowerEar attack consists of
measuring the power consumption of a charging mobile
device. We deﬁne as power trace the series of consecutive
readings from the electrical current sensor deployed by the
adversary in the power supply. To obtain a reading, an analog-
to-digital converter (ADC) is necessary since the output of a
current sensor is typically an analog signal. According to its
capabilities, it is possible to conﬁgure the ADC to work at
a certain conversion speed (i.e., sampling rate) and resolution

measured in bits. For an ADC with a resolution of r bits, the
value of the least signiﬁcant bit (LSB) is V = (Vmax −Vmin)/2r
where Vmin and Vmax are the upper and lower extremes of the
ADC measurement range, respectively. Selecting the proper
sampling rate plays a pivotal role in the power trace acquisition
process. According to the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem,
to accurately represent a signal σ and its properties, it is
necessary that the minimum sampling rate fs is at least twice
the highest frequency fmax of the signal σ.
In the context of mobile device charging, the power con-
verter within a USB adapter adjusts current and voltage [5]
by performing high-frequency oscillations with on-oﬀ cycles
(e.g., pulse-width modulation [48]) or resonance circuits (e.g.,
Qi Wireless charging standard uses a dual resonance circuit
that works at 100kHz and 1MHz [55]). When the current
sensor signal contains high-frequency components and the
ADC sampling rate is low, aliasing eﬀects may occur (i.e.,
incorrect mapping of high-frequency components in lower-
frequency ranges). To mitigate the aliasing eﬀect, we collect
power traces with a sampling rate of fs = 32kHz, which is
equivalent to twice the sampling rate fmax = 16kHz of the
W A V audio ﬁles played by the built-in loudspeaker during
data collection (see Section V-A).

B. Training Parameters
We train our models following the BicycleGAN training
process for a total of 100 epochs. In the ﬁrst 50 epochs,
we set for all our neural networks a relatively low learning
rate of 2 ·10−4 to limit the pace of each update of internal
parameters. This would reduce the risk of skipping local
optimum or excessive loss value oscillations. In the second
50 epochs, we dynamically adjust the learning rate using
the Adam optimizer [19]. This allows a fast response to
new gradient information while maintaining enough inertia to
smooth out ﬂuctuations.We set the hyperparameters for the
losses at λV AE= 1.0, λLR = 0.5, and λKL = 10−3. We provide
further details on the selection of the number of epochs and
hyperparameter tuning in Appendix A.

power traces of a device (i.e., Nova, Sx14, and Op8) while
playing the training audio of English-speaking individuals at

VI. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
In this section, we discuss the limitations of PowerEar attack
and possible countermeasures.
A. Battery Level and Attack Window
Due to a similar threat model, our PowerEar attack shares
the same limitation in terms of battery level as all other power
channel-based attacks on mobile devices. In particular, this
limitation is present for attacks in both wired [6], [29], [33],
[43] and wireless charging scenarios [23], [27], [34], [35].
As we explain in Section III-A, it is not possible to observe
the current variation while the mobile device is in the CC
phase due to the properties of Li-ion batteries. Therefore, we
can perform our attack only once the device is in the CV
or FCC phases. In Section V-C.7, we experimentally assess
how PowerEar’s audio reconstruction performance improves

B. Countermeasures
Researchers have investigated methods to counteract the
leakage of private information through power channels via
software or hardware countermeasures.
1) Software Countermeasures: aim to randomize the power
consumption of the device using energy-aggressive apps to
obfuscate leakages in wired [29], wireless [27], or multiport
[33] charging scenarios. Unfortunately, while being eﬀective,
these countermeasures also aﬀect the eﬃciency of battery
charging as they introduce charging time and resource (e.g.,
CPU, memory) overheads. However, a simple yet eﬀective
countermeasure is to set the mobile device to stop charging
once it reaches the CV phase (e.g., Wise charging feature in
ColorOS v13).
2) Hardware Countermeasures: typically involve external
electronic circuitry, as it is not realistic to rely on manufactur-
ers to redesign their charging solutions and protocols. Ni et al.
in [33] propose a device to be installed on the USB port, which
smooths power ﬂuctuations employing an LC-based ﬁlter and
a low dropout voltage regulator (i.e., AMS1117). Cronin et al.
in [6] suggest removing high-frequency components with a
low-pass ﬁlter into a device’s charging circuit.
To evaluate the eﬀectiveness of these defenses, we imple-
ment three passive low-pass ﬁlters (i.e., RC, LC, CLC ﬁlters)
and a voltage regulator-based (Reg) defense [33], which
schematics and circuits are reported in ﬁgures 15(a)-(d). In
particular, we select the values for resistors, inductors, and
capacitors to achieve a cutoﬀ frequency below 250Hz, thus
excluding the frequency range of human speech [3]. How-
ever, these ﬁlters cause a voltage drop VD = VIN -VOUT that
prevents VOUT from reaching the 5V necessary to not hinder
the device’s charging performance. VD can be signiﬁcant
especially with a high current I since VD = R ·I, where R
is the resistance of a ﬁlter. While the voltage drop on the RC
ﬁlter is minimal, it is non-negligible in the LC, CLC, and
Reg ﬁlters because of the inductors’ DC resistance (DCR).
Therefore, we can attain VOUT ≈5V by increasing the voltage
in VIN accordingly. In Figure 15e, we report power traces
measured while playing audio without (i.e., None) and with
low-pass ﬁlters. We can observe that these ﬁlters prevent
audio leakage by signiﬁcantly attenuating frequencies above
250Hz; therefore, they are eﬀective countermeasures against
our attack.
VII. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose PowerEar, a power channel-based
acoustic eavesdropping method that reconstructs the audio
played by the built-in loudspeaker of a mobile device with
high ﬁdelity and an unconstrained vocabulary. An adversary
can exploit PowerEar capabilities to seriously hinder user
privacy by eavesdropping while using the mobile device for
audio-related activities, such as making video or voice calls,
listening to audio messages, watching videos, or using voice-
assisted navigation. PowerEar method relies on a combination
of signal processing and generative adversarial networks to
ﬁrst learn the underlying power trace-to-audio mapping and
then reconstruct the audio played on the mobile device from
its power traces. We extensively evaluated the eﬀectiveness of
PowerEar using audio recordings from 48 individuals that span
six languages, three audio speech datasets, and power traces
collected from a wide range of mobile devices (i.e., 15 Android
and 7 iOS devices) in various settings (e.g., wireless and
wired charging, battery, and volume level), and audio-related
activities (e.g., navigation, voice/video call, and audio/video
streaming).

distinct conﬁgurations: baseline ( None), Wiener ﬁlter (Wien)
