4.3 Moiré Pattern Detection by Photodiode
After generating the amplified moiré pattern, we need to capture the
signal and digitize it for further processing. Prior work has relied
on analyzing video frames [30–32, 45]; however, such methods are
limited by low sample rates, which are insufficient for reconstruct-
ing audio signals at kilohertz frequencies. In contrast, we leverage
a unique property of moiré patterns: their periodic structure repli-
cates motion patterns uniformly across the entire grating. This
allows us to replace video capture with a simple one-dimensional
photodiode, which samples a single point while retaining com-
plete motion information. With sampling rates of up to 11 kHz, the
photodiode enables accurate, real-time audio reconstruction.

Moiré-based Sensing
Moiré patterns have recently been explored for high-precision mo-
tion sensing due to their ability to amplify subtle displacements into
detectable patterns. Xiao et al. [45] proposed MoiréBoard, which en-
ables 3-DoF camera translation tracking using a dual-layer passive
marker. Building on this idea, Ning et al. [ 30] introduced Moiré-
Pose, extending the approach to 6-DoF pose estimation in camera-
to-screen interaction scenarios. Qiu et al. [32] further developed
MoiréTag, achieving accurate angular tracking via a double-sided
printed marker. More recently, Ning et al. [31] presented MoiréVib,
enabling high-accuracy vibration sensing in dynamic environments.
Although these works highlight the strong potential of moiré-based
sensing, their performance remains constrained by controlled se-
tups, dedicated gratings, and limited sample rates. Consequently,
existing moiré-based sensing approaches are not yet suitable for cap-
turing full-bandwidth speech information. We are the first to lever-
age moiré patterns for eavesdropping and to address the unique
challenges involved. We believe the proposed methods can also
benefit conventional moiré-based motion sensing.

Abstract
Eavesdropping poses a critical threat to the confidentiality and in-
tegrity of voice communications. In recent years, techniques have
advanced beyond traditional microphone-based methods toward
more intelligent approaches, such as leveraging millimeter-wave
sensing to detect the subtle vibrations induced by speakers and re-
construct voice information without direct audio capture. Despite
their technical feasibility, these methods remain constrained by
limited working ranges—typically only several meters—rendering
them impractical for real-world stealthy eavesdropping. In this
work, we propose MoiréEar, the first long-range passive eavesdrop-
ping system based on moiré patterns. The key idea is to exploit
the amplification capability of moiré patterns, which amplify the
minute vibrations induced by acoustic signals by hundreds of times,
enabling long-range eavesdropping. To make the proposed method
even more practical and stealthy, we develop new theoretical foun-
dations that relax the strict requirements for generating moiré pat-
terns. Specifically, our approach enables the use of irregular stripe
structures (e.g., commonly seen barcodes) instead of standard moiré
gratings1 to generate moiré patterns. We implement our design
using a low-cost photodiode instead of cameras, achieving real-time
eavesdropping with lightweight signal processing. Comprehensive
experiments show that the system can extract intelligible audio at

1 Introduction
Eavesdropping poses a significant threat to the confidentiality and
integrity of voice communications. By intercepting private con-
versations, unauthorized parties can access sensitive information,
manipulate communication content, and exploit the data for mali-
cious purposes [6, 35]. As voice communication systems become
increasingly pervasive in both personal and professional contexts,
the threat of eavesdropping grows, posing serious risks to privacy,
security, and trust in the IoT era [ 2–4, 15, 17, 21, 23, 25, 34, 47].
Earlier optical approaches, such as laser-based eavesdropping, can
recover speech over long distances, but they rely on active opti-
cal emissions, making them easily detectable and vulnerable to
commercial countermeasures [ 26, 29, 42]. In recent years, new
eavesdropping modalities based on wireless sensing have been
proposed [5, 18, 38, 43, 44]. The core idea is to use wireless signals,
such as millimeter waves, to sense minute vibrations caused by a
speaker and thereby recover the acoustic content. While promising,
the effective range of these systems remains very limited—typically
only a few meters—making them impractical for stealthy eaves-
dropping. Furthermore, these methods require the transmission
of dedicated signals (e.g., millimeter-wave signals), which are de-
tectable and therefore vulnerable to countermeasures.
We propose, for the first time, to employ moiré patterns for eaves-
dropping. The core idea lies in leveraging the strong amplification
capability of moiré patterns to amplify tiny vibrations on everyday
objects (e.g., a plastic box containing grapes) in our surrounding en-
vironment. The underlying principle is that when two gratings with
similar spatial frequencies overlap, small relative displacements are

such as MoiréVib and MoiréPose [30, 31], has showcased the power
of moiré amplification for motion sensing. Although prior work
has demonstrated the power of moiré amplification, applying it to
audio eavesdropping presents fundamentally different challenges.
•Challenge 1: Previous measurement-oriented applications rely
on well-designed, dedicated moiré gratings. However, achieving
practical and stealthy eavesdropping requires leveraging every-
day objects. For instance, instead of relying on specially designed
moiré gratings, we aim to use the barcode on a supermarket grape
box for eavesdropping. This introduces fundamental challenges,
as barcodes differ substantially from moiré gratings, exhibiting
non-uniform spacing and stripe widths.
•Challenge 2: A practical challenge is that the target may have
arbitrary orientations, and moiré effect is sensitive to these varia-
tions. Since we have no control over the target, achieving reliable
eavesdropping becomes difficult. Moreover, the target’s apparent
size depends on its actual size and distance from the eavesdropper,
which further complicates the eavesdropping process.
•Challenge 3: Existing moiré-sensing technique is used to monitor
machine vibrations, typically involving only one or a few frequen-
cies. In contrast, audio eavesdropping requires reconstructing
full-spectrum acoustic signals with rich spectral details. This
demands a sampling rate of at least several kilohertz to capture
intelligible audio, whereas prior moiré-sensing systems rely on
cameras that can only provide frame rates in the range of 30∼300
fps, which is far too low for effective eavesdropping.
These fundamental differences make existing moiré-sensing
methods unsuitable for audio eavesdropping. In the following, we
present our corresponding solutions.
To address the challenge of orientation diversity, a straightfor-
ward solution is to design stripes covering all orientations. However,
this introduces a trade-off: supporting additional orientations re-
quires superimposing more stripes at the eavesdropper side, which
increases the stripe area, reduces transparency, and compromises
the clarity of the captured target barcode. Through theoretical analy-
sis and experiments, we found that superimposing four orientations
provides a good balance. With four orientations, for a randomly
oriented target, the worst-case reduction in moiré amplification
compared to a fully aligned orientation is theoretically bounded at
38%, which is sufficient for eavesdropping from several meters.
While multiple orientations help mitigate the effects of random
target orientation, they also produce overlapping moiré patterns,
leading to optical aliasing. To address this, we exploit a key property
of photodiodes: their output integrates the total light intensity
across the aperture. Consequently, moiré patterns with spacing
much smaller than the aperture minimally affect the photodiode
output, as the aperture integrates over multiple moiré periods,
keeping the measured result relatively stable. By adjusting the
aperture to slightly below the largest moiré pattern spacing, we
can capture the largest moiré pattern for sensing while suppressing
the rest interfering moiré patterns.
To address target size diversity, which varies with both the tar-
get’s actual size and its distance, we propose, for the first time,
incorporating a telescope into the moiré pattern generation process.
Compared with traditional solutions using a high-speed camera,
the telescope-based approach is more cost-effective and efficient.
This is because cameras rely on a combination of digital zoom and
optical zoom, of which only the optical zoom contributes to moiré
amplification. By contrast, telescopes offer exclusively optical zoom.
While promising, however, when telescope is incorporated into the
system, the original mathematical relationship governing the am-
plification of moiré patterns no longer holds. We therefore establish
a new theoretical model to guide the tuning of telescope for strong
moiré amplification, enabling stealthy long-range eavesdropping.
To address the challenge of non-uniform barcode stripes, we
observed that typical barcodes (e.g., EAN-13 [13, 14]) employ only
four distinct stripe widths and spacings, which we refer to as stripe
types. A straightforward approach is to design a reference grat-
ing containing all four stripe types to match the target barcode.
However, with four orientations per stripe, this multi-stripe-type
design would entail 16 unique stripes, resulting in significant stripe
overlap that compromises sensing capability. To address this issue,
we smartly leverage two key observations of barcodes: (i) The de-
gree of overlap between different stripes varies significantly (For
example, stripe type 1 overlaps with stripe type 2 by 66.7%, whereas
stripe type 3 overlaps with stripe type 4 by only 28.6%). (ii) The
four stripe types occupy substantially different proportions of the
barcode area, ranging from 10% to 40%. Based on these observations,
we formulate an objective function to maximize the barcode area
that can contribute to moiré pattern generation while minimizing
overlap. Guided by this objective, we retain only two stripe types,
which allow about 70% of the barcode area to be used for moiré
pattern generation while keeping the overlap minimal.
To address the final challenge—the insufficient frame rate of ex-
isting camera-based solutions—prior work has proposed using two
cameras to increase the frame rate from 30∼60 fps up to around 300
fps [31]. However, this improvement is still far from sufficient for
recovering human voice, which requires a sampling rate of several
kilohertz. The low frame rate of camera is primarily due to the large
number of pixels in each image. For example, a typical image has
12 MP (4000×3000 pixels), and buffering such a large amount of
information for so many pixels limits the frame rate [27]. However,
for moiré generation, millions of pixels are unnecessary—actually,
a single pixel suffices. Based on this critical insight, we propose
using a photodiode, which is effectively equivalent to one pixel
of the camera. It is cheaper and can achieve a significantly higher
sampling rate (up to 11 kHz), which is sufficient for voice recov-
ery. Moreover, using a single-pixel signal makes the processing
lightweight and capable of real-time operation.

•For the first time, we propose using moiré patterns for eaves-

2 System Components
The basic principle of MoiréEar is shown in Fig. 1. The important
system components are introduced below:
•Target Barcode. We utilize an actual barcode for eavesdropping.
For example, a supermarket grape box with a barcode is placed
on a desk, with a nearby speaker.
•Reference grating. At the eavesdropper side, a plate with a
reference grating is positioned in front of the telescope. Sub-
tle vibrations of the target barcode are optically amplified into
pronounced moiré pattern motions, observable at the receiver.
•Telescope. A telescope, priced at tens of dollars, typically pro-
vides an optical zoom of up to 200×. It amplifies the distant target
barcode onto the reference grating to generate the moiré pattern.
The optical zoom can be adjusted for targets at different distances
and of varying sizes, enabling long-distance eavesdropping.
•Photodiode. A one-dimensional high-speed photodiode is po-
sitioned behind the reference grating. It captures the temporal
intensity fluctuations of the moiré patterns, and the resulting in-
tensity signals are digitized and processed through a lightweight
pipeline to reconstruct intelligible audio in real time.
3 Background
Moiré patterns emerge when two gratings with similar but not iden-
tical spatial frequencies (i.e., stripe densities) overlap. The overlap
produces low-frequency moiré patterns that amplify tiny displace-
ments into large, visible shifts. Based on this characteristic, existing
works have exploited moiré patterns for motion sensing [30–32, 45].

4 Theoretical Model
In practical eavesdropping, target gratings may rotate and are rarely
perfectly aligned (i.e., parallel) with the reference grating, which
diminishes the amplification effect. Therefore, we first present a
generalized moiré model that characterizes the amplification effect
under angular mismatch. We then extend this model by incorporat-
ing telescope parameters, enabling it for long-range sensing. Finally,
we model the moiré pattern as an optical signal captured by the
photodiode, which carries the amplification effect.

4.2 Moiré Pattern in the Presence of Telescope
From the analysis of general moiré generation, the target and
reference gratings must have nearly identical spatial frequencies.
However, this condition is difficult to satisfy in practical eaves-
dropping scenarios. For instance, target grating spacing can vary
widely—from 0.33 mm (e.g., EAN-13 barcodes [ 13, 14]) to 2 mm
(e.g., sunshades). Moreover, variations in eavesdropping distance
further exacerbate the mismatch. For instance, a 0.33 mm stripe
spacing at 90 meters appears equivalent to about 0.0037 mm at a
1-meter viewing distance. Directly matching this with reference
gratings would require extremely fine stripe spacing, far beyond
the capability of standard printers (typically limited to 0.0212 mm,
or 1200 dots per inch (DPI)).
We thus involve a zoomable telescope and revise the model
of general moiré pattern in Sec. 4.1. This design enables optimal
amplification without the need to replace the reference grating and
a single 0.5 mm-spacing reference grating supports target grating
spacings from 0.33 to 2 mm over distances ranging from 20 to 90 m.

4.3 Moiré Pattern Detection by Photodiode
After generating the amplified moiré pattern, we need to capture the
signal and digitize it for further processing. Prior work has relied
on analyzing video frames [30–32, 45]; however, such methods are
limited by low sample rates, which are insufficient for reconstruct-
ing audio signals at kilohertz frequencies. In contrast, we leverage
a unique property of moiré patterns: their periodic structure repli-
cates motion patterns uniformly across the entire grating. This
allows us to replace video capture with a simple one-dimensional
photodiode, which samples a single point while retaining com-
plete motion information. With sampling rates of up to 11 kHz, the
photodiode enables accurate, real-time audio reconstruction.
We place a photodiode behind the reference gratings, converting
the optical signal 𝐼′𝑚 (®𝒑𝑚,𝑡)into a voltage signal 𝑈(𝑡):

5 Moiré Pattern in the Wild
Our theoretical model lays the foundation for long-distance moiré
generation and eavesdropping. To make the attack even more prac-
tical, we aim to use stripes from everyday objects (i.e., barcodes)
as target gratings. In this setting, two factors introduce additional

5.1 Handle Random Target Orientations
Degradation due to unaligned orientations. When the orienta-
tions of the target and reference gratings are not perfectly aligned,
the resulting moiré pattern becomes narrower and less discernible.
Theoretically, the amplification effect decreases as the orientation
difference |𝜃2 −𝜃1|increases. Experiments confirm this trend. In
Fig. 5, we fix the reference grating and change the target grating
orientation in the plane. Fig. 5 (c) ∼(e) illustrate that orientation
angles of 5◦, 10◦, and 45◦progressively narrow the moiré patterns
compared with the perfectly aligned case ( 0◦). At 90◦, the moiré
patterns disappear entirely, consistent with the prediction of Eq.(8).
Beyond narrowing the moiré patterns, orientation differences
also affect the amplification of dynamic displacements. As shown in
Eq. (10), moiré amplification is highly anisotropic, strongest when
displacement direction is aligned with the grating direction and
weakest when orthogonal. Experimental results in Fig. 6 confirm
this effect. First, the target grating in (a) moves downward by1/2𝑓1
(half grating spacing), producing moiré shifts by 1/2𝑓𝑚 (half moiré
spacing) in (b). Although the shift of target grating is very small and

almost invisible, it induces a significant change in the moiré pattern,
turning from light to dark (Fig. 6 (c)), i.e., motion amplification.
While in Fig. 6 (d), the target grating moves rightward, and the
moiré shift is still 1/2𝑓1, yet no motion amplification is observed.
The moiré pattern hardly shifts (Fig. 6 (e)), and its intensity remains
nearly constant (Fig. 6 (f)).

In moiré sensing, the widest moiré pattern with the greatest am-

the photodiode readout remains unchanged (Fig. 7 (c)). Thus, by
reducing the aperture with electrical tape to slightly below the
largest moiré pattern spacing, we capture only the dominant moiré
pattern.

Perspective Distortion
For more realistic eavesdropping, we relax the requirement for
a carefully-designed target grating and instead utilize everyday
stripes (e.g., barcodes2). However, this introduces two challenges:
non-uniform spacing and oblique viewing.
Performance degradation with barcodes. As shown in Fig. 8,
the spatial frequency of the barcode stripes is non-uniform. Typ-
ically, an EAN-13 barcode [13, 14] consists of four stripe widths
and spacings, ranging from 1𝑤𝑏 to 4𝑤𝑏 as shown in Fig. 8. And
the corresponding frequencies are 1/2𝑤𝑏,1/4𝑤𝑏,1/6𝑤𝑏 and 1/8𝑤𝑏,
respectively. This non-uniformity violates the uniform-frequency
matching condition for moiré generation (Eq. (7)). As shown in
Fig. 9 (a)∼(d), using the four corresponding single-frequency refer-
ence gratings produces locally fragmented moiré patterns, leading
to a lack of amplification in some regions.

For the EAN-13 barcode with four distinct stripe spacings [13, 14],
we superimpose four four-orientation reference gratings, each cor-
responding to one stripe spacing (Fig. 11 (a)∼(d). The resulting ref-
erence grating is shown in Fig. 11 (e). However, the heavy overlap
of stripes renders the four frequencies indistinguishable, severely
hindering frequency matching for moiré generation.
To address the stripe-overlapping issue while preserving strong
amplification, we smartly leverage two key observations of bar-
codes: (i) the degree of overlap between different-frequency stripes
varies significantly (e.g., overlap between1/2𝑤𝑏- and1/4𝑤𝑏-frequency
stripes is severe since one is an integer multiple of the other, whereas
overlap between 1/4𝑤𝑏 and 1/6𝑤𝑏 is less pronounced), and (ii) the
four stripe types occupy different proportions of the barcode area
(e.g., the2𝑤𝑏-width stripe covers 40%, whereas the4𝑤𝑏-width stripe
covers only 10%). Based on these observations, we formulate an
objective function to maximize the barcode area that can contribute
to moiré pattern generation while minimizing overlap. Guided by
this objective, we retain only two stripe types, which allow about
70% of the barcode area to be utilized for moiré pattern generation
while keeping the overlap minimal.
The resulting final reference grating is illustrated in Fig. 11 (f).
By incorporating multiple frequencies into the multi-orientation
reference grating, clear moiré patterns can be generated even for
non-uniform or tilted barcodes (Fig. 12).

6 Signal Processing Pipeline
Although moiré amplification makes subtle vibrations visible, the
extracted optical signals are highly susceptible to environmental
noise and interference. Thus, we design a two-stage signal process-
ing pipeline: (i) suppression of dominant noise components and (ii)
enhancement of audio-relevant spectral features, shown in Fig. 13.
6.1 Noise Suppression
We identify two dominant types of noise and interference and
suppress each using tailored strategies:
•Reference grating vibrations. Ambient sound near the
reference grating induces unintended micro-vibrations, pro-
ducing spurious moiré patterns. To suppress this effect, a
microphone is placed near the reference grating to record the
ambient sound, and its spectral components are subtracted
from the measured moiré signals. Note that the reference
grating is located on the attacker’s side, and no device needs
to be placed near the target.
•Low-frequency background noise. Mechanical vibrations
of the telescope and airflow introduce low-frequency noise,
which can obscure the spectrum of the measured audio sig-
nals. To address this, a high-pass filter with a 300 Hz cutoff is
applied to remove the low-frequency noise while preserving
the band containing most audio information [22].
6.2 Spectral Enhancement
Although noise suppression removes dominant interference, it also
attenuates other frequency components (Fig. 13 (c)), limiting audio

7 Evaluation
To validate the design under realistic conditions, we use barcodes
as target gratings (Fig. 14), with an eavesdropper in one room
attempting to recover audio played in a different room. We then
use sunshades as target gratings (Fig. 15). In both scenarios, we
evaluate the system’s performance across varying audio content
and configurable parameters.
7.1 Experimental Setup
Devices. (i) Target grating: We employ barcodes on different ob-
jects—including a plastic box, plastic wrap, a potato chip bag, and
a bottle of water—for eavesdropping experiments. We further use
sunshades in the room as an additional target grating for eaves-
dropping. The audio source is a speaker (Lumens M1000 [24]) in
the meeting room. Note that this device is part of the room’s default
audio setup and was not specifically selected by the authors. The
reference grating is fabricated by printing stripe patterns on a 2 mm-
thick acrylic plate and the total cost including the plate is $0.2. The
reference grating is mounted in front of the telescope eyepiece. (ii)
Telescope: We employ a Celestron 80EQ refracting telescope ($90)
for the optical zoom [7]. It has a 900 mm focal length and supports
22.5×∼225×amplification using eyepieces of varying focal lengths.
(iii) Photodiode: A Hamamatsu PDC6-S1336-8BQ Photodiode ($25)3
is placed behind the reference grating to capture the optical sig-
nal [16]. The analog output is digitized by an AD9826K ADC [12]
and transmitted to a laptop via an STM32H7 microcontroller [39]
for real-time processing, whose sampling rate is 11 kHz.
Datasets. The audio sources used in this work are drawn from
three representative datasets, covering isolated digits, continuous
speech, and music: (i) TIDIGITS [ 9]: Designed for digit recognition,
this dataset consists of spoken digits (0∼9) from 30 speakers, evenly
split by gender. Each speaker provides two utterances for every digit.
(ii) Harvard Sentences [ 20, 33]: A dataset for speech intelligibility,
featuring 30 phonetically balanced English sentences read by 30
speakers, respectively. (iii) GTZAN Genre Collection [ 41]: It includes
music tracks spanning ten musical genres.
Metrics. (i) Accuracy: For the TIDIGITS dataset, we evaluate
the classification accuracy across three predefined categories: digit,
speaker identity (shortened as speaker), and speaker gender (short-
ened as gender). For the GTZAN Genre Collection dataset, we
assess the accuracy of music recognition using the commercial
software Shazam [37], which reflects whether the reconstructed
signal retains sufficient acoustic detail. (ii) STOI: To quantify audio
intelligibility, we employ the Short-Time Objective Intelligibility

Impact of different target barcode materials. We evaluate
five representative everyday target gratings, including barcodes
on a fruit box, plastic wrap, a potato chip bag, and a water bottle,
as well as a mesh-patterned sunshade shown in Fig. 20 (a), using

8 Countermeasures
Beyond highlighting the risks of moiré-based acoustic eavesdrop-
ping, we propose countermeasures to safeguard audio privacy.
Removing potential gratings from the environment. Since
MoiréEar relies on vibrations of thin, lightweight striped surfaces,
one mitigation strategy is to remove such objects. However, this
may not always be practical, as striped designs (e.g., barcodes,
blinds) are ubiquitous and often unavoidable.
Blocking visual access. Curtains, frosted glass, or opaque cov-
ers can block the optical path required for MoiréEar. However, such
measures also reduce indoor lighting, limiting their practicality in
everyday environments.
Spectral obfuscation. Dedicated noise can be injected as an
active defense so that eavesdroppers capture only distorted sig-
nals. But this requires additional hardware and coordination for
authorized users to remove the noise.
9 Discussion and Future Work
Realistic deployment under ambient noise, vibrations, and
lighting conditions. (1) Ambient noise and vibration. On the eaves-
dropper side, Section 7.4 shows that using a relatively thick 2 mm
reference grating limits mechanical resonance caused by ambient
audio noise. Vibration effects, which typically occur at relatively
constant frequencies, can be readily identified and removed in the
frequency domain. Nevertheless, we recommend placing the eaves-
dropper device on a rigid surface to provide greater stability against
vibration. On the victim side, Periodic noise sources such as air con-
ditioners can be mitigated through frequency-domain filtering, as
they typically exhibit stable, narrow-band spectral characteristics.
For more complex noise sources, such as television audio, existing
acoustic signal processing techniques provide potential mitigation
approaches, such as feature learning–based source separation. (2)
Lighting conditions. Lighting conditions influence the performance
of the proposed system. Nevertheless, based on our experiments,
the system can tolerate a wide range of real-world lighting condi-
tions. During daytime operation, even when all lights in the victim’s
room are turned off, the system remains functional by increasing
the gain of the photodiode sensor on the eavesdropper side. The
system remains operational at ambient light levels as low as 10 lux.
Deployment efforts. The aiming process consists of two steps,
coarse aiming and fine aiming. During the initial coarse aiming, the
telescope is equipped with an optical finder scope that serves as a
reticle-based aiming aid.5 After coarse aiming, fine adjustments are
performed by tuning the horizontal and vertical adjustment screws
on the telescope tripod, allowing small angular corrections until the
telescope is precisely aligned with the target barcode. The eyepiece
focus is then adjusted to clearly observe the barcode pattern through
the telescope. These procedures require only minimal training, and
the system can typically be set up within five minutes.
Attack scope. The current system exploits the resonance be-
tween loudspeaker-generated sound and nearby barcodes. Loud-
speakers generate relatively higher sound pressure, inducing clear
vibrations in everyday lightweight materials such as plastic packag-
ing. In contrast, the human throat generates lower sound pressure,
resulting in a shorter working range. To enable an attack using
throat-generated voice, dedicated materials (e.g., materials simi-
lar to earphone diaphragms) could be placed in the environment,
allowing the system to operate over a longer distance than with ev-
eryday objects. In addition, learning-based methods, rather than the
current signal-processing approach, could be leveraged to extract
weaker vibrations, thereby further extending the working range.
Importantly, speaker-based eavesdropping alone poses a meaning-
ful privacy risk, as sensitive speech played through loudspeakers
may be unintentionally exposed, particularly in meeting rooms and
online conferencing environments.
Broader real-world target gratings. In addition to barcodes,
numerous everyday striped items—ranging from QR codes and
venetian blinds to windshield heating wires, fruit nets, striped um-
brellas, and tablecloths—may function as unintentional gratings. A
systematic study of these objects could quantify their leakage risks
and highlight the practical prevalence of such threats.
Covert target gratings with invisible inks. While our system
relies on visible gratings for alignment, attackers could deliberately
plant hidden gratings using UV- or IR-sensitive inks. Such gratings
are invisible to the naked eye but detectable with the appropriate
sensors, making eavesdropping even harder to detect.
Ubiquitous surveillance cameras. Commodity cameras, un-
like our telescope and photodiode setup, are currently limited by

10 Related Work
10.1 Acoustic Eavesdropping
Acoustic eavesdropping techniques can be broadly divided into four
main categories, as summarized in Tab. 2. Below, we review each
category and discuss the corresponding characteristics:
IMU-based eavesdropping: Early studies exploit inertial mea-
surement units (IMU) embedded in smartphones and wearables for
eavesdropping. Gyroscopes can sense subtle air-pressure changes
induced by nearby sound waves, enabling inference of spoken
words [25]. Similarly, accelerometers have been used to classify
speech segments, digits, or user commands [2, 4, 47]. Later works
combine gyroscope and accelerometer data for signal fusion, im-
proving robustness against noise and motion artifacts [17]. These
approaches provide high sensitivity for short-range sensing, typi-
cally within 30 cm to 1.2 m. Due to the short eavesdropping range,
it is typically impractical for an attacker to deploy a separate IMU;
instead, they would need to access the IMU of the target device.
Consequently, these methods require direct access to the target
device, limiting their applicability in broader scenarios.
RF eavesdropping: Radio-frequency (RF) techniques enable
remote sensing of acoustic vibrations. Early works use commod-
ity WiFi devices to capture channel state information (CSI) varia-
tions for sensing speech content [43, 44]. While these systems can
achieve ranges of several meters, the relatively large signal wave-
length limits their sensing granularity. More recently, mmWave
sensing has emerged as a promising approach. Hu et al. proposed
MILLIEAR [18], which combines FMCW mmWave radar with gen-
erative models to reconstruct spoken sentences. This technique
achieves a range of up to 5 m and enables free-vocabulary recovery
through walls. However, mmWave systems still rely on active signal
emissions, which can be readily detected, limiting their practicality.
Optical-based eavesdropping: Optical approaches capture
surface vibrations induced by sound, offering high spatial reso-
lution. High-speed cameras can record subtle motion on every-
day objects, and reconstruct the corresponding speech [11]. Laser-
based techniques sense reflected light from windows or nearby
objects [ 26, 42, 46], achieving eavesdropping distances beyond
200 m. Recent works discovered that some light sources uninten-
tionally emit optical signals correlated with audio. For example,
Glowworm [28] recovers speech from LED status indicators at dis-
tances up to 35 m, and Lamphone [29] leverages bulb vibrations for
similar purposes. While optical sensing can achieve long ranges,
high-speed cameras and precision lasers are often expensive and
less suitable for covert deployment. LED- and bulb-based methods
require stable lighting conditions, which limits flexibility.
Other side-channel eavesdropping: Beyond IMU, RF, and
optics, researchers have explored other channels for eavesdrop-
ping. Vibration motors can be repurposed as acoustic sensors [34].
Speakers and headphones can be turned into microphones through
software reconfiguration [15]. Magnetic hard drives also emit micro-
vibrations correlated with sound [ 21]. MagEar [ 23] shows that
speakers emit weak magnetic signals while playing sound, which
can be picked up by nearby sensors at a distance of 60 cm. These
methods demonstrate eavesdropping capabilities but depend on
specific hardware configurations or controlled environments.
Overall, prior works explore diverse eavesdropping modalities
with varying trade-offs in intrusiveness, stealth, range, and hard-
ware complexity. In contrast, MoiréEar achieves a strong balance:
it passively captures long-range speech without requiring access
to the target device or emitting signals, while supporting free-
vocabulary recovery using low-cost hardware.
10.2 Moiré-based Sensing
Moiré patterns have recently been explored for high-precision mo-
tion sensing due to their ability to amplify subtle displacements into
detectable patterns. Xiao et al. [45] proposed MoiréBoard, which en-
ables 3-DoF camera translation tracking using a dual-layer passive
marker. Building on this idea, Ning et al. [ 30] introduced Moiré-
Pose, extending the approach to 6-DoF pose estimation in camera-
to-screen interaction scenarios. Qiu et al. [32] further developed
MoiréTag, achieving accurate angular tracking via a double-sided
printed marker. More recently, Ning et al. [31] presented MoiréVib,
enabling high-accuracy vibration sensing in dynamic environments.
Although these works highlight the strong potential of moiré-based
sensing, their performance remains constrained by controlled se-
tups, dedicated gratings, and limited sample rates. Consequently,
existing moiré-based sensing approaches are not yet suitable for cap-
turing full-bandwidth speech information. We are the first to lever-
age moiré patterns for eavesdropping and to address the unique
challenges involved. We believe the proposed methods can also
benefit conventional moiré-based motion sensing.
11 Conclusion
MoiréEar is the first long-range passive eavesdropping system to
capture audio through moiré-based amplification. It leverages com-
mon striped textures—such as barcodes and sunshades—together
with a low-cost photodiode to enable real-time audio eavesdropping.
The system captures intelligible audio from distances of up to 90 m,
demonstrating a practical and robust eavesdropping paradigm.
