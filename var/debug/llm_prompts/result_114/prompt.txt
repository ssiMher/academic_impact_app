Abstract
Bone conduction headphones have gained popularity due to
their comfort and versatility, as they transmit sound via vibra-
tions through the user’s skull bones rather than the eardrums.
However, the transmitted audio may contain sensitive infor-
mation, posing serious privacy risks if intercepted by unau-
thorized parties. While prior research has explored acoustic
eavesdropping attacksvia side channels such as millimeter
wave (mmWave) radar, existing methods remain constrained
by limited generalizability and degraded reconstruction qual-
ity. In this paper, we propose EchoLLM, the ﬁrst mmWave-
based eavesdropping attack speciﬁcally targeting the semantic
content of audio transmitted through a victim’s bone conduc-
tion headphones. EchoLLM exploits the fact that audio signals
in bone conduction headphones are transmitted as mechani-
cal vibrations, which can be captured as a side channel. The
attack is designed to be context-aware, target-aware, low-cost,
and robust. To improve automatic speech recognition (ASR)
under weak mmWave signals, EchoLLM introduces a novel
multi-modal speech recognition model that leverages the vic-
tim’s own speech as contextual input for better ASR accuracy.
To provide higher-quality input to the multi-modal model,
EchoLLM incorporates signal enhancement techniques such
as target identiﬁcation and background reﬂection reduction.
Our experiments show that EchoLLM achieves a word error
rate (WER) as low as 5.23%, and conﬁrm that it can effectively
reconstruct the content from the audio transmitted through a
targeted bone conduction headphone under realistic scenarios.
1 Introduction
Bone conduction headphones transmit audio through the
user’s skull bones rather than the eardrums, offering a more
comfortable and ﬂexible listening experience. Unlike tradi-
tional headsets, bone conduction headphones are worn on the
cheekbones instead of inserted into ear canals. As a result,
they have rapidly gained popularity and captured a signiﬁcant
share of the global headphone market. According to Valuates
Reports1, the global market for bone conduction headphones
is projected to grow from $1.07 billion in 2024 to over $3.67
billion by 2030, with a compound annual growth rate (CAGR)
of 22.8%, which substantially exceeds the 3.7% CAGR of
the overall global headphone market. However, the increasing
use of bone conduction headphones in crowded public envi-
ronments (e.g., subways, ofﬁces) has raised privacy concerns,
as audio output from these devices may inadvertently expose
sensitive information. For example, a caller wearing bone
conduction headphones may receive a phone call in which
the callee replies, “Y our appointment is conﬁrmed for 3PM at
the cardiology clinic.” If such response content is intercepted
via acoustic eavesdropping attacks, it could lead to severe
breaches of appointment and location privacy.
Various acoustic eavesdropping attacks have been pro-
posed in recent literature to extract sensitive information
from victims’ audio devices. These attacks typically ex-
ploit the physical phenomenon that sound emitted by ac-
tive sources (e.g., speakers, human vocal tracts) induces sub-
tle vibrations either directly on the emitting surface or in-
directly on nearby objects (e.g., tables, light bulbs). Such
vibrations can be sensed and analyzed using photoelectric sen-
sors [9,30,33,34,38,51], ultrasonic sensors [20,22,24,40,54],
and other sensor modalities [2, 4, 12, 32, 44, 46, 53]. Recently,
compact and low-cost millimeter-wave (mmWave) radars
have emerged as promising tools for acoustic eavesdropping
attacks [6,10,13,17,18,26,28,35,41,49], owing to their high
sensitivity and capability to detect subtle vibrations. However,
existing attacks exhibit two notable limitations. First, they
often assume that the target audio comprises predeﬁned cat-
egorical content (e.g., letters or digits), and that the attacker
possesses prior knowledge of these categories. Second, they
suffer from limited generalizability due to their dependence on
stable reﬂective surfaces (e.g., smartphone back panels), their
inability to handle context-dependent speech, and their lack
of multimodal data integration capabilities. Representative
acoustic eavesdropping studies are summarized in Table 1.

In this work, we propose a new acoustic eavesdropping
attack, EchoLLM, leveraging a mmWave radar and large lan-
guage model (LLM). In particular, the adversary aims to re-
cover the audio contents from a bone conduction headphone
the victim wears. We outline the research goals of the pro-
posed attack in the following.
(1) Context-aware eavesdropping.In real-world scenar-
ios, such as phone calls or video conferences, users not only
listen to others’ voice through devices like bone conduction
headphones but also respond by speaking. When reconstruct-
ing audio from a victim’s device (e.g., a bone conduction
headphone) during an attack, we expect that the victim’s re-
sponse can provide the crucial semantic context that signiﬁ-
cantly enhances the attack. In the proposed attack, we intend
to exploit such a semantic context effectively.
(2) Robust attack with multi-modal fusion.A robust
eavesdropping attack in our targeted scenarios needs to coun-
teract the adverse effects of various factors such as complex
attack environments. We thus design our attack with a multi-
modal fusion feature that combines two data sources together:
one from the mmWave radar aiming at the victim’s head-
phone and the other from the microphone aiming at the victim
themselves. Such a feature is critical in practical where one
modality (e.g., radar signal) could be compromised severely.
(3) Stealthy and low-cost attack.The proposed attack is
designed to be discreet, undetectable, and cost-effective. In-
stead of using expensive lab setup or chunky sensors (e.g.,
photoelectric or ultrasonic), we choose to use a low-cost com-
mercial mmWave radar for EchoLLM. Aside from the offered
feasibility, the small size of such mmWave radars also helps
render our attack stealthy.
(4) Target-aware and motion-resilient attack.We aim
to design our attack to be target-aware and motion-resilient
so that it is highly applicable in practical. First, in many at-
tack scenarios, there exist different reﬂection sources (e.g.,
furniture in the environment) that cause interference to the
desired mmWave signal from the victim’s headphone. Second,
in practical, the victim could move their head spontaneously
in different directions (e.g. left-right) during an attack, which
can disrupt the attacker’s effort to extract the subtle vibrations
(in μm level) of the victim’s headphone. Our attack needs to
counteract the above adverse conditions.
To perform a non-invasive acoustic eavesdropping attack
achieving the above research goals, we need to properly ad-
dress three major challenges:
(1) How do we accurately distinguish the reﬂected mmW ave
signals from the target object among many others in the envi-
ronment? We tackle this challenge in two steps, based on our
observations and intuition. First, we differentiate the victim
from objects like tables or nearby pedestrians by recognizing
that they exhibit very distinct velocity characteristics. Next,
we further distinguish the reﬂected signal from the victim’s
headphones from those of the victim’s body parts (such as
head and arms) by leveraging the observation that the former
contains audio information while the latter does not.
(2) How do we mitigate the interference induced by the vic-
tim’s spontaneous movements during an attack?Minimizing
the impact of inevitable interference is crucial, as the ‘useful’
vibration we aim to extract is inherently small and easily over-
shadowed. In particular, the victim’s spontaneous movements
introduce large ‘spikes’ into the reﬂected mmWave signal,
which obscure the subtle vibrations from the headphones.
To address this challenge, we propose our SpikeFree algo-
rithm in EchoLLM, which uses a Long Short-Term Memory
(LSTM) model to interpolate signal values between consec-
utive radar frames and then smooths the received mmWave
signal through averaging and offset calibration.
(3) How do we effectively leverage multi-modal fusion and
unique context for recognizing the audio contents?To tackle
this challenge, we explore the relationship between the two
modalities used in our attack and conjecture that the audios
from the victim’ headphone and those of the victim them-
selves are highly dependent on each other as they respond
to each other. We thus concatenate the two data sources and
use the fused signal as input (see Figure 9). Next, we design
an LLM-augmented speech recognition model to predict the
most likely text for a given fused signal input and output the
text corresponding to the audio from the victim’s headphone.

technology is for the radar to transmit periodic mmWave sig-

frequency of the transmitted signal increases linearly within
one period, denoted by

4 Methodology
4.1 Overview
As discussed earlier, the basic idea behind EchoLLM is that
bone conduction headphones transmit audio signals through

vibrations induced by the audio, which propagate from the
headphone to the user’s skull. An mmWave radar can detect
these tiny vibrations when its FMCW signal is reﬂected by
the targeted headphones (see Section 2). However, obtaining
high-quality vibrations (and thus corresponding audios) is a
signiﬁcant challenge due to several factors: (1) the vibration
amplitude is extremely small (on the order of μm), (2) there
are strong interferences, such as the victim’s head movements
and reﬂected mmWave signals from various distances, and (3)
vibration signals extracted by mmWave radars often lack the
high-frequency components (≥ 1.0 kHz) required by existing
speech enhancement techniques. Furthermore, EchoLLM is
designed to infer the exact content of human audios, rather
than just categorical data like numbers and letters.
Figure 2 illustrates the pipeline of the proposed EchoLLM
attack, consisting of three key steps: vibration extraction, sig-
nal enhancement, and context-aware ASR. In the vibration
extraction, we ﬁrst compute the baseband signal v(t) from the
received signal s(t), isolate components related to the victim
and their headphone, and then extract phase information pro-
portional to vibration. In the signal enhancement, we remove
the distortions from background reﬂections and the victim’s
head motions, which typically result in large low-frequency
reﬂections that interfere with the signal. Finally, in context-
aware ASR, we introduce LeASR, an LLM-augmented speech
recognition model that combines the extracted mmWave sig-
nal from the headphone and the victim’s recorded audio to
compensate for missing high-frequency components in the
extracted vibrations. Each step is explained in detail below.

4.2.1 Victim v.s. Other Objects
To identify the speciﬁc crest corresponding to the victim, we
divide objects in typical indoor environments, i.e., people,
furniture, and appliances, into three categories based on their
motion characteristics: moving objects, ﬁxtures, and quasi-
stationary objects that are nearly static with small movements.
Particularly in our interested scenarios, examples of moving
objects can be running pets, autonomous vacuum cleaners,
and walking people, those of ﬁxtures are desks and sofas,
and those of quasi-stationary objects are stationary people
(seated or standing) with small body movements like shaking

4.3 Signal Enhancement
In this step, we enhance the quality of the extracted vibration
signal by addressing two key factors. First, even with the cor-
rect location for the victim’s headphone, the reﬂected signals
often include contributions from other sources, such as the
victim’s face, ears, hair, etc, which reduce the SNR. Second,
as introduced earlier, the extracted vibration from the radar is
inevitably affected by the victim’s head movements, which
deteriorates the extraction performance as well. In what fol-
lows, we would use background reﬂection reduction and head
motion calibration to reduce the above impact.

4.4 Context-Aware Multi-Modal ASR
As shown in Figure 1, the attacker’s goal is to infer the exact
content of the audio signal (i.e., the callee’s speech) playing
through the victim’s headphone. To that end, the attacker is
assumed to use a mmWave radar to extract the vibrations
corresponding to the callee’s speech and a wireless micro-
phone to record the victim’s own speech. In what follows, we
introduce the design of our LLM-augmented contExt-aware
multi-modal ASR (LeASR) model, which exploits the two data
sources (i.e., one from the mmWave radar and the other from
the microphone) to predict the callee’s speech.
Speciﬁcally, LeASR incorporates two modules that signif-
icantly enhance the performance of predicting the contents
(i.e., text) of the callee’s speech. The ﬁrst one is a feature
alignment module that concatenate the embeddings of the
victim’s speech and those of the mmWave signal (correspond-
ing to the callee’s speech) together. In doing so, we exploit
the victim’s speech as one modality and useful context for
inferring the contents of the callee’s speech. The second mod-
ule adopts an encoder-decoder architecture that we train and
leverage for speech recognition. Notably, both the encoder
and decoder are based on large language models (LLMs),
enhancing the robustness of speech recognition even when
the input mmWave signal is of low quality.

4.4.2 Model Training
We use the widely adopted auto-regressive cross-entropy loss
to train LeASR, which measures the difference between the
predicted probability distribution of the next token and the ac-
tual tokens in the training data. The loss function aims to ﬁnd
the most likely text sequence corresponding to the input. It is
deﬁned as Loss = −∑T
t=1 logP(yt |y1,y2,..., yt−1,X), where
X is the feature vector of the input speech, yt is the target
output (i.e. the ground-truth text) at time t, and T is the total
length of the text sequence.
Working with limited training data.In our case, a train-
ing sample consists of a pair of recorded audio from the caller
and the vibration of a bone conduction headphone correspond-
ing to the callee’s speech. The challenge lies in the scarcity of
open-source conversational speech data and the impracticality
of collecting large amounts of mmWave samples for training.
To tackle such a challenge, we ﬁrst use existing speech syn-
thesis tools to synthesize conversational audios. See Sect. 5.3
for details. Next, we use a two-stage ﬁne-tuning process [50]
to reduce the need for a large number of mmWave samples
for training. Speciﬁcally, in the ﬁrst stage, we use both the
noise obtained from real environments using a mmWave radar
and the synthesized conversational audios to build a large syn-
thetic dataset. Later on, we pre-train LeASR on this dataset so
that the model can differentiate the three components within
one input sample: the caller’s audio, BOS (i.e., the speech
recognizer), and the callee’s audio. In the second stage, we
further ﬁne-tune
LeASR using a small dataset consisting of
pairs of self-collected data (both the caller’s audio signal and
the callee’s mmWave signal) to optimize its performance. As
demonstrated in Sect. 5, LeASR only uses 3,000 samples for
ﬁne-turning while achieving as low as 5.23% WER.
5 Performance Evaluation
5.1 How does EchoLLM Work in Practical?
To launch an eavesdropping attack using EchoLLM, the at-
tacker ﬁrst sets up the radar and voice recording device to
capture both the victim’s headphone vibration and speech.
Then, the attacker conﬁgures system parameters and runs the
EchoLLM code to extract the audio content (text output from
ASR). Recommended system setup: 1) Set the microphone’s
sampling rate to 8 kHz. 2) Conﬁgure the mmWave radar to
emit chirp signals every 125 μs, resulting in a 8 kHz sampling
rate. 3) Set vu to 0.6m /s and vl to 0.05 m/s to distinguish be-
tween moving objects, ﬁxtures, and quasi-stationary humans,
with a minimum velocity resolution of 0.03 m/s. 4) Resam-
ple the model input (victim’s speech, BOS, and headphone
vibration) to 16 kHz, and pass it to LeASR for text extraction.
5.2 System Setup
The data collection system used in our experiments includes
an FMCW radar board (IWR1443BOOST, $300), a data cap-
ture board (DCA1000EVM, $600), and a wireless micro-
phone (BOYA mini, $30). The IWR1443BOOST features
three transmit and four receive antennas, and operates in the
76-81 GHz frequency range. Its analog-to-digital converter
(ADC) supports a sampling rate of up to 37.5 million samples
per second. Detailed conﬁguration parameters are provided in
Appendix 12.3. Data are processed on a server, with speciﬁca-
tions listed in Appendix 12.4. Additional costs (e.g., API calls
and training) total approximately $60, bringing the overall
system cost to $990.

We experiment with four LLMs:HuBART uses HuBERT [16]

5.5 Performance Metrics
We adopt word error rate(WER) as our main evaluation met-
ric for EchoLLM. WER measures the discrepancy between the
model’s output and ground-truth by comparing the output
words to the ground-truth. It is calcuated as WER = I+D+S
N ×
100%, where I is the number of insertions, D is deletions,
and S is substitutions. N is the total number of words in the
ground-truth sentence. Obviously, a low WER indicates better
performance of ASR model used.
Note that in our case, the input to LeASR includes both the
callee’s and caller’s speech. Since the attacker is assumed to
have already recorded the caller’s speech, we compute WER
only for the callee’s speech, i.e., the vibrations extracted from
the headphones, excluding the caller’s speech and BOS.

5.6.2 Ablation Study
We conducted ablation experiments to evaluate the roles of
context, audio enhancement, and mmWave radar sensing.
Impact of context: To evaluate the impact of context
(i.e., caller’s speech) on attack performance, we compare
three setups on synthetic and real datasets: 1) caller–callee
(caller speaks ﬁrst), 2) callee–caller (reversed order), and 3)
no context (callee speech only). Results in Table 3, based
on HuBART-L, indicate that: 1) Incorporating context greatly
enhances attack performance, reducing WER on real data from
28.39% (no context) to 5.23% (caller–callee). 2) Synthetic
data yields even lower WER (1.38%). 3) WER for callee–caller
is consistently slightly higher than for caller–callee, increas-
ing from 1.38% to 1.66% on synthetic datasets. This trend
aligns with LLMs’ self-attention tendency to prioritize earlier
and cleaner input [47]. Additional experimental results and
examples are provided in Appendices 12.8 and 12.9.

5.6.3 Attack Robustness
To comprehensively evaluateEchoLLM’s robustness, we exam-
ine the impact of varying noise levels, diverse environments,
and different bone conduction headphones.
Different noise levels: To assess environmental noise im-
pact on bone conduction headphones vibrations, we used
the SA-model, playing TUT noise samples [14]a t3 0 –70 dB
(10 dB steps) while capturing vibrations with mmWave radar.
As shown in Figure 15(a), environmental noise had minimal
effect: WER of HuBART increased by only 2% as noise rose
from 30 dB to 70 dB. To validate this, we transmitted 0–3 kHz
linear frequency modulation signals through the bone con-
duction headphones and measured the signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR) under the same noise levels. Figure 15(b) shows stable
SNRs (e.g., 16.19 at 30 dB and 15.06 at 70 dB). This suggests
that bone conduction headphones materials attenuate external
noise and captured vibrations mainly reﬂect internal audio.
Different environments: To evaluate EchoLLM’s robust-
ness across environments, we performed cross-evaluation us-
ing environment-speciﬁc HuBART-L models on text inputs
from different acoustic settings. As shown in Figure 16(a),
the performance of the ﬁne-tuned models varied only slightly,
indicating that ﬁrst-stage training with noise-augmented syn-
thetic data already imparts strong robustness, reducing the
need for environment-speciﬁc ﬁne-tuning. A marginal WER
increase with higher noise levels further suggests that en-
vironmental noise has limited but noticeable impact. More
experimental results are provided in Appendix 12.10.

5.6.4 User Diversity Study
To evaluate the impact of user diversity on attack performance,
we recruited 18 participants across 9 groups based on a 3 ×3

5.6.6 EchoLLM vs. Other Eavesdropping Attacks
To compare EchoLLM with existing mmWave eavesdropping

methods and its own performance under bone conduction
headphones-based conditions. These results highlight that: 1)
physical properties such as target size and material signiﬁ-

6 Related Work
6.1 Acoustic Eavesdropping Attacks
Acoustic signals can be transmitted through air, headphones,
loudspeakers, etc. In recent years, many eavesdropping at-
tacks have been explored to utilize different sensors (e.g.,
photoelectric sensors [9, 30, 33, 34, 38, 51], inertial measure-
ment unit (IMU) sensors [2, 4, 12, 32, 53], ultrasonic Doppler
sensors [20, 22, 24, 40, 54], WI-FI devices [44]) to obtain vi-
brations induced by sounds for reconstructing the original
audio. These attacks have been known to suffer from critical
drawbacks. For example, the authors in [30, 51] utilized the
laser radar to acquire sound and successfully attacked devices
such as robot vacuum cleaners [38]. However, laser radars are
vulnerable to external environments such as rain and snow
and require the targeted objects to be within line-of-sight.
6.2 Attacks Based on mmWave Radar
Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of using
mmWave radar for side-channel attacks by capturing acous-
tic or mechanical vibrations to reconstruct private informa-
tion [6, 10, 13, 17, 18, 26, 28, 35, 41, 49]. These attacks can
be categorized based on acoustic source types: active acous-
tic sources(e.g., loudspeakers, human throats) and passive
acoustic sources (e.g., tables, light bulbs). Active-source
attacks directly detect vibrations from the speaker or de-
vice. For example, Li et al. [28] captured screen vibrations
to infer on-screen content, while WaveEar [49] recovered
speech by sensing throat vibrations in noisy environments.
Subsequent studies enhanced speaker recognition and speech
detection using mmWave signals [13, 26]. Other studies, such
as MILLIEAR [18], mmSpy [6], and mmWave-Whisper [7],
focused on eavesdropping phone conversations by capturing
speech radiated from smartphones. Passive-source attacks,
by contrast, extract secondary vibrations from nearby objects.
Radiomic [35] proposed reconstructing speech via micro-
vibrations on non-contact surfaces. mmPhone [41] augmented
sensing with piezoelectric ﬁlms to address weak vibration sce-
narios. mmEcho [17] departed from learning-based methods,
instead using signal processing to recover intelligible speech
from radar reﬂections off everyday indoor objects.
Differences inEchoLLM. Compared to existing mmWave
radar-based eavesdropping attacks, EchoLLM introduces four
key distinctions: 1) As an active acoustic source attack, it
eliminates the need for dedicated sound-absorbing materials
required in passive and non-contact scenarios. 2) It uniquely
leverages the victim’s own audio as contextual input to im-
prove headphone-transmitted speech recognition. 3) Bone
conduction headphones produce signiﬁcantly weaker high-
frequency reﬂections (≥1.0 kHz) compared to rigid devices
or reﬂective human surfaces, due to their coin-size reﬂective
area and low reﬂectivity of silicone or rubber materials. 4)
EchoLLM supports recognition of unconstrained speech con-
tent rather than being limited to ﬁxed-vocabulary scenarios.
7 Discussions
Limitations. As discussed in Section 5.6.1, we found that
when the volume drops below 50dB or the distance exceeds
120 cm, the WER of EchoLLM can reach up to 30%. This
limitation is mainly due to the mmWave radar’s transmis-
sion power and the bone conduction headset’s reﬂection area.
According to the IWR1443 object-distance relationship data
provided by TI7, even a metal-surfaced coin with a large radar
cross-section achieves a maximum detection range of about
100 cm. To extend the attack range, we could use a higher-
power radar, such as the IWR1642, which offers 12.5 dBm
(about 17.8mW) of transmission power, offering a notable
advantage over the IWR1443’s 12 dBm (about 15.8mW).
Countermeasures. Based on performance evaluation, two
countermeasures against EchoLLM emerge: 1) Victims can
prevent audio recording by employing scanning techniques to
detect hidden recorders or by raising privacy awareness, and
2) Victims can introduce more head movements, especially
during sensitive conversations, to disrupt signal capture.
8 Conclusion
To conclude, we propose EchoLLM in this paper, a novel
mmWave-based eavesdropping attack on bone conduction
headphones, which has not been explored yet. EchoLLM fea-
tures two key designs that contribute to strong attack perfor-
mance: incorporating the victim’s own speech as the context
for generating the exact text for the audio from the victim’s
headphone, and LLM for text generation counteracting the
challenge due to lack of critical frequency components. Ex-
tensive evaluations over synthetic and self-collected data and
on a range of factors conﬁrm both the low WER and the high
robustness of EchoLLM. Our research thus calls for further
protection for this new type of headsets.

12.1 CNN-based Voice Activity Detector
The CNN-based voice activity detector performs binary classi-
ﬁcation to determine whether the input spectrogram contains
speech. It comprises three convolutional layers, each followed
by a max-pooling layer, and two fully connected layers. The
number of channels increases progressively to 32, 64, and 128.
After convolution and pooling, the feature maps are ﬂattened
into a one-dimensional vector of length 8,192, which is then
processed by the fully connected layers. To mitigate overﬁt-
ting, a Dropout layer is introduced, and a Sigmoid activation
function outputs the probability of speech presence. During
training, binary cross-entropy loss (i.e., BCELoss) is used,
and the Adam optimizer updates the model parameters. Exper-
imental results indicate that the model achieves an accuracy
of 98.8% in detecting speech in the spectrogram.

12.3 Detailed mmWave Radar Conﬁguration
This section describes the mmWave radar conﬁguration em-
ployed for phase-based audio signal extraction. For each radar
chirp, we identify the FFT peak corresponding to the bone
conduction headphones vibration signal and extract its phase.
Since the extracted phase is subsequently converted into au-
dio, the chirp’s transmission frequency directly determines
the resulting phase sampling rate. Key chirp parameters in-
ﬂuencing the sampling rate are summarized in Table 12.I n
our conﬁguration, the resulting audio sampling rate is 8 kHz,
implying that, according to the Nyquist theorem [ 23], fre-
quency components up to 4 kHz can be captured. This range
is sufﬁcient for most speech recognition applications.

12.7 Architectures of LeASR
Here, we brieﬂy introduce the various LeASR architectures
employed in the experiments.
HuBERT-BARTis an encoder-decoder architecture. The
encoder employs the self-supervised speech model HuBERT
(Hidden-Unit BERT), built on the Transformer architecture,
speciﬁcally designed to learn effective feature representa-
tions from unlabeled speech data. Its architecture includes
a front end with seven 1D convolutional layers, responsible
for processing raw audio signals and extracting local tempo-
ral features. The core component is a 12-layer Transformer
encoder, with each layer comprising 768 hidden units and
12 attention heads, leveraging self-attention mechanisms to
capture global contextual information from the audio. The
HuBERT model contains approximately 94 million parame-
ters, with 9.4 million in the convolutional front end and 84.6
million in the Transformer encoder. This model efﬁciently
learns audio features, making it well-suited for downstream
tasks like automatic speech recognition (ASR), while reduc-
ing the need for large amounts of labeled data. The decoder
directly uses the decoder part of BART (Bidirectional and
Auto-Regressive Transformers), which is widely applied in
tasks such as text generation, summarization, and translation.
The decoder comprises 12 layers, each with a hidden size of
768, a feed-forward network dimension of 3,072, and 12 at-
tention heads, totaling approximately 139M parameters. The
decoder generates output using autoregressive decoding.
HuBERT-BART-Largeis an encoder-decoder architec-
ture. The encoder utilizes HuBERT, while the decoder lever-
ages BART-Large, an extended version of BART with a larger
model size and enhanced modeling capabilities. BART-Large
retains 12 encoder and 12 decoder layers, but with an in-
creased hidden size of 1,024, a feed-forward network dimen-
sion of 4,096, and 16 attention heads, totaling approximately
407M parameters. The larger model size enables it to capture
more complex semantics and long-range dependencies, im-
proving coherence and accuracy in text generation, making
it particularly suitable for tasks requiring high-quality output
and sophisticated language understanding.
Whisper is an efﬁcient speech recognition and processing
model based on a Transformer encoder-decoder architecture,
with 74M parameters. The model consists of 6 encoder and 6
decoder layers, each containing 512 hidden units and 8 self-
attention heads. The feed-forward network has 2,048 hidden
units. Whisper processes mel-spectrograms extracted from
speech signals and supports multiple languages, making it
suitable for cross-lingual speech recognition tasks. Pretrained
on a large-scale multilingual dataset, the model excels in
handling diverse accents and language styles, providing robust
solutions for speech recognition and translation.
SpeechT5 is a pre-trained model for multi-task speech
processing, containing 310 M parameters. It features 12 en-
coder layers and 12 decoder layers, each with 768 hidden
units and 12 self-attention heads. The feed-forward network
includes 3,072 hidden units. The model supports a vocabulary
size ranging from 30,000 to 50,000 and can process input
sequences up to 512 tokens. Leveraging the Transformer ar-
chitecture, SpeechT5 captures long-range dependencies via
self-attention, facilitating knowledge sharing across various
speech tasks and enhancing generalization and efﬁciency.
