Abstract—Optical image stabilization (OIS), powered by a
special micro-electromechanical structure in the camera lenses to
compensate for the optical distortion caused by camera shakes,
has become an indispensable feature in many smartphones.
However, we discover that this seemingly benign component
can be exploited to eavesdrop on nearby audio signals, posing
a signiﬁcant threat to people’s privacy during conversations or
phone calls. Speciﬁcally, the OIS component can be inﬂuenced
by external acoustic stimuli leading to slight vibrations, and at
the same time, the coil and magnetized components inside the
OIS induce electromagnetic leakage as they vibrate, according
to Faraday’s Law of Electromagnetic Induction. This electro-
magnetic leakage contains voice information that can be used to
recover the audio signals if intercepted by individuals with mali-
cious intent. Inspired by the above discovery, we proposeOISMic,
a new acoustic eavesdropping attack that takes advantage of
sound-induced OIS vibrations on smartphones. Unlike other
existing acoustic eavesdropping attacks, eavesdropping exploiting
OIS vibrations not only overcomes the constraints imposed by
system permissions for many sensor-based approaches but is also
immune to ultrasonic jammer that hinders the methods relying
on microwave or light reﬂections to sense sound-induced vibra-
tions. To execute this non-trivial attack in practical scenarios,
we developed a prototype circuit that has a compact design
capable of capturing the electromagnetic leakage caused by OIS
vibrations. After converting the collected leaked electromagnetic
signals into audio signals, a software-based phase-locked loop
(PLL) method is developed to enhance the representation of
voice components. Meanwhile, to reconstruct the weak audio
signals, we also designed a diffusion-based neural network
to learn the distribution of electromagnetic noise within the
audio spectrum. Extensive experiments indicate thatOISMic can
accurately reconstruct voice under various scenarios, achieving
an average word correct rate of 90.57% across different devices.

B. Experimental Validation
In order to further validate the aforementioned theoretical
analysis, we formulated three key research questions about
OIS voice information leakage:
Exp-1. Does electromagnetic leakage from the OIS module
exist and can it be captured? As shown in Figure 3, we used a
speaker to play a 75 dB 0-2 kHz sweep signal as a reference,
placed inside a high-permeability µ-metal shield to eliminate
electromagnetic interference. The speaker was positioned 1
m away from the device under test (iPhone 8) to simulate
common usage scenarios and ensure sound propagation. The
iPhone 8 was ﬁxed with its camera facing downwards, while
the receiving coil was secured under the table, directly beneath
the iPhone 8’s camera. The distance between the two was kept
at 15 mm, with a wooden tabletop in between, to effectively
capture the weak electromagnetic signals produced by the OIS
module. To prevent the receiving coil from being affected
by potential electromagnetic leakage from the speaker, we
used additional high-permeability materials for extra shielding.
This experimental setup aims to accurately detect and analyze
the electromagnetic leakage signals generated by the OIS
module, while ensuring the controllability of the experimental
environment and the reliability of the results. The purpose
of this experiment is to verify the existence and capturability
of electromagnetic leakage caused by the OIS module when
subjected to external audio signals. The results in Figures 4(a)
and 4(b) show that the coil captured electromagnetic leakage
and converted it into a 0-2 kHz sweep signal without non-
linear effects. The spectrogram reveals a declining frequency
response, indicating that the OIS module responds weakly
to high-frequency sounds and converts more effectively at
lower frequencies. Signal quality and strength decrease with
increasing frequency, highlighting the challenges in extracting
voice information using this method.
Exp-2. Is the OIS module speciﬁcally responsible for the ob-
served electromagnetic leakage? We further veriﬁed whether
this phenomenon was caused by the OIS camera module. To
do this, we removed the camera module from the iPhone 8 and
conducted tests under the same experimental conditions. The
result, as shown in Figure 4(c), strongly suggests that after
removing the OIS camera module, the previously observed
conversion phenomenon from audio signals to electromagnetic
leakage almost completely disappears. This result further
conﬁrms that the OIS module played a crucial role in the
acoustic-electromagnetic conversion observed earlier. Without
the OIS module, the phone is almost incapable of converting
audio signals into detectable electromagnetic leakage.
Exp-3. Can the OIS-based electromagnetic leakage capture
complex audio signals from various sources? We used a piano
as the sound source because it does not generate electro-
magnetic leakage. During the experiment, sounds emitted by
the speaker and piano were recorded simultaneously using a
receiving coil and a microphone. The results are shown in Fig-
ure 4(d). The OIS module effectively transduced piano audio
into electromagnetic emissions, performing well at lower fre-
quencies but showing reduced sensitivity and increased noise
at higher frequencies. Future improvements should focus on
specialized signal reception circuits, low-noise ampliﬁers, and
advanced audio enhancement algorithms. These advancements
could signiﬁcantly improve system efﬁciency and broaden its
application in acoustic-electromagnetic conversion.
The above experiments rule out the possibility of other
side-channels that may cause voice information leakage and
conﬁrm our theoretical analysis and the feasiblity of acoustic
eavesdropping exploiting sound-induced OIS vibrations.

III. OISM IC DESIGN
A. Overview

OIS vibrations on smartphones for achieving covert voice
eavesdropping. To capture the weak electromagnetic leakage
caused by OIS vibrations, we developed a specialized Electro-

hardware design employing multi-turn coils and ferrite cores
for efﬁcient signal reception and a differential ampliﬁer to sup-
press common-mode noise. Once the leaked electromagnetic
signals are collected and converted into electric signals, where
the original audio is embedded, we applied PLL-based V oice

B. Electromagnetic Leakage Harvesting
To capture the unique side-channel signals from the OIS
module, the hardware conﬁguration of OISMic mainly consists
of a receiving coil and a signal processing circuit.
Coil Design. To capture high-ﬁdelity signals and reduce
interference, we implemented a series-connected dual-coil
design, with ferrite cores placed at the center of each coil.
The dual coils are wound in opposite directions, causing the
electromotive forces generated by external interference signals
in the two coils to cancel each other out, thereby reducing
common-mode noise. This dual-coil setup is connected to the
Signal Input port of the OISMic hardware circuit, serving as
a differential signal input.
Signal Processing Circuit Design. To effectively process
the electromagnetic signals received by the coil from the OIS
system, we have designed an efﬁcient signal acquisition and
processing circuit. The electromagnetic leakage signals gener-
ated by the OIS stabilization module are extremely weak and
mixed with signiﬁcant noise. Therefore, we need a circuit that
can accurately amplify the signal and effectively suppress the
noise. This circuit mainly consists of a differential ampliﬁer,
post-stage ampliﬁer, DC blocking and low-pass ﬁlters, as illus-
trated in Figures 6(a) and 6(b). Our front end uses the INA849
instrumentation ampliﬁer, which can receive and amplify the
differential components of the input signal while suppressing
common-mode noise. Through a precise resistor network and
high-precision operational ampliﬁers, the INA849 ensures high
gain accuracy and low noise performance. The differential
input design effectively ampliﬁes the difference between the
two input signals, signiﬁcantly enhancing the signal-to-noise
ratio. After ampliﬁcation by the INA849, the signal proceeds
to the post-stage ampliﬁcation circuit for further enhancement.
Utilizing a standard operational ampliﬁer, this stage ensures
the signal reaches the desired amplitude. The signal, routed
through resistors and coupling capacitors, undergoes additional
reﬁnement, improving its strength and clarity. Finally, the
circuit includes a DC blocking and low-pass ﬁlter section
to eliminate DC components and high-frequency noise. The
DC blocking capacitor removes any DC component from the
output signal, while the low-pass ﬁlter retains low-frequency
components and removes high-frequency noise, enhancing
signal quality and stability. For data output, the information
can be transmitted via a wireless microphone module or stored
locally, offering ﬂexible options for data handling and analysis.

PLL Computational Implementation. In severe electro-
magnetic noise, it is impossible to obtain the audio signal
through denoising models directly. To address this issue,
we introduce phase-locked loop (PLL) technology, leveraging
the synchronization effects known to exist in the auditory
system to robustly extract and enhance the audio signal. The
auditory system ﬁrst decomposes the input signal using a
bank of band-pass ﬁlters and then employs a group of PLLs
to capture frequency information present at speciﬁc moments
in time. Subsequently, this frequency distribution information
is converted into a synchronization effect-based spectrogram
representation. This approach not only effectively suppresses
electromagnetic noise but also enhances the clarity and quality
of the audio signal.
First, the input audio signal is decomposed using a set of
carefully designed band-pass ﬁlters. These ﬁlters are biologi-
cally inspired [7] and feature asymmetric, overlapping charac-
teristics. To ensure they effectively target the electromagnetic
characteristics of the audio signal, they are further modiﬁed
accordingly. Consequently, these ﬁlters cover the frequency
range from 50 Hz to 8000 Hz. Speciﬁcally, 387 FIR ﬁlters of
order 2048 are used.
Next, the output of each ﬁlter is connected to a PLL.
A PLL consists of a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO), a
phase detector (PD), and a loop ﬁlter, which are used to
obtain synchrony information and frequency estimation of the
signal. The PLL parameters are optimized to achieve the best
performance under noisy conditions.

We designed a specialized hardware device to conduct
acoustic eavesdropping attacks. The hardware part of our
OISMic system includes a designed differential-mode signal
receiver and a signal ampliﬁcation circuit to capture and
convert the leaked near-ﬁeld electromagnetic signals into au-

concealed underneath the table for eavesdropping, as shown

personal computer, where the audio data is initially processed
through the PLL voice enhancement algorithm and further
enhanced using a diffusion model. The audio processing and
enhancement codes are implemented in Python. In our experi-

electromagnetic channel speaker and record the human voices

Overall, these approaches typically rely on a well-established
acoustic side channel with direct observation and adequate
lighting, which limits their practicality in everyday scenarios.
Wireless-based. Wireless-based approaches spy on voice
information by analyzing wireless signals reﬂected from vi-
brating objects. For instance, Wei et al. [14] inspects subtle
disturbances on a target loudspeaker and recovers the audio
signal by analyzing the received signal strength (RSS) read-
ings of WiFi packets. Moreover, researchers also use RFID
tags [15] and mmWave radar [16] to detect reﬂected vibration
signals for eavesdropping. These methods enable acoustic
eavesdropping through physical barriers on various wireless
devices, but require stable wireless conditions and advanced
signal processing, potentially lacking robustness in complex
environments.
Unlike previous works, OISMic uses magnetic side-channel
signals for acoustic eavesdropping, leveraging the OIS module
in phones to covertly intercept audio without needing proxim-
ity to the target or sacriﬁcing its stealthiness. Furthermore,
this method has no requirements for illumination or line of
sight, posing a signiﬁcant security threat to sensitive voice
information.
VI. C
ONCLUSION
In this paper, we proposeOISMic, a new acoustic eavesdrop-
ping attack that exploits the leaked electromagnetic signals
caused by the built-in OIS vibrations on smartphones. To
intercept the weak electromagnetic leakage induced by OIS
vibrations, we developed a compact prototype circuit operating
at dual-coil differential mode to suppress common-mode noise.
To effectively recover weak audio signals in complex electro-
magnetic environments, we designed a software-based PLL
method and a diffusion-based model for boosting the voice
component and denoising, respectively. Extensive experiments
have demonstrated that OISMic can accurately reconstruct
audio for achieving an average WCR of 90.57% across dif-
ferent devices, with our prototype device achieving a SNR of
10.25 dB from the OIS electromagnetic leakage. Furthermore,
even when subjected to interference from ultrasonic jammers,
OISMic still achieves high speech recognition accuracy. This
implies that an attacker could potentially eavesdrop on users’
private conversations to steal sensitive information, such as
passwords, locations, and phone numbers, posing signiﬁcant
threats to user privacy and property security.
To counter this vulnerability, we recommend enhancing the
electromagnetic shielding of OIS components, developing sig-
nal obfuscation techniques to mask electromagnetic leakage,
and considering the replacement of magnetic structure optical
image stabilization modules with new technologies, such as
SMA-based optical stabilization.
