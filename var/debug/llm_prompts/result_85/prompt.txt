In this paper, we present MoiréTag—a novel tag-like device that magnifies displacement without active
components for indirect sensing of subtle tangible interactions. The device consists of two overlapping layers
of stripe patterns with distinct pattern frequencies. These layers create Moiré fringes that can move faster
than the actual movement of a layer. Using a customized image processing pipeline, we show that MoiréTag
can reliably detect sub-mm movement in real-time (mean error = 0.043 mm) under varying lighting conditions,
camera angles, and camera distances. We also demonstrate five applications of MoiréTag to showcase its
potential as a low-cost solution to capture and monitor small changes in movement and other physical
properties, such as force and volume, by converting them into displacement.

1 Introduction
With the advancements in computing technologies, computers are now more closely connected to
the real world than ever. Computers sense and utilize interactions that happen in the real world,
such as tapping and scratching of a surface [12, 13, 41], and interacting with physical objects [17],
enabling tangible interactions in scenarios where traditional user interfaces are not available or
not effective. Many of these are done through active sensors worn on the user’s body [17, 41] or
attached to the object surface [12, 13]. Some use conductive material to cover the object surface
to sense interaction with the surface [ 44]. While useful, these methods need wires and active
components to be worn on the user’s body or active circuits to be instrumented on the object
surface.
On the other hand, recent advancements in computer vision and machine learning algorithms
enabled non-contact indirect sensing of real-world input using a camera. For example, MediaPipe
[1] and DeepPose [34] allow human body posture and movement to be tracked with a regular 2D
camera by using deep learning algorithms, and YOLACT allows sensing and tracking of objects in
a video [3] without instrumenting the user or the object with active sensors. These vision-based
methods have the limitation of precision and stability of the sensed movement, making it more
suitable for sensing large-scale movements such as moving an arm or a hand. However, in the real
world, people also utilize small movements, such as precisely aligning objects, and even tiny actions
that may not be easily noticeable, such as applying a force on a physical object. Other studies have
shown that using laser speckles can enable high-accuracy sensing of small movements, such as
the movement of hand and object [ 45] and the movement caused by the force interaction [ 23].
However, methods that use laser speckle require a speckle projector and a speckle sensor [45] or a
defocused camera [23].
In this paper, we present MoiréTag, a novel low-cost tag that enables camera-based sensing of
precise movement from physical interactions. It utilizes the Moiré phenomenon, which is seen
when two repetitive patterns with similar spacings are superimposed in the form of dark and
light fringes. If one of the superimposed patterns moves, the Moiré fringe will also move, but at
a different rate than the pattern movement. This allows the small displacement caused by the
subtle interaction to be significantly magnified so that it can be easily captured by a regular camera.
This Moiré phenomenon has been shown to be effective in various applications, such as pose [21]
and camera position [37]. With the well-established theory framework of Moiré pattern as the
basis, MoiréTag provides an easy way for people to use the Moiré phenomenon to create and use
ad-hoc interfaces. MoiréTag consists of two overlapping paper layers that have stripe patterns
with different grating periods that create Moiré fringes that serve as a displacement magnifier for
a camera to precisely capture the displacement. We implemented an image processing pipeline
that recognizes the MoiréTag from a camera image and detects the movement of the Moiré fringes.
Our technical evaluation showed that MoiréTag and the image processing pipeline could enable
the sensing of sub-mm displacement using a regular smartphone camera with an average error of
0.043 mm. The evaluation also showed that the displacement could be reliably detected in various
lighting conditions and when captured from different camera angles and distances.
MoiréTags can be configured in various ways to be used for measuring different types of physical
properties. For example, MoiréTags with a handle attached to the moving layer can be used as a
tangible user interface for applications requiring multiple precise inputs, such as video editing.
When coupled with an elastic material, MoiréTags can serve as a force sensor by magnifying the
small displacement caused by the compression or extension of the elastic material. MoiréTags can
also be attached to a strap wrapped around the human chest to measure chest volume changes

2 Related Work
In this section, we summarize prior work on indirect interaction sensing methods and sensing
techniques that utilize Moiré patterns.
2.1 Non-contact Interaction Sensing Methods
Kinematic and kinetic interactions, such as moving objects and applying forces, are commonly used
in human-computer interaction. While these interactions are often sensed using active sensors
placed at the location of the interaction, many methods have been developed to enable the non-
contact sensing of such interactions.
Motion and displacement are commonly used to control computers, and using cameras is a
popular way of sensing the movements of hands and objects. Previous studies have shown that
using a depth camera can allow users to perform touch interactions on unequipped surfaces
[11, 38, 39]. Recent improvements in machine learning based computer vision algorithms enabled
reliable sensing and tracking of objects and body parts in a 2D image [ 1]. However, measuring
small changes in the movement using vision-based methods is challenging due to the resolution
of the images. Some methods use optical artifacts to enable high-precision position and motion
sensing with a camera. For example, SpeckleSense [ 45] uses the laser speckle projected to the
surface and achieves high-precision and high-speed tracking of hands without wearing any devices.
For sensing small changes in the video, Wu et al. demonstrated the use of spatial decomposition
and temporal filtering can magnify subtle color changes and motions[35]. Another approach is to
use the acoustic signal. Lamello [28] uses 3D-printed objects with comb-like structures that make
unique sounds as the user manipulates the objects so that the movement can be captured by a
microphone attached to the device.
Force is also a commonly used modality for interaction. Sensing forces often requires an active
sensor to be placed between two objects, which necessitates wires or batteries to be connected
to the sensor. Researchers have demonstrated that transforming force into wireless signals can
make the forces sensor a low-powered device and allow force to be measured and transmitted
using limited power sources such as energy harvesting [10]. However, this method may still require
an electric force sensor to be fabricated and installed at the force measuring point. To enable
force sensing without active components, researchers have been investigating camera-based force
sensing methods. Since force is not visible, many sensing methods measure the deformation of the
surface where the force is exerted. For example, Lee et al. introduced a camera-based approach
that captured the deformation of a viscoelastic mat when a person stands on it and subsequently
estimated their weight, which was the force applied to the mat [19]. Several methods have shown
that using optical artifacts and sensing the changes in the distribution of the optical artifacts caused
by the force-induced deformation could enable accurate force sensing without making contact.
For example, ForceSight estimates the magnitude of force applied by measuring the laser speckle
shifts on a deformed surface resulting from touch force [23]. Kamiyama et al. developed a method
that uses elastic blocks containing spherical markers [15] for capturing the traction fields using
the movement of spherical markers caused by the force interaction with a camera. Other methods
proposed to measure the interaction forces from the color changes of the fingertip using a camera

2.2 Moiré-based Sensing Methods
To enable the indirect vision-based sensing of small movements, we utilize Moiré patterns. Moiré
patterns are produced when two periodic patterns are superimposed [22], and they can amplify
the feature along one dimension compared to the original pattern [14]. The geometric properties
of Moiré patterns have been well studied [2]. The movement of the constituent layers of Moiré
patterns will lead to a more pronounced movement of the Moiré patterns themselves. Additionally,
if there is a gap between the planes of two layers, subtle camera movement can also lead to an
amplified movement of the Moiré patterns. Previous research has explored various approaches of
using Moiré patterns’ amplification characteristics to detect displacement at sub-millimeter levels
[25, 36, 43], offering an alternative to traditional sensors like the fiber optic displacement sensor
(FODS) [5, 40] and the laser displacement sensor (LDS) [8, 27]. Compared to traditional sensors
like LDS and FODS, camera-based displacement sensing using Moiré patterns is more robust to
environmental noise induced by sensor movement, measurement angle, inaccurate calibration, and
interfering signals. Other camera-based methods employing computer vision techniques may also
detect displacement by comparing pixel locations of corresponding features across frames [16, 20].
However, these approaches require camera stability or a stationary background, constraints that
do not apply to Moiré-based methods. In contrast to computer vision approaches that require
distinguishing between the object and the background, Moiré pattern sensing does not directly
sense the object itself, thereby avoiding this issue.
Because Moiré patterns can be generated in various ways and have the capability to consistently
show the movements of constituent layers or the camera, they have been used in many other
displacement-related measurement methods. Circular Moiré patterns have been used to measure
crack growth on the surface [26, 42]. Successful attempts have also been made to employ Moiré
patterns for tracking camera and object positions [7, 37], as well as advancing to pose estimation
[21, 24, 32, 33]. MoiréTag adopted the validated methodology of calculating real displacement from
Moiré pattern displacement and applied it in creating novel tangible user interfaces. Furthermore,
Moiré patterns may be used to indirectly measure subtle changes in other physical properties.
Takaki et al. [31] built an endoscopic surgical instrument that visualizes force applied with Moiré
patterns for surgeons by converting the force to Moiré patterns’ displacement using an elastic
structure. While this study demonstrated the potential of Moiré patterns in effectively visualizing
changes in force, it was designed for surgeons to visually monitor the force. Ding et al. [6] measured
intraocular pressure by monitoring changes in Moiré patterns formed by ring gratings on contact
lenses and pre-stored digital line gratings. Chen et al. [4] proposed a sheer stress sensor utilizing
Moiré patterns.
While there have been several attempts to use Moiré patterns for sensing and tracking objects [21],
visualizing interactions [31], and analyzing mechanical properties [4], little has been studied about
the use of Moiré patterns for indirectly sensing human interactions. Building upon the findings
from Moiré-based sensing methods, we present a low-cost tag-like module and accompanying
mechanisms to create various types of tangible interfaces for precise tangible interactions with just
a regular camera.

3.3 Image Processing Pipeline
The customized image processing pipeline can be summarized into the 6 steps below:
1) Receive input image and locate the MoiréTag using the convolutional neural network object
detection tool.
2) In the cropped image from step 1, start to locate rectangle areas within the white boarder for
displaying Moiré fringes by extracting all contours with four edge points and filtering out
the ones with incorrect height-to-width ratios.
3) Among the remaining contours, adjust the orientation based on the dark marker along the
white border. Iterate through the contours to crop, resize, and rectify the regions within them,
and stop once determined the cropped region is correct for proceeding.
4) Extract the binary ID attached to the MoiréTag.
5) Extract the average pixel location of fringes.
6) Mitigate the potential jittering induced by the contour detection method and output the
calculated displacement of the bottom paper layer 𝐵.
As shown in Figure 3, the pipeline begins by taking a frame from a live camera feed or a video as
the input. We use YOLACT [3], a convolution neural network object detection and segmentation
model trained on MoiréTag images, to locate the MoiréTag from the noisy background and at a
distance. We trained a custom YOLACT model with 300 images generated using three MoiréTag
foreground images and ten noisy background images. The model was trained using 15,000 iterations,
and no failure was observed in the test set of 100 different images. If the YOLACT model fails to
detect the MoireTag in one frame, this frame will be skipped. This will not affect displacement
detection if the tracking loss is minimal, as the Moiré fringe location in the next frame will reflect

the cumulative movement over time. YOLACT is not used to directly locate the fringes from the
input frame because precise separation of fringes across frames is required to calculate the fringes’
displacement accurately. To achieve this, we added additional steps to crop the areas within the
white paper border on the screen that contains the Moiré fringes, which are described in the next

After cropping the MoiréTag from the original image using YOLACT, the pipeline converts it into
greyscale and applies a threshold for contour detection using OpenCV. Given we intend to crop the
rectangle area inside the white border around the screen, all contours that have an incorrect number
of vertices or ratio of length and width are filtered. Each of the remaining contours is rotated based
on its ratio of length and width. Then the algorithm locates the dark marker on the top left corner of
the white border to adjust the orientation of the contour accordingly. This is achieved by comparing
the average RGB pixel values of the outer regions of all four corners and identifying the darkest
region. Next, the area inside each contour is cropped, resized to 600 ×600 pixels, and rectified.
Then the top 200 rows that only contain fringes are hashed by Python’s ImageHash library. If the
difference between the hash values of the cropped image and of a pre-stored image of Moiré fringes’
display area is below a preset threshold, the pipeline considers it as the correct match and proceeds
to the next step.
On the 600 ×600 pixels cropped image, 80 pixels are removed from each side to delete the
remaining areas of screen edges from the image and only keep the Moiré fringes. The bottom 200
rows of pixels are dedicated to ID detection, while the top 200 rows that only contain fringes, are
used for displacement detection. During the ID detection stage, the cropped image of the ID area is
divided into four equal sections vertically. If the average RGB pixel value of a section is below a set
threshold, it is classified as dark, otherwise, it is classified as white. The dark region is assigned a
value of 1, while the white region is assigned a value of 0. The results of these classifications are
then combined to create a four-digit binary number(e.g., 1011 in Figure 1a) which is then converted
to a decimal ID number.
The rows that only contain fringes are flattened to 1 pixel in the y-direction for faster calculation
speed since the fringes move in the x-direction. Because the fringes are repetitive, we can extract
some columns of pixels after calculation and concatenate them on the left and right sides to extend
the width of the image. A Gaussian blur is then applied to produce qualified waveforms of pixel
brightness for calculation. The extended width ensures that the columns of pixels in the original
cropped image are well blurred. Finally, we cut out these columns from the extended image and
use them for fringe detection.
The brightness of pixels on the blurred image will be distributed as inverted bell curves as shown
in Figure 3. The pixel locations of the valleys in the curves indicate the positions of the centers of
the Moiré fringes. To detect the valleys, the slope of the curve is analyzed for zero-crossing points.
To determine the displacement, we calculate the difference between the averages of the previous
and the current valley locations.
When using OpenCV contour detection to identify the edge points of the rectangle inside the
white border, slight variations may occur between frames, leading to jittering in the locations
of the detected valleys even when there is no actual displacement. To mitigate this, we average
the currently detected and previous four displacements and use it as the current displacement,
while also storing the raw data for future calculations. This helps to reduce the jittering to within
approximately negative to positive 5 pixels. Finally, we use Equation 2 to calculate the actual
displacement of the moving paper layers 𝐵from the detected displacement of the Moiré fringes.

3.4 Validation
To evaluate the performance of MoiréTag in sensing small displacements under different lighting
conditions, camera angles, and camera distances, we conducted three sets of validation experiments
using a linear translation stage with a step size of 0.01 mm to create small movements precisely.
The moving strip layer was pasted onto the translation stage’s moving surface and pushed by the
pillar, while the screen and fixed paper layer of MoiréTag were attached to a 3D-printed supporting
structure. To prevent accidental movement, the translation stage and supporting structure were
securely taped to the table. By twisting the handle of the translation stage, moving Moiré fringes
were created for an iPhone 13 Pro’s wide lens camera (equivalent focal length: 26 mm) to capture
from the top.
In this experiment, the grating period of the fixed layer of stripe patterns 𝑇𝐴 is 1.752 mm and
the grating period of the moving layer 𝑇𝐵 is 1.422 mm. Together they create Moiré fringes with a
grating period 𝑇𝑀 of 7.651 mm. This indicated that for every 1 mm movement of the moving layer,
there would be a 5.378 mm movement of Moiré fringes. We used Cricut (144 dpi: 0.176 mm/step) to
cut the fixed layer and a laser printer (1200 dpi: 0.021 mm/step) to print the moving layer. While
the printing resolution affects the precision of an individual strip, the Moiré fringe is created based
on the frequency of the multiple strips and the effect of printing resolution on the overall accuracy
becomes smaller, because the error from the printing resolution does not accumulate over the
distance.

3.4.4 Discussion. The results of the three validation experiments showed that the detected dis-
placement of the Moiré fringes exhibited a nearly linear relation with the actual displacement of
the moving paper layer. The average error remained no greater than 0.043 mm when measuring
bidirectional movements spanning 4 mm with a step size of 0.2 mm. The highest average relative
error was 0.09, recorded at a camera distance of 140 cm. This was caused by the average absolute
error of around 0.03 mm when the displacement was small (0.2 and 0.4 mm). The absolute errors did
not exhibit any consistent relationship with the displacement, although in some of the conditions,
the absolute errors increased with the displacement. This demonstrates the suitability of Moiré-
Tag for applications that may involve varying lighting conditions and camera positions. Notably,
measurement errors and misalignment between detected forward and backward movements were
primarily attributed to the jittering introduced during the image cropping process using OpenCV’s
contour detection method, as described in section 3.3. Overall, no noticeable pattern was observed
in how illumination level, camera angle, and camera distance affected the sensing process.

5 Discussion and Conclusion
We presented a novel camera-based approach to detect small displacement changes in daily in-
teractions by utilizing a low-cost passive tag that magnifies displacement using Moiré fringes.
The validation experiments show consistent and accurate sensing of displacement under vary-
ing lighting conditions, camera angles, and camera distances. Additionally, we demonstrate the
conversion of touch force and volume changes into displacement and the ability to sense them
using repurposed MoiréTag prototypes and our image processing pipeline in practical applications.
MoiréTag offers the following advantages over existing sensing methods:
Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact., Vol. 8, No. ISS, Article 525. Publication date: December 2024.
MoiréTag: A Low-Cost Tag for High-Precision Tangible Interactions without Active Components 525:17
- Affordable and easy-to-make tag design. The prototype can be made with paper alone,
using a printable template and machine-cut stripe patterns for the fixed layer. If the chosen
grating period for the fixed paper layer is sufficiently large, it can also be printed and manually
cut.
- Robust sensing in various lighting and different camera placements. The customized
image processing pipeline is able to detect displacement with sub-mm errors under different
lighting conditions and from varying camera angles and distances.
- Adjustable sensing range and sensitivity. The sensing range can be adjusted by changing
the grating periods of the two overlapping paper layers of stripe patterns to meet the different
sensing needs.
- Versatile sensing capabilities. MoiréTag can be repurposed as a sensor for touch force or
volume changes during respiration by adding an elastic block or a strap.
Like all camera-based sensing methods, MoiréTag requires uninterrupted visibility within the
camera’s field of view, without any obstructions. Our validation results indicate that MoiréTag can
function effectively with a camera angle of 60 degrees with a camera distance of 60 cm. A greater
camera angle might cause the camera to fail in capturing the Moiré fringes. Using a lens with a
wider field of view may enable detection with a larger camera angle. Moreover, an extended camera
distance may render MoiréTag too small for detection or cause the Moiré fringes to become too
blurry for tracking. However, these issues could potentially be mitigated by employing a MoiréTag
with a larger size or a camera with a higher resolution.
In addition to the jittering of cropped Moiré fringes resulting from slight variations in contours
detected by OpenCV across frames, another issue contributing to errors in displacement sensing
is related to drastic camera movement and angle changes. The gap between two layers of stripe
patterns may also affect the grating period of Moiré fringes, and may cause noticeable movement if
the camera moves drastically. In our design of prototypes, we tried to avoid creating such a gap
with the 3D-printed structures, and tapes for the paper prototype. If the camera angles change
greatly, for example from left perpendicular to the tag to right perpendicular to the tag, the structure
above the screen may affect the rectification of the fringes and thus lead to detected Moiré fringes
movement. Lastly, it is important to note that if the camera moves too quickly, motion blur can
potentially impact the detection and tracking of Moiré fringes, especially in low-light conditions. If
Moiré fringes move rapidly, the image processing pipeline’s ability to detect displacement changes
may be constrained by the frame rate. A future work will be needed to investigate the tracking
performance in more dynamic use scenarios and to optimize the pipeline to improve the frame rate.
MoiréTag was designed to measure movement in only one direction. One potential future
improvement that could be made to MoiréTag is the use of Moiré patterns with different shapes, such
as circular ones, to enable displacement measurement in multiple directions. Another improvement
could be creating Moiré fringes on multiple surfaces to allow for displacement detection from
more camera angles. Overall, MoiréTag demonstrates the potential of using Moiré fringes to
accurately detect small displacements and indirectly infer various physical properties during
everyday interactions.
