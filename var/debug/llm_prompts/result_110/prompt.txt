SPACE: Speaker Adaptation for Acoustic
Eavesdropping using mmWave Radio Signals
Running Zhao, Luca Jiang-Tao Yu, Tingle Li, Zhihan Jiang, Chenwei Zhang, Chenshu Wu, Senior Member, IEEE,
Hang Zhao, and Edith C.H. Ngai, Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—The prevalence of voice-related interaction and
communication has raised concerns about privacy leakage and
security. For example, millimeter-wave (mmWave) radio signals
have been exploited as a potential attacker for acoustic eavesdrop-
ping. However, speaker variability and low-quality input pose
significant challenges for the practical deployment of mmWave-
based eavesdropping. In this paper, we propose SPACE, an
acoustic eavesdropping system to recover intelligible speech from
low-quality mmWave signals, which can adapt to numerous
different speakers and unseen ones. SPACE is a two-stage system
that first reconstructs the spectrogram using a novel Radio
TransUNet and then synthesizes the waveform through a neural
vocoder. Specifically, to alleviate the negative effect of speaker
variability, we introduce a speaker encoder to capture speaker
features and a fusion network to condition the spectrogram
reconstruction based on the extracted speaker characteristics.
Further, to facilitate intelligible speech recovery from low-quality
input, we design a Frequency Transformation Layer to exploit
the correlation among all frequency harmonics and incorporate
the neural vocoder to synthesize the speech waveform from
the reconstructed spectrogram without using the contaminated
phase. The experimental results show that SPACE outperforms
existing mmWave-based approaches in scenarios with numerous
different speakers and unseen speakers.
Index Terms —Wireless sensing, acoustic eavesdropping,
mmWave radio signals, speech recovery, speaker adaptation.

and irrelevant sound constrain the feasibility. To overcome the

been exploited for various sensing applications [9]–[13]. These
distinctive attributes also empower the usage of RF signals
for acoustic eavesdropping, where the basic idea is to decode
the sound-related vibration from the reflected signal of the
target loudspeaker for voice recovery or recognition. Partic-
ularly, among many RF signals, millimeter-wave (mmWave)
technology appears to be more promising for measuring subtle
motions like voice vibration, thanks to its short wavelengths
and large bandwidths [14].

Speaker variability. In real deployment, the target victim is
not fixed, which expects the system to have the ability to
recover the speech of numerous different speakers (Figure
1). However, different speaker-level characteristics, such as
accent, age, and gender, result in distinct acoustic properties of
each speaker. Therefore, with an increased number of speakers,
speaker variability will significantly increase the difficulty of
speech recovery as the method has to adapt to the unique
features of each speaker [20], especially with low-quality
mmWave signals as the input (detailed below). This difficulty
limits the number of distinct speakers in existing mmWave-
based eavesdropping systems to typically a few dozen, less
than one hundred. Moreover, since the target victim varies
in different application scenarios, all eavesdropped speakers
cannot occur in the training set. In other words, speakers
in the test set are always unseen for the training set. The
inconsistency among speakers creates a mismatch between
the statistical distributions of training and test data [21].
Apparently, the mismatch between speakers poses challenges
for existing works as they mainly focus on speakers that are
the same in both training and test sets. Therefore, enabling the
mmWave-based eavesdropping system to adapt to numerous
different speakers or unseen speakers while maintaining high
recovery performance remains intractable.
Low-quality input mmWave signals. The low-quality
mmWave signals are attributed to the absence of a high-
frequency band and the fact that such signals are overwhelmed
by noise (Figure 1). On the one hand, commodity mmWave
radar design results in a limited upper frequency that the radar
can perceive (typically below 1 KHz), making the input signal
lack high-frequency bands [22]. On the other hand, the noise
from wireless channels and multipath signals contaminates
input mmWave signals, especially for the phase. Although
existing works achieve good quantitative results with low-
quality input mmWave signals, there is still a gap between
the intelligibility of recovered speech and original speech
in terms of human perception, especially for the utterances
acquired from numerous different and unseen speakers. The
application of mmWave-based eavesdropping expects the re-
covered speech as close to the original as possible so that it
is intelligible to listeners.
In this paper, we introduce SPACE, speaker adaptation for
acoustic eavesdropping using mmWave radio signals, which
is able to adapt to numerous different speakers (up to 630
speakers) and unseen speakers. SPACE can recover intelligible
speech from low-quality input mmWave signals. In addition,
SPACE is capable of recovering high-quality speech not only
for numerous different speakers seen during training but also
for speakers never seen before, alleviating the challenge of
speaker variability. SPACE is a system with a two-stage
recovery scheme consisting of spectrogram reconstruction and
waveform synthesis. In the first stage, we design the Radio
TransUNet for low-quality input mmWave signals to recon-
struct the accurate spectrogram guided by the extracted speaker
characteristics. Specifically, it includes a speaker encoder for
extracting speaker characteristics, a spectrogram encoder for
capturing spectrogram embedding, a fusion network for in-
corporating speaker embedding into spectrogram embedding,
and a spectrogram decoder for spectrogram reconstruction.
In the second stage, we introduce the neural vocoder to di-
rectly synthesize the speech waveform from the reconstructed
spectrogram. Notably, our design overcomes the above two
challenges in a distinct way:
To address the first challenge, we introduce a speaker
encoder to capture speaker-aware features as auxiliary infor-
mation for spectrogram reconstruction, alleviating the negative
effect of speaker variability, i.e., the increasing number of
different speakers and speaker mismatch between training and
test sets. Beyond this, we design a fusion network composed
of Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) and Transformer
layers, where the former conditions the spectrogram embed-
ding by the speaker embedding, and the latter captures the
contextual information of the conditioned features and further
fuses them to enhance spectrogram reconstruction. Under the
guidance of the speaker embedding, the extracted spectrogram
embedding can integrate speaker characteristics for reconstruc-
tion, so that the network can adapt to numerous different
speakers and unseen speakers. For the second challenge of
the low-quality input mmWave signals, we specially design a
Frequency Transformation Layer (FTL), inserted behind each
spectrogram encoder layer, to exploit the correlation among all
frequency harmonics. This enables the network to make full
use of the limited frequency information to predict the accurate
and affluent high-frequency bands. Moreover, considering the
high quality of the reconstructed spectrogram and the noise-
contaminated phase, the neural vocoder directly synthesizes
the speech waveform from the reconstructed spectrogram,
avoiding noise interference.
Overall, our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose SPACE, a mmWave-based eavesdropping
system that recovers intelligible speech from low-quality
input and maintains the capacity to adapt to numerous
different speakers and unseen speakers.
• We introduce the speaker encoder to capture the speaker-
aware features, and we further propose the fusion net-
work with FiLM and Transformer layers to condition the
extracted spectrogram embedding by the speaker embed-
ding with the speaker characteristics, thereby adapting
to numerous different speakers (up to 630 speakers) and
unseen speakers while maintaining high quality.
• We introduce the FTL to exploit the correlation among
all frequency harmonics, facilitating the missing high-
frequency band prediction. Moreover, we introduce the
neural vocoder to synthesize speech waveform from the
reconstructed spectrogram with the accurate and affluent
high-frequency band. Both introductions guarantee intel-
ligible speech recovery from low-quality input.
• We implement SPACE on the commercial mmWave radar
and perform extensive evaluations on different scenarios
and settings. The experimental results show that SPACE
outperforms other mmWave-based approaches on the
datasets with numerous different speakers and unseen
speakers in terms of quantitative and qualitative evalu-
ations.

II. P RELIMINARY KNOWLEDGE
A. mmWave Sensing for Vibration Estimation
The core of mmWave-based eavesdropping is to demodulate
the vibration-related information from frequency-modulated
continuous-wave (FMCW) radar signals. Specifically, the
FMCW radar transmits “chirp” signals and captures the re-
flection from objects. Given the carrier frequency fc, chirp
slope K, The transmitted chirp is defined as STX (t) =
exp[j(2πfct + πKt2)]. An object placed at a distance d0
vibrating as x(t) would cause the received signal to be a
time-delayed version of transmitted chirp: SRX(t) = A ·
exp[j(2πfc(t − τ) +πK(t − τ)2)], where A is the path loss
coefficient, τ is the round-trip delay given by 2(d0+x(t))
c , and c
is the light speed. A mixer is used to demodulate the received
signal, generating the Intermediate Frequency (IF) signal as:
SIF (t) =A · exp(j2πKτt + j2πfcτ). (1)
Although the frequency of the IF signal contains vibration-
related information, the frequency change caused by the weak
vibration is not discernible in the spectrum. Fortunately, the
phase is more sensitive to vibration with weak amplitude, and
thus, the phase of the IF signal can be used to demodulate
the vibration signal [23]. First, we perform range-FFT over
the received IF signal to locate the target. Then, we separate
the target reflection from irrelevant inferences by taking the
corresponding peak in magnitude (range bin) and combining
samples across chirps. The sample sequence can be expressed
as S(n) = A · exp(j2πfcτ), where τ = 2(d0+x(n))
c . After
removing static clutters, we extract the phase variation ϕ(n)
across multiple chirps and unwrap it to resolve the phase am-
biguity. With the above process, the speech-related vibration
signal x(n) is demodulated from mmWave FMCW signals.

A. System Overview
The overall structure of SPACE is shown in Fig. 4. The
captured mmWave signals are converted into radio Mel spec-
trogram, which is used as the input of the proposed system. In
the Radio TransUNet for spectrogram reconstruction, speaker
encoder and spectrogram encoder first encode the input radio
Mel spectrogram into speaker embeddings and spectrogram
embeddings, respectively, and then, they are fused by the
fusion network with FiLM and Transformer layers. Subse-
quently, spectrogram decoder reconstructs the speech Mel
spectrogram from the fused embedding. The Radio TransUNet
not only performs the modality transformation from radio
to audio, but also injects the speaker characteristics into the
spectrogram and enhances the quality of the spectrogram by
predicting accurate and affluent high-frequency bands. For the
waveform synthesis, the neural vocoder synthesizes the high-
quality speech waveform from the reconstructed speech Mel
spectrogram. In summary, SPACE is a system with a two-stage
recovery strategy, and it consists of five components, including

B. Speaker Encoder
The speaker encoder is utilized to extract the speaker-
discriminative embeddings from the Mel spectrogram of
mmWave signals, which can represent the characteristics of
different speakers. With the extracted speaker embedding,
the speaker-related information can condition the following
spectrogram reconstruction, allowing the network to adapt to
numerous different speakers and unseen speakers.
We follow a speaker verification model with the generalized
end-to-end loss [24] and use it as the speaker encoder. The
speaker verification is the task to verify whether the utterance
belongs to a specific speaker. Therefore, the model is able
to effectively capture the decisive feature of speakers that
can be differentiated from other speakers. Considering the
real application scenarios of SPACE, each target may say
totally different sentences. This motivates us to train the
speaker encoder in a text-independent setting. Specifically, the
speaker encoder is a simple but effective network consisting
of 3 LSTM layers and a projection layer. It takes the Mel
spectrogram of mmWave signals with arbitrary length as the
input and maps the spectrogram into a fixed-dimensional
embedding vector. In the training process, the network is
optimized by the generalized end-to-end verification loss to
discriminate the feature embeddings of different speakers in a

C. Spectrogram Encoder
The spectrogram encoder extracts the spectrogram embed-
dings from the Mel spectrogram of mmWave signals with
limited frequency bands, exploiting speech-related information
among all frequency harmonics for the following speech
spectrogram reconstruction.
We design the spectrogram encoder by combining the con-
volution layer and the frequency transformation layer (FTL).
To achieve the goal of modality transformation and spec-
trogram enhancement, the spectrogram encoder and decoder
(see Section III-E) take the form of a UNet [26] due to
its effectiveness in prediction tasks with the help of skip
connection. Beyond this, considering the low quality of the
input spectrogram, it is important to make full use of the
limited frequency for high-frequency prediction. In particular,
harmonic correlation along the whole frequency axis has
been proven to be helpful for time-frequency representation
reconstruction [27]. Therefore, we further introduce the FTL,
inserted behind each convolution layer, to capture harmonics
from the mmWave spectrogram with limited frequency bands.
As shown in Fig. 6, FTL is composed of three stacked
convolution layers with a kernel size of 5 × 5, a frequency
fully connected layer, and a 1 × 1 convolution layer. The
three stacked convolution layers are used to capture local time-

the key component to capture global harmonic correlations and
it acts as a trainable transformation matrix, which applies to

D. Fusion Network

and spectrogram embeddings by inserting the condition signals
from speaker embeddings into spectrogram embeddings and
further exploiting the contextual information.
In our design, we employ the FiLM layer [28] and Trans-

embeddings. For this reason, we adopt the FiLM that applies
an affine transformation to the spectrogram embeddings based
on the speaker embeddings, thereby learning to adaptively
influence the spectrogram embeddings. As shown in Fig. 7(a),
assuming the speaker embeddings Espk and the spectrogram

E. Spectrogram Decoder
The spectrogram decoder aims to reconstruct the speech Mel
spectrogram from the fused embeddings that are the output of
the fusion network.
As mentioned in Section III-C, the spectrogram decoder is
paired with the spectrogram encoder to construct the UNet-
style network with the skip connection. The spectrogram
decoder consists of multiple upsampling blocks to decode
the fused embeddings for outputting the final speech Mel
spectrogram. In contrast to convolution for downsampling in
the spectrogram encoder, the spectrogram decoder utilizes
the PixelShuffle to upsample the fused embeddings, with
the same multiple for upsampling and downsampling. The
skip-connections between the encoder and decoder allow the
feature flow, facilitating the output reconstruction and training

F . Neural Vocoder
After reconstructing the high-quality Mel spectrogram using
the designed Radio TransUNet in the first stage, we introduce
the neural vocoder, Parallel WaveGAN [32], to synthesize nat-
ural speech waveform from the reconstructed Mel spectrogram
in the second stage.
Based on the fact that Radio TransUNet reconstructs the Mel
spectrogram with accurate and affluent speech features, we
directly use the neural vocoder to synthesize speech waveform
from the Mel spectrogram without using the contaminated
phase. Although the intuition is to directly employ inverse
Short Time Fourier Transform (iSTFT) on the phase of the
input mmWave spectrogram and the estimated amplitude spec-
trogram to recover speech, the noise from the wireless channel
and the multipath signal contaminates the phase, which signif-
icantly affects the perceptual quality of the generated speech
waveform [33]. Among the many neural vocoders, we adopt
Parallel WaveGAN to transform the recovered Mel spectro-
gram into a speech waveform due to its fast and small-footprint
characteristics. Parallel WaveGAN is based on the generated
network of WaveNet, and it is composed of a generator and
discriminator. To improve the stability and efficiency of the
adversarial training process, the multi-resolution STFT loss is
adopted as the auxiliary loss. These designs allow the model
to generate natural-sounding waveforms with a small number
of model parameters.

B. Dataset Preparation
We use the TIMIT [36] as the speech source for loudspeaker
playback. TIMIT consists of 6300 clips uttered by 630 speak-
ers (each speaker with 10 utterances) with different accents
lasting 3.5 hours. We use two strategies to separate the training
and test sets for TIMIT to evaluate the performance of models.
• Various seen speaker scenario: Speakers in training
and test sets are from numerous different speakers, and
speakers in the test set are seen in the training set. We
randomly select one utterance from each speaker of the
TIMIT, a total of 630 samples from 630 speakers, for
evaluation, and the others (i.e., 5670 samples from 630
speakers) are used for training.
• Unseen speaker scenario: Speakers also from numerous
different speakers but speakers in the test set are unseen
for the training set. We use all samples from randomly
selected 567 speakers (i.e., 5670 samples) as the training
set, and all samples from the remaining 63 speakers (i.e.,
630 samples) as the test set.
According to the paired audio signals, the collected
mmWave signals are separated using the same strategies to
obtain training and test sets in two scenarios, including the
various seen speaker scenario and the unseen speaker scenario.
Therefore, the paired mmWave-speech signals construct two
datasets for training and testing.
mmWave signals are used as the input for the network,
and they are upsampled from 5.1 KHz to 8 KHz. The cor-
responding speech signals are downsampled to 8 KHz before
feeding into the network as the ground truth. To facilitate
training, mmWave and speech signals are transformed into 80-
dimensional log-Mel-Spectrogram features in the band-limited
frequency range (60 to 4000 Hz), with a window size of 512
and a hop size of 128.
C. Network Configuration
The implementation of SPACE is based on the open-sourced
code1. The spectrogram encoder includes one input layer and
three encoder layers, each encoder layer followed by an FTL.
Each encoder layer contains two stacks, and each of them
includes the convolution layer with a kernel size of 3 × 3, a
batch normalization, and a ReLU activation function, while
the stride of the former and latter convolution layer is 2 × 2
and 1×1 and the channels expansion factors of them are 2 and
1. The input layer has a similar structure but only increases
the channel size from 1 to 128 without changing the height
and width. As discussed in Section III-C, FTL consists of
three stacked convolution layers with a kernel size of 5 × 5,
a frequency fully connected layer with the dimension equal
to the frequency dimension of input feature, and a 1 × 1
convolution layer. The speaker encoder is a 3-layer LSTM
with a hidden size of 256, followed by a projection to 256
dimensions. As for the fusion network, the linear layer in
FiLM projects the speaker embeddings into two embeddings
with the size equal to the channels of spectrogram embeddings
as 1024, representing functions of γ(·) and β(·). Moreover, the
Transformer has 12 layers, each layer with a head number of 8
and a hidden size of 768. To construct UNet, correspondingly,
the spectrogram decoder includes three decoder layers and
one output layer. Each decoder layer contains two stacks with
a similar structure to the encoder, but the PixelShuffle for
upsampling twice operator is inserted after the first stack.
Moreover, the output layer is a convolution layer to converts
the channels into the input as one dimension.
The Radio TransUNet is trained using the AdamW op-
timizer with a learning rate of 0.005 for 130 epochs. The
Transformer layers in the fusion network are pretrained on
ImageNet, and the speaker encoder is pretrained as in [25].
Moreover, the training of Parallel WaveGAN follows the
setting in [32], and it uses the corresponding corpus with
8KHz sampling rate as input. We use PyTorch to implement
all networks and train them with NVIDIA RTX 3090.

VI. R ELATED WORK
A. Acoustic Eavesdropping
The principle of acoustic eavesdropping is to recover the
audio from the related vibration. To achieve this goal, exist-
ing works utilize various sensors to detect these vibrations,
which can generally be divided into the following three major
categories:
Vision-based audio sensing. Cameras, Lidar, and laser have
been employed for audio sensing. Davis et al. [7] reconstructed
audio by capturing the vibration of tiny objects caused by the
waves of sound. In [5], the researchers proposed to recover
audio by analyzing the signal collected by Lidar sensor.
Zhang et al. exploit ambient-light reflections and a cGAN to
passively recover nearby speech [40]. However, these vision-
based methods can be significantly influenced by occlusions
and varying illumination conditions.
Motion-based audio sensing. Numerous works have uti-
lized motion-based audio sensing, such as Inertial Mea-
surement Units (IMU), vibration motors, ultrasound-based,
and magnetic hard drives, for audio sensing. For example,
Michalevsky et al. [41] and Hu et al. [42] employed gyro-
scopes and accelerometers, respectively, to capture and recover
speech content. Furthermore, in [43], researchers enhanced au-
dio sensing accuracy by analyzing data from both gyroscopes
and accelerometers. Moreover, Maruri et al. [44] integrated
vibration sensors into the nasal pads of glasses for audio
sensing. Besides, ultrasound-based solutions like USpeech
[45] and UltraSE [46] captured the motion information of
vocal tract for speech enhancement. Roy et al. [47] and Kwong
[48] achieved audio reconstruction using magnetic hard drives

and vibrating motors. However, the performance of these
methods is highly dependent on the sampling frequency.
RF-based audio sensing. Compared with vision and motion
sensor related solutions, RF is immune to lighting condi-
tions and occlusions and is more sensitive to vibrations.
The RF-based methods can be classified according to the
RF technologies used, including WiFi, RFID, and mmWave.
Wei et al. [2] implemented an audio sensing system with
a WiFi antenna array for RF-based speech recovery. Wang
et al. [49] implemented WiHear to detect and analyze radio
reflections from mouth movements based on WiFi signals.
As for RFID-based methods, researchers have explored the
possibility of using COTS RFID tags to perform the thru-the-
wall eavesdropping on the loudspeaker [50]. Chen et al. [51]
attached a low-cost RFID tag on the glass bridge to sense
subtle facial speech dynamics and thereby achieve live voice
eavesdropping.
Besides, there are numerous works utilizing mmWave for
audio reconstruction. WaveEar [15] and MilliEar [18] recov-
ered the speech by detecting throat vibration and loudspeaker
vibration using mmWave signals, respectively. Note that Mil-
liEar demonstrates reliable generalization to different and
unseen speakers. However, the number of different speakers
it can adapt to is limited, and an increase in the number
of different speakers poses a challenge to its adaptability.
mmEavesdropper was proposed for directional eavesdropping
with mmWave radar [17]. RadioMic reconstructed sound based
on tiny vibrations on active and passive objects [16]. Similarly,
Wang et al. [19] decoded speech from piezoelectric film based
on a mmWave-characterized piezoelectric effect. Li et al. [52]
developed an mmWave microphone for perceiving the vibra-
tion response of multiple light objects. Phone calls can also be
eavesdropped by detecting minute vibrations from the earpiece
[22], [53]. Hu et al. [54] introduced mmWave-based acoustic
sensing systems to measure the micrometer-level vibration of
an object induced by sound waves without relying on machine
learning or prior knowledge. Li et al. leverage intra-chirp
phase across multiple antennas to reconstruct vocabulary-free
speech without prior training [55]. Wang et al. demonstrate an
RF retroreflector attack that wirelessly captures speech from
audio cables [56].
In addition to audio recovery, some researchers exploited
mmWave-based speech recognition for eavesdropping. mm-
MIC [57] extracted features of lip motion and vocal-cords
vibration for mmWave-based speech recognition. Zhao et al.
proposed Radio2Speech to recover high-quality speech from
mmWave signals in various scenarios [58] and further pro-
posed Radio2Text for streaming automatic speech recognition
[59]. In [60], a mmWave-based eavesdropping system that
performs speech recognition in non-line-of-sight scenarios was
proposed. Moreover, mmWave sensing was also leveraged for
joint audio-radio speech enhancement and separation [61].
However, these mmWave-based methods neglect the chal-
lenges of speaker variability and low-quality input mmWave
signals in practical applications. The speaker variability in-
creases the difficulty of existing works to adapt to numerous
different speakers and unseen speakers. Thus, the number
of distinct speakers in existing works is typically limited
to a few dozen, less than one hundred, and they mainly
focus on speakers that are the same in training and test
sets. Moreover, constrained by the low-quality input mmWave
signals, there remains a gap in intelligibility between the
speech recovered by existing works and the original speech
from human auditory, especially for utterances acquired from
numerous different and unseen speakers. In contrast, with the
specially designed components, the proposed SPACE is able
to recover intelligible speech from low-quality input mmWave
signals and adapt to numerous different speakers (up to 630
speakers) and unseen speakers.
B. Speaker Adaptation
Speaker adaptation seeks to minimize the discrepancies
between training and test speakers, thereby improving perfor-
mance by customizing the system to each speaker’s unique
characteristics [62]. Generally, speaker adaptation approaches
can be categorized into feature-based methods and model-
based methods [63].
Feature-based methods include transforming feature space
to produce speaker-independent acoustic features and intro-
ducing auxiliary speaker knowledge [62]. Ochiai et al. [64]
improved speech recognition by combining noisy speech fea-
tures with a speech-enhanced pathway. Compact speaker em-
beddings, such as i-vectors and x-vectors, encode speaker char-
acteristics and enhance recognition performance when com-
bined with acoustic features [65], [66]. Speaker embeddings
derived from a sequence summary network (s-vectors) [67]
and memory networks (m-vectors) [68] were also employed
to capture speaker-specific information. Recent advancements
have explored incorporating x-vectors and ECAPA-TDNN
embeddings to improve recognition performance [62].
Model-based methods modify the acoustic model to align
with the testing conditions by learning speaker or environment-
dependent transformations [62]. Various transformation tech-
niques, such as linear input network [69], learning hidden
network [70], learning hidden unit contributions [71], parame-
terized activation functions [72], factorized linear transforma-
tion [73], and speaker code-based bias vector [74]. In [75],
an interpolation vector is estimated to combine the weight
basis into a single adapted weight matrix for a new acoustic
condition during the adaptation. Liao et al. [76] retrained
the input layer, output layer, or the entire network to adapt
the DNN to speakers. Meng et al. [77] proposed a novel
adversarial speaker adaptation scheme that uses adversarial
learning to align the distribution of deep hidden features in a
speaker-dependent DNN acoustic model with those of a fixed
speaker-independent DNN model during adaptation.
In this work, we transfer the success of speaker adaptation
in various speech-related tasks into mmWave-based speech
recovery. We introduce a speaker encoder to capture the
speaker-aware features and a fusion network to condition the
extracted spectrogram embedding by the speaker embedding
with the speaker characteristics, which facilitates adaptation
to numerous different speakers and unseen speakers while
maintaining high quality.

VII. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose an acoustic eavesdropping system,
named SPACE, which leverages low-quality mmWave radio
signals to recover intelligible speech for numerous different
speakers and unseen speakers. SPACE first reconstructs the
spectrogram using the designed Radio TransUNet , which
consists of spectrogram encoder, speaker encoder, fusion
network and spectrogram decoder, and then synthesizes the
speech waveform through the neural vocoder, coping with
the challenges of speaker variability and low-quality input.
The experimental results show that SPACE works well in the
quiet scenario, and even in noisy and soundproof scenarios.
Moreover, compared with other mmWave-based baselines,
SPACE archives the state-of-the-art performance on the dataset
with numerous different speakers and unseen speakers in terms
of quantitative and qualitative evaluations. In future work,
we will consider the scenarios of multi-speaker and moving
speaker to make our system adaptable to various application
scenarios.
