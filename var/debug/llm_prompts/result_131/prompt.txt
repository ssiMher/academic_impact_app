ABSTRACT
Air-gapping is widely regarded as a robust defense for safe-
guarding sensitive systems. Yet, recent advances in covert
channel research reveal that even physically isolated ma-
chines remain susceptible to sophisticated data exfiltration.
We present FAN-RFID, a novel covert channel that exploits
existing or implanted passive RFID tags as controllable RF
signal sources. By modulating an RFID tag through precise
control of a cooling fan’s rotation speed, we encode sensi-
tive information into variations of RFID backscatter induced
by the fan blades. These modulated signals can be reliably
captured at distances exceeding 3.5 m using a COTS RFID
reader, even through non-metallic barriers. We detail the at-
tack model, encoding scheme, and frame structure, and report
experimental results demonstrating that FAN-RFID surpasses
prior vibration-based and acoustic-based covert channels in
both transmission range and obstacle penetration. Finally, we
outline practical countermeasures to mitigate such threats in
air-apped environments.
Index Terms— Air-gap, Covert channels, Vibrations,
RFID, Network security
1. INTRODUCTION
Protecting sensitive information (e.g., state secrets, intellec-
tual property, and personal data) is a cornerstone of cyberse-
curity. A widely adopted solution to prevent data breaches
is the use of air-gapped systems, which are physically iso-
lated from external networks such as the Internet. However,
these systems can still be breached by advanced persistent
threats[1] (APTs) that leverage physical media or insider as-
sistance instead of a network connection. A well-known ex-
ample is the 2010 Stuxnet[1] incident, in which malware on
infected USB drives infiltrated Iran’s nuclear facilities. This

event demonstrated that even highly secure, isolated systems
can be compromised when attackers gain physical access or
exploit insiders. Once malware resides on an air-gapped sys-
tem, it can commandeer existing hardware components[2, 3,
4, 5] to establish covert channels[6] for data exfiltration.
Among these components, the cooling fan[7, 8] is a par-
ticularly attractive target for attackers. Fan speed can be
controlled by either varying CPU workload to adjust thermal
conditions or by applying pulse-width modulation (PWM)
signals[7, 8]. Previous research has explored two fan-based
exfiltration approaches: an acoustic method[7] that encodes
data in fan-generated sound at audible frequencies and a
vibration-based method[8] that transmits information via
chassis resonance. However, these techniques suffer from
significant limitations: they offer only a limited transmis-
sion range, are highly susceptible to environmental interfer-
ence, and require direct physical contact. Consequently, their
practical effectiveness in real-world deployments is severely
constrained.
Radio-frequency identification[9] (RFID) technology is
increasingly prevalent for asset management in secure facil-
ities. It typically consists of a reader, an antenna, and tags.
The reader emits electromagnetic fields, and the tags respond
by backscattering signals that carry identifying information.
It enables automated inventory tracking and access control
without physical contact. Recent studies demonstrate that
RFID systems can also detect minute environmental vibra-
tions, including acoustic waves[10, 11, 12] and mechanical
oscillations[13, 14, 15], by monitoring subtle changes in
backscattered signals. This capability suggests that otherwise
trusted RFID infrastructure could be repurposed to detect
maliciously induced vibrations, such as those generated by a
manipulated cooling fan.
In this paper, we present FAN-RFID, a covert commu-
nication method that combines fan manipulation with RFID
sensing to exfiltrate data from air-gapped systems. The core

2. METHODOLOGY
2.1. Attack Model
The adversarial attack model considers an attacker with APTs
capabilities whose objective is to exfiltrate data from an air-
gapped system by exploiting its cooling fan. We assume that
the attacker has already implanted malware on the target com-
puter through social engineering, supply-chain compromise,
or insider assistance. The malware can control the cooling fan
either by varying CPU workload to adjust thermal conditions
or by using PWM signals. The attacker may place an RFID
tag near the target system’s cooling fan through an insider or
by leveraging existing RFID tags. The malware collects and
encodes sensitive information, such as encryption keys or cre-
dentials, into a sequence of fan-speed changes. These varia-
tions, caused by fan rotation, modulate the backscatter signal
of the RFID tag and embed the data into the RF signal. The
attacker does not need to enter the secured room. Instead, a
commercial RFID reader or a software-defined radio (for ex-
ample, USRP) can be placed in an adjacent room or corridor
to receive, demodulate, and decode the covert signal.
2.2. Frames
The data is structured in discrete frames, each comprising
three fundamental components (as shown in Figure. 1): a
preamble, a payload, and a Parity bit.

• Preamble: Each frame begins with a predefined
preamble composed of four alternating bits (‘1010’).
This sequence assists the receiver in identifying the
start of a transmission and supports symbol synchro-
nization.
• Payload: The payload carries the actual data intended
for transmission. In our design, each payload contains
8 bits, representing one ASCII character of sensitive
information.
• Parity bit: A single odd parity bit is appended at the
end of each frame to facilitate basic error detection.
Upon receiving a frame, the receiver computes the par-
ity of the payload and compares it with the received
parity bit. A mismatch indicates a transmission error.
2.3. Control and Encoding Mechanisms
A Raspberry Pi 3B+ is used to regulate the fan speed in the
FAN-RFID setup. Its GPIO pins generate programmable
PWM signals, providing direct fan speed control independent
of the operating system’s thermal management.
As shown in Figure 2, the fan’s PWM control wire con-
nects to GPIO 12 (BCM18) to set the target speed, while the
tachometer (TACH) wire connects to GPIO 16 (BCM23) to
monitor current speed. The fan is powered by an external
12V DC supply, with the Raspberry Pi, fan, and power source
sharing a common ground for signal stability.
Malware modulates the PWM duty cycle to switch the
fan speed between two target values: RPM0 and RPM1.
Sensitive data such as keys or passwords is segmented into
bytes and encoded into frames containing a preamble, pay-
load, and parity bit. Binary Frequency-Shift Keying (BFSK)
maps logical ’0’ to RPM0 and ’1’ to RPM1. Both speeds
lie within the fan’s normal operating range, with a sufficient
difference ∆RPM = |RPM1 - RPM0| to ensure receiver dis-
crimination while avoiding noticeable speed changes. Details
are provided in Algorithm 1.

2.4. Signal Reception and Decoding
The attacker uses a commercial RFID reader or a software-
defined radio (e.g., USRP) placed outside the air-gapped area
as a receiver. Decoding follows two primary stages (see Al-
gorithm 2):
• Signal Preprocessing: The RF signal is captured and
demodulated to obtain baseband phase data. Differ-
ential processing removes the DC component and en-
hances signal variations. The signal is resampled at
1000 Hz via linear interpolation to form a uniform
time series. Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) is
then applied to generate a time-frequency representa-
tion. Peaks in the averaged spectrum are identified to
determine two dominant frequencies, f0 and f1, corre-
sponding to logical ’0’ and ’1’ based on fan speeds.
• Decoding: For each time segment, the signal energy
at f0 and f1 is compared. The frequency with higher
energy is decoded as the logical ’0’ or ’1’, producing
a binary stream. The stream is searched for the pream-
ble ’1010’ to synchronize frames. Subsequent bits are
parsed according to a frame structure of 8 data bits and
an odd parity bit. Only frames passing the parity check
are accepted, and the payload is reconstructed into the
original data.

3.1. Setup and Metrics
A passive H47 RFID tag was affixed 1 cm in front of the cool-
ing fan, with its speed regulated by a Raspberry Pi 3B+. A
COTS RFID reader (Impinj Speedway R420) served as the
receiver. All experiments were repeated 10 times to ensure
statistical significance. The transmitted word was “confiden-
tial”. The bit error rate (BER), defined as the ratio of incor-
rectly received bits to the total number of transmitted bits,
was employed as the performance metric. Lower BER values
indicate higher communication reliability.
3.2. Transmission Range
Transmission distance tests were conducted in a semi-anechoic
chamber. The data transmission rate was fixed at 0.2 bps, with
the distance increased by 0.5 m each time. As shown in Table.
1, FAN-RFID achieved reliable communication with a BER
of 0 at distances of up to 3.5 meters. When the range was
increased to 4 meters, the BER began to rise due to signal
path loss, showing an increasing trend with greater range.
Table. 2 provides a comparison between FAN-RFID and
two typical fan-based covert communication techniques. The
results demonstrate that FAN-RFID surpasses both vibration-
based (1.6 m) and acoustic-based (3 m) methods in transmis-
sion distance, non-line-of-sight, and stealthy data exfiltration.

3.3. Penetration
To evaluate the performance of FAN-RFID in non-line-of-
sight (NLOS) scenarios, we selected two different materials
(wood, 1.5 cm thick, and glass, 3.5 cm thick) and placed them
within the transmission path between the transmitter and the
receiver. The transmitter was positioned 10 cm from the ob-
stacle, and the receiver was placed 50 cm from the transmit-
ter, simulating a scenario where the attacker is in the corridor.
Experimental results demonstrate that we can successfully re-
cover “confidential” with 0 BER under these conditions.
3.4. Stealthiness
The stealthiness of FAN-RFID was evaluated from two as-
pects:
• RF Stealthiness:The carrier signal may originate from
a legitimate RFID tag already present in the air-gapped
zone. As a result, the attack activity is indistinguishable
from normal inventory operations, making it difficult to
detect using conventional monitoring systems.
• Acoustic Stealthiness:We used a BK sound level me-
ter in a semi-anechoic room to record the sound pres-
sure level (SPL) and calculated SNR at different speeds
(Figure. 4). The results show that lower rotational
speeds exhibit stronger acoustic stealth, making the at-
tack easier to implement. When the sound pressure
level difference between the two chosen speeds is be-
low 3 dB(A)[16], which is under the human perception
threshold, no abnormal whistling or noticeable acoustic
change occurs.

4. COUNTERMEASURES
Although the FAN-RFID attack is highly stealthy, a multi-
layered defense framework can effectively detect and mitigate
such threats.
Physical Layer:Embed metal shielding in secure zones
to block RF leakage and reception; Locate RFID tags at a
safe distance from fans and CPUs to prevent malicious mod-
ulation.
Over-the-Air Layer: Continuously scan assigned fre-
quency bands with an RF monitoring system; Flag unusual
polling rates or reader activity outside normal hours.
System Layer:Introduce driver- or firmware-level checks
for periodic fan-speed changes that do not match thermal re-
quirements.
5. CONCLUSION
This study presents FAN-RFID, a covert channel that exfil-
trates data from air-gapped systems by modulating cooling
fan speeds to alter passive RFID tag backscatter. Experi-
ments confirm its feasibility under realistic conditions, and
multi-layered countermeasures are proposed to mitigate such
threats.
