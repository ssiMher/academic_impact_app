RFID-based vibration sensing has been widely used in areas like 
machine fault diagnosis [8–16], vital signs monitoring [17,18], and 
speech eavesdropping [19–23]. Compared to traditional sensors [3], RF

sight detection, contactless operation, and multi-target sensing-due to 
their free-space propagation properties.

Endowing IoT devices with self-security monitoring capabilities without relying on external hardware marks 
a signiﬁcant advancement in the ﬁeld. RFID-equipped smart cabinets, while providing robust protection for 
sensitive items such as documents and electronic devices, remain vulnerable to violent break-ins or physical dis-
turbances such as slapping and shaking, which produce characteristic vibration patterns. We demonstrated that 
the cabinet’s integral RFID system can inherently detect such vibrations, thus enhancing its self-security. How-
ever, overcoming environmental dependency remains a critical challenge: variations in the shape, size, material, 
and spatial arrangement of items inside the cabinet interfere with RFID signal propagation, resulting in complex 
multipath eﬀects that compromise vibration-sensing accuracy and weaken security detection. To address this 
limitation and enable self-security monitoring, we proposed RF-AbVib, a novel solution that utilizes commercial 
oﬀ-the-shelf RFID readers in conjunction with a ﬁxed reference tag mounted on the inner wall of the cabinet to 
achieve environment-independent vibration monitoring. We pre-trained and ﬁne-tuned a meta-learning model 
to enable RF-AbVib to process variable-length data and adapt to diverse environmental conditions. Furthermore, 
we proposed a bilateral threshold ﬁltering (BTF) algorithm combined with discrete wavelet transform (DWT) to 
remove outliers and hardware noise while preserving subtle vibration features in RFID signals. Evaluated across 
31 distinct environments, RF-AbVib achieved 95.59 % accuracy in detecting three abnormal behaviors with only 
one sample, regardless of the reference tag’s position, orientation, or type. Relevant data has been uploaded to 
the RF-AbVib dataset.
1.  Introduction
Driven by rapid advancements in ﬁelds such as the internet of things, 
embedded systems, and artiﬁcial intelligence, RFID-equipped smart cab-
inets [1,2] have emerged as an innovative intelligent storage solution. 
These cabinets are progressively transforming traditional notions of stor-
age by integrating advanced technologies, such as RFID and infrared 
sensing. With a fully integrated RFID system, smart cabinets facilitate 
eﬃcient, standardized management and in-situ monitoring of sensitive 
items-including documents, tools, hazardous chemicals, medical sup-
plies, and precious metals-through the use of RFID tags.
The security of smart cabinets is crucial for safeguarding stored 
items. Consider a scenario in which an unauthorized individual attempts 
to damage or forcibly open the cabinet to steal contents, or shakes 
it violently, causing internal damage. Current RFID-based smart stor-

3.  Detailed design of RF-AbVib
3.1.  Overview
The key components and system workﬂow are illustrated in Fig. 4.
• Data Collection: The data collection module records signal parame-
ters, including phase, RSSI, and timestamps. We deﬁne three types 
of abnormal behaviors and deploy 31 distinct experimental environ-
ments. In each environment, a reader is used to collect vibration 
signals corresponding to the three abnormal behaviors as well as 
baseline signals, supporting subsequent processing and model train-
ing.
• Signal Preprocessing: We extract the phase and RSSI from raw data and 
process them separately. For phase data, we ﬁrst apply unwrapping 
due to its periodic nature. To reduce outliers, we propose a bilateral 
threshold ﬁltering (BTF) algorithm combined with discrete wavelet 
transform (DWT) for eﬀective smoothing. Next, to suppress static 
environmental interference and mitigate global trend variations, we 
compute the phase diﬀerence. For RSSI, we perform normalization. 
Finally, the processed data are segmented to meet the input require-
ments of the model.
• Model training and classiﬁcation: To ensure consistent input length, we 
adjust and ﬁx the data sequence length based on a predeﬁned time 
window. The processed data are then partitioned into training and 
testing sets, with each set containing unique support and query sets 
to meet the requirements of few-shot learning-based model training 
and evaluation. Finally, the model is tested under various experi-
mental conﬁgurations to comprehensively assess its performance.
3.2.  Bilateral threshold ﬁltering algorithm
In an RFID system, two main noise sources exist: hardware noise 
and environmental interference from items inside cabinets. Hardware 
noise causes phase ﬂuctuations of about 0.1 radians [15,16]. Objects 
in the cabinet can block and reﬂect signals, creating multipath eﬀects 
that lead to signal outliers. These outliers greatly impact ﬁltering and 
recognition accuracy. Traditional methods like amplitude-limiting ﬁl-
tering [43], median ﬁltering [44], and mean ﬁltering [45] are eﬀective 
for general smoothing but not suitable for our application. Amplitude-
limiting ﬁltering struggles to determine an appropriate threshold be-
cause it is sensitive to changes in cabinet layout. Median and mean 
ﬁltering, while capable of removing outliers, also distort the original 
vibration characteristics. To overcome these limitations, we propose a 
Bilateral Threshold Filtering (BTF) strategy that identiﬁes potential out-
liers by comparing each sample point with its neighbors and reallocates 
them accordingly.
Similar to most ﬁltering algorithms, the BTF applies a sliding win-
dow of ﬁve samples with a stride of one, as detailed in Algorithm 1. Let 
{𝜙1, …, 𝜙𝑖, …, 𝜙𝑛} denote a phase sequence of length 𝑛, where 𝜙𝑖 is the 
𝑖th sample, 𝑇  is the threshold, 𝑚𝑒𝑛𝑎(.) computes the arithmetic mean, 
and 𝑎𝑏𝑠(.) returns the absolute value. This algorithm identiﬁes a point 
as an outlier if its value deviates from both of its two immediate preced-
ing neighbors by a predeﬁned threshold. Once classiﬁed as an outlier, 
the point is updated to the average of the valid values among its four 
neighboring points. Therefore, the most critical step in BTF is determin-
ing the optimal threshold. As shown in Fig. 3, placing bookshelves and 
ﬁle boxes inside the cabinet generates the highest number of outliers. 
Hence, we focus on this scenario for threshold selection. Four environ-
mental layouts were created by varying the positions of ﬁle boxes on 
shelves, with 20,000 sample points collected for each conﬁguration. To 
identify the optimal threshold, we tested values from 0 to 0.2 in 0.01 
increments and recorded the variance of the ﬁltered samples. Results

3.3.2.  Filtering and smoothing
As discussed in Section 3.2, raw phase data often contain numer-
ous outliers that reduce recognition accuracy. To address this, we pro-
pose a BTF method that identiﬁes and reassigns outliers by comparing 
them with adjacent samples. However, even after ﬁltering, the signal 
still shows a 0.1 radian ﬂuctuation due to hardware noise, which re-
quires further smoothing. Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) oﬀers ad-
vantages in multi-resolution analysis and eﬃcient computation, making 
it suitable for signal smoothing, denoising, and compression. We there-
fore apply DWT to suppress residual noise remaining after BTF.

Hardware. We employ an Impinj R420 reader connected to a Laird 
S9028 antenna (8 dBic gain) via a feeder cable, along with an H47 tag 
serving as the reference tag. The antenna operates at a transmission fre-

is linked to an Acer PC featuring an Intel Core i7-10510U 2.30 GHz CPU 
and 16.0 GB RAM through an Ethernet cable.
Software. The RF-AbVib prototype was implemented using PyCharm 
2023.1.4 Community Edition as the integrated development environ-

1.14.0 and the Python 3.6.13 interpreter. Data was collected oﬄine us-

the inner wall, and 2 cm-thick foam boards are placed above and below

4.2.  Dataset collection
We deﬁned the data collected under normal and abnormal conditions 
separately. When the cabinet is stationary, the collected data is consid-
ered normal; when abnormal behaviors are applied, the data is classiﬁed 
as abnormal. Three types of abnormal behaviors were identiﬁed: slap-
ping, shaking, and pushing/pulling the cabinet door. To standardize the 
intensity of the slapping, a 200-gram sandbag was dropped freely from 
a height of 20 cm above the cabinet surface. Each data lasted approx-
imately 10 s. The abnormal behaviors were performed around the 5-s 
mark, lasted 1–2 s, and was executed only once per trial. In each envi-
ronment, 20 repetitions of normal data and 20 repetitions of each of the 
three types of abnormal data were collected, resulting in a total of 80 
data samples per environment.
A total of 12,480 oﬄine data samples were collected across the 31 
environments, with 2480 data samples allocated for model training and 
the remaining 10,000 used for performance evaluation. It is important 
to note that the data collected in each environment must be transformed 
into individual tasks, which includes both training tasks and testing 
tasks. Each task is further divided into a support set and a query set. 
For each data type, 5 samples are the support set, while the remain-
ing 15 samples are the query set. All data have been uploaded to the 
RF-AbVib dataset.

4.10.  Impact of cabinet material
To evaluate the model’s generalization, we extended testing to 
wooden and plastic cabinets whose internal layouts match that of the 
metal cabinet. The wooden cabinet measures 33 cm in length, 30.5 cm 
in width, and 46.5 cm in height, with 12 mm walls, while the plastic 
cabinet measures 38 cm in length, 37 cm in width, and 46 cm in height, 
with 4 mm walls, as shown in Fig. 18. We conducted experiments across 
the same ﬁve environments described in Section 4.6 and the results are 
presented in Table 4. As shown, the model’s performance was highest 
with the metal cabinet, intermediate with the plastic cabinet, and lowest 
with the wooden cabinet. We attribute the observed performance dif-
ferences to three main factors. (1) Electromagnetic shielding eﬀect. Metal 
eﬀectively isolates the cabinet’s interior from external electromagnetic 
interference and prevents signal ﬂuctuations caused by external envi-
ronmental changes, such as people walking by or waving their arms. 
(2) Material reﬂection. Metal surfaces reﬂect electromagnetic waves far 
more eﬃciently than wood or plastic, so even when the tag’s vibration 
amplitude falls below the system’s minimum detectable threshold (see 
Section 5.2), multi-path reﬂections still carry enough information to re-
veal abnormal behavior. (3) Material rigidity and thickness. The wooden 
cabinet’s walls are three times thicker than the plastic cabinet’s and 
about twenty times thicker than the metal cabinet’s, yet wood is far less 
ﬂexible. This extra rigidity and thickness dampen vibrations, producing 
smaller amplitudes and thereby lowering detection accuracy relative to 
the more responsive plastic and metal cases.

Impinj states in the Octane LLRP Guide [47] that the phase ﬁeld 
is a 12-bit value, corresponding to a minimum phase step of 2𝜋∕212 ≈
0.00153 rad for the Speedway R420. In contrast, ItemTest only supports 
the standard 10-bit phase output and lacks a vendor-extension interface, 
limiting its minimum phase step to 2𝜋∕210 ≈ 0 .00614 rad. Although this 
10-bit resolution is adequate for basic tag reading, the coarse quantiza-
tion error constrains vibration sensing accuracy. At this precision level, 
the minimum vibration amplitude detectable by the tag is:

The above accuracy analysis assumes ideal conditions. In practice,

For ﬁner detection, higher-frequency signals (e.g., millimeter waves) or 
advanced platforms (e.g., USRP) are recommended.
5.3.  Potential application scenarios
Although this paper focuses on smart cabinets, the RF-AbVib frame-
work can, in principle, be extended to vibration and shock monitoring 
in a variety of security scenarios. Potential applications include pry-
ing detection in safe-deposit boxes, museum exhibition cases, and bank 
ATMs, as well as warning systems for unauthorized breaches or damage 
to factory fences and warehouse gates. By analyzing the micro-vibration 
signatures of RFID tags, the system oﬀers non-contact alerts for intru-
sion and tampering. However, these applications remain at the proof-
of-concept stage. Because of substantial variations in materials, noise 
conditions, and installation conﬁgurations among diﬀerent targets, rig-
orous ﬁeld trials-supported by appropriate hardware selection and algo-
rithmic reﬁnement-are required to assess the approach’s responsiveness, 
sensitivity, and reliability.
5.4.  Security and privacy
While the application of RFID technology in smart cabinets has im-
proved storage security, it has also raised concerns about data security 
and user privacy. Monitoring abnormal vibrations with RF-AbVib may 
involve collecting user behavior data and environmental data. If these 
data are misused or leaked, it could lead to privacy breaches. There-
fore, future research should not only focus on the technical performance 
of the system but also address data security and privacy protection is-
sues. Implementing encryption technology and access control measures 
can ensure data security. Moreover, establishing transparent user agree-
ments and data processing procedures will help enhance user trust in 
the system, promoting the widespread application of RFID technology 
in smart cabinets.

6.  Related work
6.1.  Vibration sensing using RFID
RFID-based vibration sensing has been widely used in areas like 
machine fault diagnosis [8–16], vital signs monitoring [17,18], and 
speech eavesdropping [19–23]. Compared to traditional sensors [3], RF 
signals provide key advantages-long-distance transmission, non-line-of-
sight detection, contactless operation, and multi-target sensing-due to 
their free-space propagation properties.
6.1.1.  Machine fault diagnosis
Real-time vibration signal analysis is essential for equipment main-
tenance and safety. The simplest RFID-based method involves attaching 
tags directly to machines [8,11,12,15,16]. However, this can interfere 
with precision instruments and lead to tag detachment over time, re-
ducing accuracy. Therefore, researchers have focused on non-contact 
alternatives. For example, TagSMM [13] uses multiple tags to amplify 
vibration-induced phase changes, achieving high-resolution sensing. RF-
Ear [9] and RF-Ear+ [10] detect vibrations from multiple targets using 
a single tag, while RF-VSensing [14] applies machine learning to iden-
tify vibration states.
6.1.2.  Vital signs monitoring
Respiration and pulse generate rhythmic movements that are a form 
of vibration. Traditional methods use wearable devices [48], which 
can be uncomfortable and unsuitable for long-term monitoring. Tag-
Breathe [17] is the ﬁrst RFID-based system for respiration monitoring. 
It tracks multiple people’s breathing at once and separates respiration 
signals from body movement noise in diﬀerent environments. Escobedo 
et al. [18] developed a new bed-integrated RFID-based ballistocardio-
gram (BCG) system for non-contact vital sign monitoring. It accurately 
measures heart rate and respiration for two people lying in bed.
6.1.3.  Voice eavesdropping
Sound is a mechanical wave that travels through vibration. RF sig-
nals can detect voice remotely in soundproof environments, overcoming 
limits of traditional audio sensing. They interact with the environment 
during propagation-such as via Doppler and multipath eﬀects-to capture 
sound-induced vibrations. TagMic [22], built with COTS devices, was 
the ﬁrst to show RFID tags could be used for eavesdropping. Wang et 
al. [23] extended RFID sensing to enable through-wall eavesdropping. 
RF-Mic [20] uses RFID tags to create a low-cost, hidden, and ﬂexible 
voice monitoring system, marking an early application of RFID in voice 
surveillance.
6.2.  Environment independent sensing based on RFID
Current wireless sensing technologies struggle to maintain adaptabil-
ity across diverse environments. Wireless signal propagation is inﬂu-
enced by obstacles, noise, and dynamic environmental changes in diﬀer-
ent settings, leading to notable variations in system performance. Thus, 
developing environment-independent wireless sensing is crucial to en-
suring consistent stability and accuracy across varied scenarios, which 
enhances its applicability and practicality.
Researchers have studied various ways to improve the environmen-
tal robustness of wireless sensing systems. The phase-based model in 
Golipoor and Sigg[49] shows high accuracy across diﬀerent settings. 
But signal quality and individual diﬀerences still aﬀect reliability. RF-
Siamese [50] uses a Siamese network to achieve accurate recognition 
with limited training data. It works well in new environments but needs 
retraining for each deployment. Its ﬁxed sliding windows may also in-
clude irrelevant signals when processing gestures at diﬀerent speeds, 
lowering performance. RF-Sign [51] improves adaptability using data 
augmentation-like noise injection and zero padding-and optimization al-
gorithms, maintaining high accuracy under diverse conditions. RFPass 
[52,53] applies a multipath direction-of-arrival algorithm to reduce in-
terference and boost authentication accuracy. RFree-GR [36] combines 
multimodal data with adversarial learning to remove environment-
speciﬁc features, improving accuracy and generalization. However, it 
requires large gesture datasets, increasing data collection eﬀort. Also, 
strict user-device positioning limits its use in real-world scenarios.
7.  Conclusion
In this paper, we propose RF-AbVib, an environment-independent 
vibration monitoring system based on COTS RFID devices. The BTF al-
gorithm, integrated with discrete wavelet transform (DWT), eﬀectively 
eliminates outliers caused by environmental interference and hard-
ware noise, improving vibration feature extraction. A meta-learning-
based network architecture handles variable sampling rates and input 
lengths, making the system adaptable to diﬀerent environments. RF-
AbVib achieves 95.59 % accuracy across 31 diverse settings using just 
one training sample, greatly reducing data requirements. It also sim-
pliﬁes hardware design by requiring only a single reference tag in the 
smart cabinet, regardless of its location or type.
