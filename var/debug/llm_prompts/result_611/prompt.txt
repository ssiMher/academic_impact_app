Large-Scale Deployment of mmWave-based Sensing Systems
DUO ZHANG, Peking University, China

Millimeter wave (mmWave) radar excels in accurately estimating the distance, speed, and angle of the signal reflectors
relative to the radar. However, for diverse sensing applications reliant on radar’s tracking capability, these estimates must
be transformed from radar to room coordinates. This transformation hinges on the mmWave radar’s location attribute,
encompassing its position and orientation in room coordinates. Traditional outdoor calibration solutions for autonomous
driving utilize corner reflectors as static reference points to derive the location attribute. When deployed in the indoor
environment, it is challenging, even for the mmWave radar with GHz bandwidth and a large antenna array, to separate the
static reference points from other multipath reflectors. To tackle the static multipath, we propose to deploy a moving reference
point (a moving robot) to fully harness the velocity resolution of mmWave radar. Specifically, we select a SLAM-capable robot
to accurately obtain its locations under room coordinates during motion, without requiring human intervention. Accurately
pairing the locations of the robot under two coordinate systems requires tight synchronization between the mmWave radar
and the robot. We therefore propose a novel trajectory correspondence based calibration algorithm that takes the estimated
trajectories of two systems as input, decoupling the operations of two systems to the maximum. Extensive experimental
results demonstrate that the proposed calibration solution exhibits very high accuracy (1.74 cm and0.43◦accuracy for location
and orientation respectively) and could ensure outstanding performance in three representative applications: fall detection,
point cloud fusion, and long-distance human tracking.

1 INTRODUCTION
Indoor mmWave-based sensing applications have attracted significant attention from both academia [1, 26] as
well as industries [7, 24, 43]. Benefiting from its tracking accuracy of about0.15𝑚for targets [79], mmWave radar
has been successfully applied to human tracking [79], fall detection [28, 70], etc. However, these applications still
have not come into our daily life on a large scale. The key reason for this inability is that we lack an accurate
correspondence between the real physical coordinate system (for example, room coordinate) and the radar
coordinate system when practically deploying these systems on a large scale.
The bridge that connects the two coordinate systems is the radar’s location and orientation in the physical
map coordinate system, which we refer to as the radar’s location attribute . As shown in Figure 1(a), the mmWave
radar can only measure the user’s distance, angle, and radial velocity relative to the radar, which needs to be
transformed into the room coordinate to obtain the user’s location in the room. Such a transformation relies on
the 3D location and orientation of the radar antenna array in the physical map, i.e., the location attribute [39, 54].
As seen in Figure 1(b), a small error in the radar’s orientation will be magnified by the distance of the target from
the radar. At a target distance of 4𝑚from the radar, an orientation error of five degrees will result in a target
localization error of 0.17𝑚, which is comparable to the systematic error of 0.15𝑚for a mmWave tracking system.

Current systems implicitly assume that the location attributes are known. In fact, every time a radar is deployed
to a new environment, it needs to be calibrated with respect to its location attribute in that environment. It is,
however, challenging to measure the location attribute accurately. One can endeavor to manually measure the
location attribute of each radar in every environment, but that will inevitably be laborious, time-consuming,
error-prone, and even impossible sometimes due to several factors. Firstly, radars may be deployed in high
places, such as ceilings, posing high inconvenience and potential safety risks for manual measurements. Secondly,
radars are often encapsulated by the manufacturer, making it extremely difficult to know the exact antenna
array location on the radar. Finally, even if we can measure the location of the radar antenna array, manual
measurement of the radar 3D orientation (pitch, yaw, and roll) is difficult to achieve the required accuracy.
The calibration of radar location attributes has received significant attention in the realm of autonomous
driving [16, 31, 35, 38–41]. With the mmWave radar on vehicles capable of sensing up to one hundred meters,
even a minor orientation error could yield meter-level inaccuracies in pedestrian localization. In autonomous
driving scenarios [39, 40], the key task of the location attribute calibration is to find the point correspondences :
the coordinates of one point under two different coordinate systems, such as radar and vehicle coordinates. They
leverage the corner reflector (CR) as the reference point and manually measure the CR’s coordinate in the vehicle
coordinate system. Given the CR’s substantial radar cross-section, the CR’s coordinate in the radar coordinate can
also be sensed by the mmWave radar. They obtain a point correspondence by pairing CR coordinates across both
vehicle and radar systems. Multiple correspondences are gathered through CR placement at diverse positions
within a low-multipath environment. Based on the point correspondences, the radar’s location attribute can be
solved [29].
In indoor radar deployments, conventional calibration algorithms from the autonomous driving domain
encounter challenges for the following reasons. Firstly, manually measuring the coordinates of reference points
is time-consuming and laborious. Unlike vehicles, which can be calibrated uniformly in a wide workshop at
the factory, deploying reference points for calibration in every new environment is impractical when the radar
is deployed on a large scale. Secondly, indoor calibration is complicated by intricate multipath effects. For the
vehicle, we can drive the vehicle to an open area with minimum multipath. It is, however, impossible to move the
reflectors with the large radar cross-section in the indoor environment, such as walls, tables, and floors. Amidst

In this paper, we present LoCal, an automatic indoor radar location attribute calibration system that achieves
extremely high accuracy (1.74 cm and 0.43◦accuracy for location and orientation calibration respectively) and
at the same time requires zero human effort . Our key observation is that even though the mmWave radar does
not have enough spatial resolution to separate the reference point from other nearby reflectors, it has sufficient
velocity resolution to separate signals of any moving object from all static objects [44]. Accordingly, we propose
to let the reference point move when we perform the calibration.
The movement of the reference point, however, poses significant challenges for obtaining the point corre-
spondences. First, even though the mmWave radar can easily locate the moving reference point under the radar
coordinate system, the system needs a method to derive the position of the reference point under the room
coordinate system. Second, pairing each instantaneous position of the moving reference under two coordinate
systems to get the point correspondence requires tight synchronization between the mmWave radar and the
extra location sensing system that provides the reference’s location under the room coordinate system. which
introduces significant hardware costs and makes the calibration process labor-intensive.
To accurately locate the reference point under the room coordinate system with zero human effort, we propose
to use a small wheeled robot with SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) system as our reference point,
just as shown in Figure 3. When deployed in a new environment, the robot first maps the physical environment
to obtain the floor map. During the calibration, the robot automatically plans a trajectory to traverse the sensing
coverage of the mmWave sensing system and locate itself under the room coordinate system.
To eliminate the requirement of tight synchronization between the robot and the mmWave system, instead of
using the point correspondence, we propose a novel trajectory correspondence based location attribute calibration
solution. Specifically, we first derive two moving trajectories of the same robot with the tracking results of the
mmWave radar and the SLAM system on the robot and then transform the trajectory from the radar coordinate
to the room coordinate using the location attribute of the mmWave radar. If a correct location attribute is used in
the transformation, the transformed trajectory and the measured trajectory from the SLAM should match. We
then search for the best location attribute that minimizes the distance between two trajectories. To accelerate
the search process, we require the robot to choose a linear trajectory when planning the route, so one motion
trajectory consists of multiple linear sub-trajectories. Therefore, instead of matching the whole trajectory, we
match each linear sub-trajectory to build a line correspondence . We establish a theoretical model for location
attribute calibration using line correspondences, based on which we have proven that theoretically, we are able
to perform the calibration with only two line correspondences.

2 PRELIMINARY OF MMWAVE RADAR
In this section, we first introduce the fundamentals of mmWave radar. Then, we describe the mathematical
representation of the radar’s location attribute (location and orientation).
2.1 mmWave Radar Primer
Commercial mmWave radars commonly employ the frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) technique,
emitting successive chirps with linearly increasing frequency over time from the transmitting antennas. The
interaction of these signals with the environment and subsequent reflection to receiving antennas introduces a
time-of-flight-induced frequency shift between transmitted and received signals. By mixing the received signal
with the transmitted signal, we can get the Intermediate Frequency (IF) signal of a chirp whose frequency is
proportional to the range between the radar and the target. Moving objects induce Doppler frequency shifts
proportional to phase differences across consecutive chirps. Accordingly, by estimating the frequencies of sample
points in a chirp and across chirps, we can estimate the distance and radial velocity of the targets.
2.1.1 Range and velocity resolution. A typical mmWave radar performs range and radial velocity measurement
by sending back-to-back chirps. Different transmitting antennas take turns sending chirps. The radar frame,
composed of multiple chirps received from different antennas, is the basic unit for estimating range and radial
velocity. Additionally, there is a predetermined period of idle time between each frame. Given a radar with
bandwidth 𝐵, the ranging and radial velocity resolutions can be calculated as follows:

2.2 Radar’s Location Attribute
The radar’s location attribute is concerned with the geometry of the radar’s installation in the physical map. It
serves as a bridge linking the radar world and the physical world, enabling the transformation of radar sensing
outcomes into the physical map. Specifically, it refers to the three-dimensional location and orientation of the
radar antenna array in the physical map.
Mathematically, we use a translation vector (𝑇) to represent the radar’s position, which describes the relative
position between the origins of the two coordinate systems, for example, the radar coordinate system and the
physical map coordinate system. Similarly, we represent the radar’s orientation using a rotation matrix ( 𝑅),
describing the rotation between the two coordinate systems. Assuming the coordinate of a target in the radar

3 SYSTEM DESIGN
We present the system design of LoCal in this section. We start with an overview of LoCal, followed by the
introduction of line correspondence generation based on trajectory correspondence, which is the preparation
for solving the radar’s location attribute. Then, we present LoCal’s algorithm to determine the deployment
orientation of the radar that needs to achieve the stringent requirement of error-sensitive applications. Finally,
we discuss how to locate the mmWave radar in the physical map based on line correspondences.
3.1 System Overview
Recall that our main goal is to automatically and accurately determine the radar’s location attribute in the
physical map, facilitating large-scale deployment. Therefore, as demonstrated in Figure 4, LoCal first leverages a
SLAM-based autonomous robot [59] to map the physical environment and automatically plan its own trajectory
to traverse the sensing coverage of the mmWave radar. Concurrently, all the mmWave radars in the environment
extract the point cloud of the robot as it moves. The robot’s trajectory under the radar coordinate can be
reconstructed by combing the point clouds of the moving robot over time. We define the mapping between
the trajectory under the physical map coordinate and the trajectory under the radar coordinate together as the
trajectory correspondence. Line correspondences are generated from the trajectory correspondence. Ultimately,
the radar’s location attribute in the physical map is estimated based on these line correspondences.
3.2 Generating Line Correspondences

dence. A line correspondence encapsulates line equations in two distinct coordinate systems. In our scenario, these
two coordinate systems refer to the physical map coordinate and the radar coordinate. Utilizing the SLAM-based
autonomous robot, we achieve accurate and automatic generation of these line correspondences.

3.2.1 Mapping the Environment. First, we need to obtain a physical map of the environment. We use the laser
SLAM algorithm equipped on the robot to build the 2D planar map of the environment. The specific SLAM
algorithm we adopt is Cartographer [22], which is a graph-based real-time SLAM algorithm commonly used for
two-dimensional localization and mapping in indoor environments. As the robot travels through the environment,
the Cartographer builds a 2D map of the environment using data provided by LiDAR as well as the Inertial
Measurement Unit (IMU).
3.2.2 Automatic Robot Navigation. Utilizing the generated floor map, the robot automatically generates a trajec-
tory and navigates itself to traverse the area covered by the mmWave radar. It is worth noting that we require
the robot to select trajectories that contain linear sub-trajectories, as shown in Figure 3. Leveraging the SLAM
algorithm’s ability to provide the robot’s position and orientation, our method orchestrates the robot to navigate
through sequential map nodes, thereby forming the desired trajectory. Nodes in the planned trajectory are
selected automatically by our algorithm based on the created map and our algorithm will guarantee no obstacles
between the nodes. To facilitate the splitting of each sub-trajectory, the robot remains stationary at each node for
three seconds before proceeding to the next node.
Although the trajectory is two-dimensional, we can easily obtain its representation in 3D space by establishing
the 3D coordinate system as shown in Figure 3. This is because the z-axis is perpendicular to the ground and its
z-coordinate does not change during the robot’s movement. Therefore, we use the height of the small robot as
the z-coordinate of its trajectory, thus obtaining a representation of that trajectory in the 3-dimensional physical
map coordinate system. This trajectory is represented by the coordinates of several nodes that the robot passes
in sequence and the order of these nodes.
3.2.3 Extracting Point Cloud. We need to obtain the trajectory of the robot in the radar coordinate system, as it
is one of the inputs to our calibration algorithm. We chose the 4D point cloud (the coordinates in 3D space plus
the velocity) based tracking algorithm to obtain the coordinates of the robot in the radar coordinate system for
the following two reasons. Firstly, while there are various tracking methods based on mmWave radar, such as
those using RSS fingerprint [20], CSI [61], or point cloud [2, 28, 49, 70, 79], the current state-of-the-art tracking
method is based on point cloud [75]. Secondly, 4D point cloud based tracking methods enables us to filter out
static multipath interference indoors using the velocity dimension in the 4D point cloud, ideal for tracking small
Proc. ACM Interact. Mob. Wearable Ubiquitous Technol., Vol. 7, No. 4, Article 199. Publication date: December 2023.
199:8 • Zhang et al.
robots in multipath-rich indoor environments. The above two reasons motivate us to choose the 4D point cloud
based tracking method.
As the robot moves, we simultaneously extract the point cloud of the robot by the mmWave radar to reconstruct
the trajectory under the radar coordinate system. In this paper, we work with the CAL60S244-IB sensor from
Calterah Semiconductor Technology, which is an FMCW-MIMO radar sensor that operates in the60𝐺𝐻𝑧 band.
This radar employs FMCW for range estimation and MIMO for determining azimuth and elevation angles,
resulting in 3D coordinates. These coordinates are subsequently converted into a 3D point cloud for each frame.
Further considering the velocity information of each point, the 4D point cloud can be constructed [28, 70].
Because we know the radial velocity of each point in the point cloud, we can keep only the point cloud of
the moving robot, thus filtering out other static objects in the environment that have zero velocity. Since the
robot will stay at each node of the trajectory for three seconds, no point cloud is retained during these three
seconds. This temporal gap significantly aids in segmenting the trajectory. It is worth noting that, given the
robot’s compact size as a reflective point, we exclusively retain the point with the highest power for the point
cloud of each frame. Consequently, the trajectory under the radar coordinate is obtained by aggregating the point
cloud of the moving robot captured over time by the mmWave radar. Together with the trajectory of the robot in
the physical map coordinate system, the trajectory correspondence is obtained.

3.2.4 Line Correspondences. Utilizing the established trajectory correspondence, we proceed to derive line
correspondences, a fundamental step in solving for the radar’s location attribute. The robot’s trajectory consists
of several linear sub-trajectories. For each linear sub-trajectory, its equation in the physical map coordinate
system can be determined simply by fitting the coordinates of the two endpoints. To enhance the precision of
equation fitting, we also use the locations provided by the SLAM as the robot moves between the two endpoints
in fitting the line equation. The fitting method we adopted is the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). The idea
behind this is that SVD can find the best-fit subspaces of a set of data points [ 8]. In terms of line fitting, the
3D locations of the moving robot are data points in 3D space and the line we expect is the one-dimensional
best-fit subspace. We leverage the parametric equation to represent the fitted line equation in the physical map
coordinate system:

three-by-one direction vector that represents the direction of the line and the𝑡is the parameter. It is worth noting
that 𝑙𝑝 can be any point on the line and ®𝑑𝑝 can be any vector parallel to the line. Instead of picking a random

attribute estimation easier. We let it follow the direction of the robot’s movement in terms of direction. In terms

We also need to fit the equation of this linear sub-trajectory in the radar coordinate system to form a line
correspondence. Since there is no dynamic point cloud generation for the three seconds that the robot remains
stationary at each node in the trajectory, we can easily split the corresponding point cloud of a sub-trajectory
from the point cloud of the whole trajectory. Figure 5 demonstrates the 4D point cloud corresponding to a
sub-trajectory.
We also use the SVD-based method for fitting the line equation in the radar coordinate system based on the
4D point cloud. However, because of the dynamic multipath indoor, utilizing the SVD directly does not produce
the desired results. Dynamic multipaths are caused by radar signals reflected from dynamic targets in the room
other than the robot (e.g., people) or by radar signals reflected multiple times by the robot. Compared to the point
cloud of a moving robot, the point cloud of dynamic multipaths is usually clustered in one place, such as the
noisy point cloud shown in Figure 5, which is the result of a person in the room.
When using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) for line fitting, it is particularly sensitive to outliers because
SVD is essentially a least square fitting method [8]. The objective of least squares fitting is to find a line that
minimizes the sum of squared distances from data points to the line. When the dataset contains outliers, these
points may be far away from other data points (as shown Figure 5), causing their influence to be magnified in
the squared sum calculation. Since SVD utilizes all data points during the computation, the impact of outliers
is weighted into the fitting result, leading to deviations from the true trend of the line (as shown Figure 6). We
adopted the RANSAC algorithm to address the issue of outliers [14]. RANSAC randomly samples subsets of the
data points and fits a line to these subsets, excluding the influence of outliers. By repeatedly sampling and fitting,
RANSAC eventually obtains a fitting result that performs well on the majority of data points, thus improving the
robustness of the fitting. After incorporating the idea of RANSAC into the SVD-based line equation fitting, we
successfully deal with outliers. As shown in Figure 6, the red solid line is the result of this method. We can see
that the line of the moving robot is successfully fitted and is not affected by the outlier points.

Hardware Implementations. Our system uses Calterah’s CAL60S244-IB [50], an antenna-in-package (Aip)
radar sensor chip with 4 TX and 4 RX antenna array. It integrates a single-chip FMCW radar sensor capable
of operation in the 60 to 64 𝐺𝐻𝑧 band. The physical appearance of the CAL60S244-IB radar device and the
arrangement of its antenna array are illustrated in Figure 8. We employ a transmit signal bandwidth of4𝐺𝐻𝑧,

has a duration of 90𝜇𝑠, corresponding to the velocity resolution of 5.43𝑐𝑚/𝑠. And the radar transmits 15 frames

memory. We use a commercial wheeled robot [59] to map the environment and also to act as a reference target

Software Implementations. We implement the signal processing on Matlab. The radar’s sampling data is
forwarded to the laptop through a TCP connection when the radar has captured the reflection signals from the
surrounding area. Similarly, the physical map created and the robot’s trajectory in the map are transmitted to the
laptop via the WIFI module on the Raspberry Pi. We then extract the point cloud, fit equations of lines in both
coordinate systems and calibrate the radar’s location attribute.

5.2.1 Fall Detection Based on Accurate Tracking of Human Height. In this application, we demonstrate LoCal can
be used to fall detection since LoCal enables the accurate tracking of human height. Falls are the leading cause of
fatal and nonfatal injuries to elders in modern society [70]. We employ the concept of life-threatening fall [70],
which means that not all falls mean a person is in danger. A critical situation occurs when a person becomes
incapacitated due to severe injury and cannot get up without assistance after falling and lying on the ground. In
this section, we implement a life-threatening fall detection system based on accurate tracking of human height.
We model a life-threatening fall as a two-stage process: sudden height drop followed by prolonged lying down
on the ground [70]. Accordingly, the system is comprised of two stages: a fall detection stage, in which we detect
potential falls depending on whether the body shows a drop in height, and a verification stage, in which we
validate whether the potential falls are indeed life-threatening based on the body’s height after a height drop.
In the first stage, we leverage the mmWave radar to extract the 4D point cloud of the human body. Using LoCal,
the 4D point cloud can be transformed from the radar coordinate system into the physical map coordinate system.

5.2.2 Point Cloud Fusion of Multiple mmWave Radars. Next, we show the feasibility of using LoCal to fuse the
point cloud of multiple mmWave radars in different views to improve the point cloud quality. There are a number
of research and commercial applications that employ mmWave radar point clouds, such as pose estimation
[15, 48, 56], and human identification [79]. However, the quality of point cloud derived from single mmWave
radar are very poor due to the limited resolution and noise in the environment [9, 56]. In this section, we propose
a method to improve the quality of the point cloud by fusing the point cloud from multiple mmWave radars in
different views.
To effectively fuse point clouds from multiple radars, we should align the point cloud in both time and space.
Firstly, temporal alignment of point clouds from multiple radars is necessary since data at different times cannot
be fused. We align the point cloud of each frame from different radars as much as possible by using the timestamp
of each frame. Another challenge is aligning the point cloud from different radars in the same coordinate system.
Thus, we transform the point cloud from multiple radars in different views into the room coordinate system
based on the location attribute of each radar. Because of the close distance between different parts of the human
body, the fusion of point clouds from different radars has a high demand for the accuracy of the location attribute.
Our proposed calibration system exactly provides the accurate location attributes that are needed.

6 RELATED WORK
RF-based sensing. In recent years, there has been an increasing amount of literature on contactless sensing
using various RF signals including WiFi [36, 45, 57, 62, 63], RFID [52, 53], UWB [72, 73], LoRa [11, 71] and FMCW
radar [9, 78] to sense people and objects. Meanwhile, researchers have developed systems ranging from human
vital sign monitoring [69, 74], sleep monitoring [67], gesture recognition to fall detection [55] and daily activity
recognition [27]. Among these technologies, FMCW radar has a broad development prospect because of the
advantages of large bandwidth, small antenna, high resolution, and the ability to sense the distance, speed, and
angle of the target through a single device.
mmWave-based sensing. Benefiting from the outstanding sensing capability and reduced cost with the manu-
facturing process maturity, mmWave radar has been widely used in autonomous driving and industrial production
in recent years. Due to mmWave radar’s short wavelength and large bandwidth, high-precision applications like
vibration measurement [26, 64], vital signs monitoring [21, 30, 65], tracking [33, 66], gesture recognition [37, 58]
are exploited. Meanwhile, the emergence of applications such as pose estimation [15, 56], personal identification
[17], and object imaging [1, 13], speech recognition [68] broaden the boundaries of mmWave radar.
Location attribute calibration of sensors. With the need for sensing in information systems, a range of
sensors are developed, such as cameras, LIDAR, WiFi, mmWave radar, etc. The information sensed directly by
the sensors is of no use unless the sensors are calibrated. In the case of cameras mounted on robots, what is
really useful is information about the perceived target relative to the robot, not relative to the camera. Therefore,
the mounting position and orientation of the camera must be identified, resulting in the extrinsic calibration
problem [6, 54]. Moreover, location attribute calibration is a common requirement for multisensor fusion. This is
because multisensor data fusion can only be performed when the data from multiple sensors are transformed
into the same coordinate system. Therefore, the location attribute calibration of various sensors, such as camera
[6, 34, 54], WiFi [5], LIDAR [38, 77] and mmWave radar [23, 39], has been extensively studied in the past decades.
Location attribute calibration of mmWave radar. As mmWave radar is adaptable to various kinds of bad
weather, it is first widely used in the field of autonomous driving as a complement to vision and LIDAR [80]. As a
result, most of the current location attribute calibration works for mmWave radar are serving for autonomous
driving [16, 23, 31, 35, 38–41, 76]. The key task of the calibration is to find the point correspondence: the
coordinates of one point under two different coordinate systems, for example, the radar coordinate system and
the vehicle/camera/LIDAR coordinate system. They typically leverage the designed calibration target, such as a
corner reflector, as the reference point to collect point correspondences in an open environment. However, the
traditional method of gathering point correspondences no longer works when the scene changes to indoors due
to the rich multipath. Besides, manually measuring the coordinates of the reference points in the indoor physical
map coordinate system is impractical when mmWave radars are deployed on a large scale.

In contrast, LoCal automatically and accurately calibrates the location attribute of mmWave radars in the
indoor environment. It achieves automation by an autonomous robot equipped with the SLAM algorithm as the
reference point and realizes high accuracy of calibration by the line correspondences based algorithm.
7 DISCUSSION
In this section, we discuss the limitation and potential direction of our future work.
Deployment effort. Although LoCal requires zero human intervention, it still relies on a robot to move around
the room and perform SLAM, which still has a relatively large deployment effort for individual consumers.
Nevertheless, when compared to manually measuring the location attributes of mmWave radar, our proposed
approach achieves remarkable accuracy while eliminating the need for any human intervention. Moreover,
enterprise-level customers like hospitals, nursing homes, and real estate developers often deploy radar systems
on a large scale, encompassing entire buildings. In such scenarios, our approach becomes highly practical for
calibrating the location attributes of numerous radars. When dealing with individual consumers, it is far easier
and more accurate to have a radar installer carry a robot than to take manual measurements. Looking ahead,
integrating this technology into commercially available sweeping robots could empower individual consumers to
calibrate their radars autonomously on their own.
Environmental Interference. As demonstrated in Section 5.1.10, LoCal cannot function properly when there
are people walking in the room. However, minor human activities (such as typing, using a phone, etc.) and the
rotation of fans, which involve limited movement within the room, do not affect our system. Therefore, when
calibrating the location attribute of the radar using LoCal, we request that people in the room try to remain
seated on chairs or sofas and avoid walking. We believe this is an acceptable requirement.
Extension to diverse sensing tasks. Our proposed system LoCal is a framework for location attribute calibration
of mmWave radar so that it is able to enhance the sensing performance of diverse sensing tasks. In this paper,
we use fall detection, point cloud fusion, and long-distance human tracking as representative sensing tasks to
illustrate the feasibility and performance of our system. We leave the extension to other sensing tasks as our
future work.
Further applications of point cloud fusion. In this paper, we demonstrate that the quality of point clouds
can be enhanced significantly by fusing point clouds from multiple radars. Further, the fused point cloud can
be employed to improve the performance of sensing tasks that are based on the radar point cloud, such as
fall detection, pose estimation, people identification, etc. In our future research, we intend to explore further
applications of point cloud fusion.
8 CONCLUSION
In this paper, we present the design and implementation of LoCal, to the best of our knowledge, the very first
indoor mmWave radar location attribute calibration system that can easily, automatically, and accurately calibrate
radar’s location attributes in complicated indoor environments, with zero human effort involved. LoCal leverages a
moving robot with SLAM capability as the reference point for calibration and propose a trajectory correspondence
based calibration algorithm. Extensive experimental results demonstrate that LoCal significantly improves the
performance of mmWave-based sensing applications.
