RFNOID: Protecting RFID Motion Privacy via Metasurface
Yanni Yang∗, Zheng Shi ∗, Zhenlin An †, Runyu Pan ∗, Yanling Bu‡, Guoming Zhang ∗, Pengfei Hu ∗, Jiannong Cao §
∗Shandong University, †University of Pittsburgh, ‡Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics,
§The Hong Kong Polytechnic University
Abstract—RFID tags have become prevailing human motion
sensors in applications, like smart homes and health monitor-
ing. However, RFID’s powerful through-wall sensing capabilities
raise significant privacy concerns about leaking human motion
information, which the RFID sensing community has overlooked.
To fill this gap, we propose RF NOID, the first system that
can protect human motion privacy against adversarial through-
wall RFID sensing. The key enabler is our design of an RFID
metasurface consisting of multiple 1-bit phase shifters that can
obfuscate motion information. As a pioneering work, we perform
theoretical modeling and exploratory studies of the metasurface
effect on RFID signals in temporal and spectral domains. Based
on the preliminary analysis, we find it is non-trivial to achieve
effective signal obfuscation in both domains due to the trade-off
in simultaneously increasing temporal signal chaos and masking
the human motion spectrum. To tackle this issue, we judiciously
devise a metasurface controlling strategy by jointly optimizing
the signal entropy, variance, and spectrum distribution to balance
the temporal and spectral motion obfuscation. Extensive experi-
ments demonstrate that RF NOID can significantly decrease the
adversarial through-wall motion detection rate to less than 6%
and increase the respiration estimation error by over 3 ×.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Today RFID-based human sensing systems have surged in
popularity, revolutionizing various aspects of our lives, such
as intrusion detection [1–3], localization and tracking [4–
6], health monitoring [7–9], and activity recognition [10–12].
However, the widespread adoption of low-cost RFID tags and
the richness of their sensing capabilities, particularly their
through-wall sensing ability, render non-negligible privacy
concerns [13–15]. Numerous studies have shown successful
attempts in through-wall human motion [3–5] and speech
eavesdropping [15–18] via RFID tags for adversarial uses.
Besides, the physiological and behavioral features [9, 19] cap-
tured by RFID signals for human authentication can be easily
intercepted via malicious sniffing. Therefore, it is imperative
to protect RFID-based human motion sensing systems from
unauthorized access and malicious attacks.
However, existing literature lacks an efficient way to safe-
guard RFID motion sensing. Current defenses to communi-
cation eavesdropping, such as channel randomization [20],
require customized RFID readers, making them incompati-
ble with existing systems. Furthermore, recent solutions for
protecting other wireless sensing systems, including FMCW
radar [21] and WiFi [22, 23], cannot be directly applied to
RFID due to essential differences in protocols and sensing
models. In short, we desire a new method to defend against
privacy intrusions in RFID-based motion sensing systems.

The devised metasurface is configured to be compatible with
current RFID systems for seamless integration. By deploying

the RFID sensing model and received signal indicator (i.e.,

III. T HREAT MODEL
RFNOID aims at protecting human motion privacy leaked
by adversarial through-wall RFID sensing. Details of the threat
model are described as follows.
Adversary setup : The adversary aims to covertly monitor
the victim’s motions within an enclosed room, such as an
office or home. Nonetheless, the adversary fails to access the
area neither physically nor visibly, but manages to place the
easily obtainable commodity RFID reader and antenna near the
target room instead, deploying them in a nearby room close
to high-risk areas such as wooden doors or glass walls, where
RFID signals are more likely to penetrate, while avoiding
locations shielded by dense materials, like thick concrete
walls, where signals would be blocked. To obtain the human
motion-affected RFID signal, the adversary may (1) place
several RFID tags behind the wall, as shown in Fig. 2(a), if no
tag is inside the target room, or (2) directly receive the signal
from the tag positioned in the room without deploying extra
tags, as depicted in Fig. 2(b). In this work, we exemplify two

IV. RFID M ETASURFACE DESIGN AND MODELING
A. Fabrication of the Reflective Metasurface
In RF NOID, we fabricate a low-cost metasurface with N
uniformly placed units, as depicted in Fig. 14(a). The detailed
composition of each unit is shown in Fig. 3. On the top of the
unit is a patch antenna that reflects the UHF RFID signal. In
the middle is a substrate followed by the ground plane at the
bottom. The key component in the unit is the PIN dioxide D
whose on/off states change the reflection coefficient. Accord-
ingly, when the controller output C(t) switches between low
and high voltages, the unit will demonstrate a 180◦ phase shift,
i.e. 1-bit phase shifter between 0 and π, over the incident RFID
signal. In our implementation, we optimize the patch antenna
size and substrate thickness in the CST studio to ensure an
effective 180◦ phase shift over the UHF frequency band. We
also add a biasing resistor Rb and a decoupling capacitor Cd
to stabilize the circuit. The LED and a current limit resistor Rl
are used to monitor the controller output voltage. We employ
Arduino Mega 2560 with 54 output pins as the controller,
which can support up to 7 ×7 sized metasurface. The total
metasurface fabrication cost for a 4 ×4 metasurface is around
$90 USD, which is comparable with the expense of one RFID
antenna. Once fabricated, the metasurface is placed in high-
risk indoor areas, i.e., behind wooden doors or glass walls.

we conduct preliminary studies to investigate the metasurface
effect from both temporal and spectral perspectives.

considering the commonly used variance-based approach to
detect human motions. From Eq. (3), we conjecture that flip-

running metasurface makes it difficult to distinguish the phase

static environments, as shown in Fig. 5(c). Thus, this strategy
can obfuscate the motion signal in the temporal domain.
However, when the phase in Fig. 5(c) is transformed into

frequency bands. Besides, the human respiration rate also lies
in a similar low-frequency band within 1 Hz. Note that simply
decreasing the unit flipping rate for stronger low-frequency
components is inadequate because this fails to mask the high-
frequency harmonics of human motions. Therefore, using the
current metasurface configuration, we face the following issue.

V. M ETASURFACE CONTROLLING STRATEGY FOR MOTION
SIGNAL OBFUSCATION
Based on the preliminary analysis, a well-designed metasur-
face controlling strategy is required to successfully obfuscate
human motion patterns from both temporal and spectral per-
spectives and defend against adversary sensing algorithms. To
achieve this, we first summarize three goals for the desired
metasurface controlling strategy as below:
Goal 1: Attain a high phase entropy. The adversary sensing
algorithms are designed to extract assured and relevant motion
information from the RFID signal phase. To obfuscate motion
signals, the metasurface incurred phase ought to attain a high
entropy with greater unpredictability and complexity.
Goal 2: Attain a high temporal phase variance . The large-
scale human motions can cause significant temporal phase
changes. The metasurface should be controlled to attain drastic
phase variation to overwhelm the motion phase changes.
Goal 3: Attain strong spectral components at human motion
frequencies. Human motions are dominant in low frequencies.
Thus, the metasurface phase spectrum shall attain stronger
low-frequency components to mask the motion spectrum.
We then devise and implement a new metasurface control-
ling strategy to fulfill these goals.
A. Goal 1: Achieving High Phase Entropy
In information theory, entropy is a measure of the signal
uncertainty. To confuse the adversary, we should maximize the
entropy of the RFID signal phase reflected by the metasurface.
As Eq. (3) indicates, the on/off state of each metasurface unit
determines the phase change. Therefore, the goal of maximiz-
ing the phase entropy can be transformed into maximizing
the entropy in selecting the unit state. As such, given a N-
sized metasurface, the selection of the unit state entails two
subsequent steps: (1) How many units to flip? and (2) Which
units to flip? Since the two steps are orthogonal, the maximum
entropy is attained by maximizing the entropy of each step.
The above two questions in steps (1) and (2) can solved by
formulating them into a mathematical problem. Specifically,

step (1) is to select an integer Xn in [0, N], and step (2) is to

actually the same problem: Given a finite discrete range, what
is the maximum entropy distribution? Based on the Principle
of Maximum Entropy [28], the answer is uniform distribution.
Thus, the number of units Xn to flip in step (1) and the
set of units Xs, where Xs = {Ui, ...} and |Xs| = Xn,
selected from the N-sized metasurface in step (2) should all
follow the uniform distribution. To this end,Xn and Xs should
be randomly picked with equal probability. Accordingly, we
shape a base controlling strategy as follows:
Base strategy : When controlling the metasurface, we
first randomly select the number of units Xn to flip from
{0, 1, 2, ..., N}, then flip a random combination of Xn units
among all units.
To generate random numbers with real-world entropy, we
utilize the Arduino free-running timer tr as the seed to gener-
ate pseudorandom numbers for Xn and Xs. Then, we apply
this strategy to control metasurface units and compare the
affected phase spectrum with and without human motions. The
orange spectrum in Fig. 8 shows that with maximized entropy,
the spectrum without human motions is dispersed among a
relatively wide frequency band with no outstanding frequency
component. In contrast to the orange spectrum with a strong
10 Hz component in Fig. 5(d), the flattened spectrum can
inhibit the adversary from eliminating the metasurface effect
using certain filters. However, enhancing the entropy alone
with randomly flipped units results in mediocre obfuscation
performance in both temporal and spectral domains. The
phase variance requires further enlargement, and the phase
spectrum demands stronger components around human motion
frequencies. Thus, two additional refinements of the Base
strategy are introduced in the following sections.
B. Goal 2: Achieving High Phase Variance
As indicated by Observation 1 and Fig. 5(a), larger phase
variations are obtained when more units flip their states. Thus,
we can attach a higher probability to the simultaneous flipping
of a greater number of units in the Base strategy. Specifically,
we add one condition that inspects the number of switched-
on states non among all units at the current timestamp. If
the current non is small (e.g., 0, 1, and 2) and we switch on
all units at the next timestamp, the number of flipped units
becomes larger. Similarly, if the current non is large (e.g.,
N −1 and N), we can switch off all units to make more units
change their states. In this way, the possibility of flipping a
large number of units is increased compared with the Base
strategy. Meanwhile, random unit flipping is still maintained.

Strategy refinement 1 : If a random flip resulted in a non

we flip units randomly according to the Base strategy.
In the mechanism above, two thresholds σs and σl are yet to
be assigned, where σs is used to find the timestamp when non
is smaller than σs × N, and σl is for detecting the timestamp
when non is larger than σl × N. To determine σs and σl, we
draw on the phase deviation curve along different percentages

detected, i.e., 25% and 75% of all units on the metasurface.
The phase deviations below 25% and above 75% units endure
prominent decline and incline, respectively. Therefore, by
setting σs and σl to 25% and 75%, we can acquire more
significant phase variation in the temporal domain.
We combine the Base strategy and refinement 1 to show
the metasurface affected phase and its standard deviation in

integrating refinement 1 introduces larger phase variances,
which are beneficial for obfuscating temporal motion signals.
C. Goal 3: Achieving Strong Spectral Components around the
Human Motion Frequency Band
Recall that the entropy enhancement in the Base strategy
falls short of obfuscating the spectral human motion pattern
in low frequencies. Thus, the third goal is to refine the
metasurface controlling strategy so that frequency components
can be dominant around the human motion frequency band.
To fulfill this goal, the first step is to elucidate which
frequency band the human-reflected RFID signal phase is
located. For the large-scale walking motion, the moving torso
and limbs have different effects on the RFID signal phase.
Suppose the torso moves with a speed of v m/s. Then, it will
introduce ∼ 2v change of the path length dh per second. Once
the dh changes over one wavelength of the RFID signal, the
phase will fluctuate over 2 π; thus the phase changes with
a frequency of 2v/λ. Since the household walking speed
is mostly within 0.4 m/s [29], the corresponding frequency
band is below 2.5 Hz. At the same time, the limb movement
swinging at 1 Hz-2 Hz directly reflects the phase frequency. In
terms of small-scale breathing activity, the human respiration
rate is mostly around 0.2 Hz-0.5 Hz [30]. In a word, the
interested human motions mainly move within 0.2 Hz-2.5 Hz.
Next, we employ Observation 2, which states that flipping
fewer units can produce stronger low-frequency components.

6) Impact of metasurface on legitimate RFID sensing:
A key question using RF NOID for privacy-preserving RFID
sensing is whether the metasurface will affect the legitimate
RFID sensing function inside the room. As shown in Fig. 2(b),
the RFID tags inside the room are utilized to monitor the
elderly person’s respiration. The metasurface should not affect
the original RFID sensing task.
Thus, we investigate the impact of metasurface on legitimate
RFID sensing. Specifically, we deploy the legitimate RFID
antenna beside the person, as depicted in Fig. 14(c). Then, we
simultaneously measure the RFID signal from the legitimate
and adversary antennas with the metasurface running. As
shown in Fig. 20, the phase from the legitimate antenna

adversary one is obfuscated. Thus, the metasurface does not
affect legitimate RFID sensing. This is because the metasur-
face facing the wall does not reflect the signal transmitted from
the legitimate antenna thanks to its directivity.
VII. R ELATED WORKS
This section introduces the related works in studying privacy
issues of RFID and RF human sensing.
A. Privacy-concerned RFID Human Motion Sensing
RFID-based human motion sensing has raised significant
privacy concerns, especially due to its through-wall sens-
ing capability [13]. RFID signals mainly expose three types
of private information, i.e., human presence and location,
living habits, and biometrics [33]. For example, Tadar [4]
and RF-HMS [3] manage to detect and track the walking
motion through the wall. TASA [34] and FEMO [12] realize
contactless human daily activity monitoring by deploying
RFID tags in the environment. Many physiological features,
e.g., respiration [9] and facial dynamics [35], and behavioral
features, e.g., gait [36] and handshake [37], can be captured
by RFID signals for human authentication and identification.
In addition, researchers have demonstrated the feasibility of
using commodity RFID devices for through-wall eavesdrop-
ping [15, 17]. Thus, it is crucial to implement privacy measures
to secure RFID sensing systems. However, existing studies
mainly focus on securing RFID communication [20, 38] but
overlook the privacy issues in RFID-based motion sensing. In
this work, we fill this gap by proposing RF NOID to protect
human motion privacy efficiently.
B. Privacy-preserving RF Human Sensing
Apart from RFID signals, other wireless signals, like WiFi
and FMCW radar, also face privacy leakage issues. Re-
searchers are intensively investigating potential attacks on
existing RF sensing systems. On the one hand, WiFi and
radar signals are exploited as adversarial sensors to directly
detect through-wall human presence [39, 40], habitual be-
haviors [41], and posture tracking [42]. On the other hand,
physical adversarial attacks are conducted to destroy the
original sensing functions by jamming the RF signal [43, 44].
Thereby, countermeasures are developed to safeguard RF-
based human sensing systems. Secur-Fi [45] actively controls
the WiFi transmitting antenna to jam the signal perceived by
the adversary. IRShield [22] and RIStealth [23] employ the
metasurface to passively overwhelm or hide the human motion
WiFi signal in the temporal domain. RF-Protect [21] designs
a radar signal reflector to generate fake motion patterns.
However, existing WiFi and radar solutions [21–23] that are
designed for obfuscating WiFi signal amplitude and radar fre-
quency cannot be directly borrowed by RFID sensing systems
in which the sensitive signal phase is mostly employed for
motion sensing. Besides, the metasurface strategies in [22, 23]
merely focus on masking the temporal human signal pattern.
Instead, RF NOID develops a dedicated signal phase obfusca-
tion strategy for both temporal and spectral domains.
VIII. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we introduce RF NOID, the first system
designed to protect human motion privacy from adversarial
through-wall RFID sensing. Central to our solution is using
the metasurface to obfuscate the channel to protect the motion
information. We envision that RF NOID will raise the bar for
privacy protection against through-wall sensing systems and
anticipate its integration into future smart surfaces to safeguard
privacy across various RF sensing modalities.
