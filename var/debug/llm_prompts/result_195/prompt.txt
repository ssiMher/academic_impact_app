1 INTRODUCTION
In recent years, with the rapid development of Internet of Things (IoT) sensing technologies, sound eavesdropping 
in audiovisual environments has become a major security concern due to the non-encrypted characteristic 
of sound, which makes it vulnerable for attackers to acquire sensitive information, such as personal privacy, 
business secrets, etc. Lessons learned from various sound eavesdropping attacks [13, 18, 42, 45] have prompted 
individuals to converse in anti-eavesdropping environments, such as soundproof rooms or locations with anti-
recording devices [8]. Hence, side-channel attack methods for sound eavesdropping become increasingly attractive. 
Existing side-channel sound eavesdropping works mainly focus on sensing sound-induced physical vibration 
(e.g., speaker/microphone vibration, surrounding surface vibration, etc.) in audiovisual environments through 
motion sensors [9, 32, 50] or RF signals, such as Wi-Fi [57] and mmWave [5, 31, 55], etc., for sound content 
recovery. However, these methods may be ineffective in noisy environments, as background noise could induce 
simultaneous physical vibration, leading to significant distortion in the recovered sound.
As one of the most common and essential accessories, audio cables are extensively used in different audiovisual 
applications, such as personal audio listening, conference calls, online commercial meetings, etc., for sound 
signal transmission. When sound signals are received/produced by a microphone/speaker, they are transmitted 
within audio cables in the form of current signals. Hence, it introduces the potential risk of eavesdropping on 
sound transmitted via these cables once capturing these current signals. Recent work [67] has demonstrated 
the capability to capture voltage changes by embedding a customized retroreflector into  earphone wires for 
sound eavesdropping. This method, however, requires physically damaging wires and embedding a customized 
retroreflector into the wires, significantly limiting its practicality in  real-world applications. Hence, it  remains 
difficult to directly capture current or voltage changes in audio cables using Commercial Off-The-Shelf (COTS) 
devices for sound eavesdropping.
From a different perspective, we observe that there exists electromagnetic leakage around audio cables when 
sound signals are transmitted via the audio cables in form of current signals. The leaked electromagnetic variation 
are correlated with the current signals being transmitted [10, 17], which potentially reveals the transmitted 
sound content. Hence, it is promising to capture these distinct signal patterns, i.e., electromagnetic variation, for 
achieving more natural sound eavesdropping.
Nowadays, Radio-frequency identification (RFID) technologies have been developed rapidly. Given that RFID 
tags have the characteristics of electromagnetic sensitivity[17], flexibility, small size, and low cost, they are suited 
for attachment onto common audio cables to capture electromagnetic variation patterns around audio cables for 
sound eavesdropping. In various audiovisual scenarios, e.g., meeting rooms, offices, command centers, etc., audio 
cables are commonly used to connect electronic equipments for sound signal transmission. When eavesdropped 
users are absent temporarily, attackers are able to secretly attach a small and battery-free RFID tag to an audio 
cable used for sound transmission, which is difficult to be noticed by the eavesdropped users in the usage process 
of the audio cable. Furthermore, owing to the outstanding penetration ability of RFID signals, a compact RFID 
reader and antennas can be camouflaged as decorations or public facilities, such as flower pots, trash bins, etc., 
placed in or outside rooms to transmit and receive RF signals for achieving secret sound eavesdropping. Hence, 
we consider attaching a battery-free RFID tag on an audio cable to capture the electromagnetic variation patterns 
around the audio cable for sound eavesdropping.
However, to eavesdrop on sound transmitted via audio cables using RF signals, we face several challenges in 
practice. First, the eavesdropping scheme should be able to capture electromagnetic variation patterns around 
audio cables accurately by using a battery-free RFID tag attached to the audio cables. Second, the eavesdropping 
scheme should be able to enhance the amplitude of RF signals and remove environmental noise, enabling effective 
sound eavesdropping under various signal attenuation scenarios. Third, the eavesdropping scheme should be 
able to reconstruct sound signals from RF signals reliably for sound content recovery.

EMTap: Eavesdropping on Sound Transmitted via Audio Cables by Sensing Electromagnetic Variation Using RFID • 74:3
In this paper, we first explore the feasibility of sound eavesdropping by modeling electromagnetic variation
around audio cables upon RF signals, and find that the electromagnetic variation patterns present unique
characteristics for different sound content transmitted via audio cables. Motivated by the observations, we
propose a sound eavesdropping system, EMTap, which senses electromagnetic variation around audio cables
leveraging an RFID tag secretly attached to an audio cable for sound content recovery. InEMTap, when sound
signals are transmitted via an audio cable attached an RFID tag, the RFID tag is first activated by RF signals
sent by a camouflaged signal-transmitting antenna, and then EMTap receives RF signals back-scattered by the
RFID tag. To sense electromagnetic variation effectively in various signal attenuation scenarios, EMTap removes
electromagnetic interference from surrounding electronic devices employing an Adaptive Electromagnetic
Interferences Removal (AEIR) algorithm, and further enhances the amplitude of received RF signals using a
designed Taylor Decomposition-based signal Adaptive Enhancement (TDAE) algorithm. After that, EMTap
obtains RF signal spectrograms corresponding to the electromagnetic variation patterns around audio cables,
and further trains a mapping relationship model between RF signal and sound spectrograms by building a
cross-modal Generative model, Nonlinear Stable Diffusion Model (NSDM), for reconstructing sound spectrograms
from RF signal spectrograms. Subsequently, EMTap converts reconstructed sound spectrograms to time-domain
sound waveforms leveraging Griffin-Lim algorithm and removes reconstruction distortion in time-domain sound
waveforms using an adaptive waveform filter. Finally, the time-domain sound waveforms are converted to sound
content through sound recognition API for achieving sound eavesdropping. Experiments in real environments
show that EMTap eavesdrops on sound transmitted via audio cables effectively.
We highlight our main contributions as follows:
•We present a side-channel sound eavesdropping system, which captures electromagnetic variation patterns
around an audio cable utilizing a small and battery-free RFID tag attached to the audio cable for sound
content recovery.
•We design an AEIR and a TDAE algorithm to remove electromagnetic interference from nearby devices and
enhance RF signal amplitude, respectively, for capturing fine-grained electromagnetic variation patterns
accurately in various signal attenuation scenarios.
•We build a cross-modal Generative model, NSDM, to reconstruct sound spectrograms from RF signal
spectrograms, and further convert sound spectrograms to sound waveforms for sound content recovery.
•We conduct extensive experiments in real environments, and the results show that EMTap achieves an
average Mel-Cepstral Distortion (MCD) of 5.49 and Word Error Rate (WER) of 16.19% for eavesdropping on
sound transmitted via audio cables.

3.1 Background
Audio cables are widely used in both professional and consumer audio applications for transmitting sound
signals in audiovisual scenarios. According to the types of cable connectors, existing audio cables can be broadly
categorized into three types: TRS, RCA, and XLR cables, as shown in Fig. 1.
In many professional settings, such as meeting rooms and live audio systems, TRS, RCA, and XLR audio
cables are widely employed for dedicated audio signal transmission due to their superior sound fidelity and
reliability. These three types of audio cables are commonly found in scenarios involving audio equipment,
primarily facilitating the connection between various electronic devices. Specifically, TRS audio cables are widely
utilized to connect personal computers (PCs)/smartphones and loudspeakers/microphones(Mics), which are
essential for transmitting audio in various online applications; RCA cables are prevalent for transmitting stereo
sound in audiovisual systems, which are commonly used to connect TVs/DVD and loudspeakers/amplifiers/Mics;
XLR cables are usually used in live sound and studio environments, and the ability to provide high-quality audio
transmission makes them ideal for connecting audio mixers and loudspeakers/Mics/E-guitars. In real audiovisual
scenarios, such as online commercial meetings, conference calls, etc., different types of audio cables are chosen
according to practical requirements for transmitting sound signals.
The process of transmitting sound signals (speaker-produced or microphone-received sound) via an audio
cable consists of the following steps. First, sound content, typically in the form of sound waves, is converted into
current signals by a transducer (e.g., a microphone). Then, the current signals are transmitted via audio cables
for delivery to various audio processing devices. Finally, the receiving end (e.g., a loudspeaker or an amplifier)
receives and processes the current signals to obtain transmitted sound content.

3.3 Feasibility Study of Eavesdropping on Sound Using RF Signals
Based on the constructed electromagnetic variation model upon RF signals, we consider using an RFID tag 
attached to an audio cable to capture the electromagnetic variation patterns around the audio cable for sound 
eavesdropping.
To explore the feasibility of sound eavesdropping by capturing electromagnetic variation patterns around 
audio cables using RF signals, we conduct experiments to analyze spectrograms of RF signals corresponding to 
some common words transmitted via audio cables. Since the third-order harmonic signals achieve a trade-off 
between sensing sensitivity and signal intensity [53], we choose the USRP N210 reader to monitor the third-order 
harmonic RF signals. As shown in Fig. 2, to avoid electromagnetic interference from other electronic devices, such 
as loudspeakers, keyboards, etc., we conduct experiments in a controlled environment. Specifically, electronic 
devices that may emit electromagnetic waves, e.g., computers, loudspeakers, etc., are stored in a metal box to 
avoid electromagnetic leakage [28]. An RFID tag is attached to an audio cable, and RFID antennas are placed 
around for capturing electromagnetic variation induced by sound transmitted via the audio cable. This method 
allows us to only sense electromagnetic variation induced by current transmitted via audio cables without being 
affected by surrounding electromagnetic interference.
Fig. 3 shows the spectrograms of RF signal phase corresponding to three audio cables, e.g., TRS, RCA, and 
XLR cable, when they transmit sound "thank you very much" across multiple tests. It can be observed from the 
figures that there are obvious differences between spectrograms of different words. We further calculate structural 
similarity [25] of spectrograms corresponding to these common words. The results show that structural similarity 
of different words’ spectrograms are all less than 0.5 and that of the same word’s spectrograms are all larger than 
0.8 for different audio cables across multiple tests. Hence, different words can be effectively distinguished based 
on spectrograms of RF signals induced by electromagnetic variation around audio cables.
The above encouraging results demonstrate the potential of eavesdropping on sound transmitted via audio 
cables by capturing electromagnetic variation patterns around audio cables using RF signals.

6.1 Normalizing Audio Cable Specificity in RF Signal Spectrograms
Usually, audio cables with various connectors (e.g., TRS, RCA, XLR cables, etc.) are used to connect different
devices for sound signal transmission. Despite differences in connectors, these cables essentially transmit sound

resistance, etc.) in cables used for current signal transmission. These differences result in varying electromagnetic
variation around audio cables when transmitting the same sound signals via cables with different physical
characteristics [16]. To ensure that a sound eavesdropping model is effective across various audio cables, it is
necessary to normalize cable specificity in RF signal spectrograms before constructing sound eavesdropping
model.

6.2 Training a Mapping Relationship Model Through NSDM Model
Based on normalized RF signal spectrograms, a mapping relationship model between RF signal and sound
spectrograms is required for reconstructing sound spectrograms from RF signal spectrograms. Benefiting from
the advanced capabilities of spectrogram encoding and decoding, Generative Model enable powerful cross-modal
sound spectrogram generation. Based on Generative model[47], we build a Stable Diffusion Model (SDM) to train
a mapping relationship model between RF signal spectrograms and sound spectrograms.
A straightforward method involves inputting RF signal spectrograms of certain sound content into SDM model
for reconstructing sound spectrograms directly. However, it encounters inherent disruption due to the nonlinear
effects of integrated circuit components in RFID tags when capturing electromagnetic variation patterns by RF
signals. Nonlinear effects are due to the non-semiconductor properties of RFID tag circuit components, such as
chips and antennas, etc. (as shown in Fig. 9 (a)), which introduces RF signals in a manner that is not proportional
to current signals. To explore the nonlinear effects of RFID tags, we transmit sinusoidal sound signals (i.e., current
signals) via an audio cable, and calculate the response curve of RF signals and current signals. The results are
presented in Fig. 9 (b). We can observe that there exist obvious non-sensitive area where response of RF signals
becomes relatively insensitive as the amplitude of current signals increases. Hence, nonlinear effects introduce
disruptions in the transduction process when capturing electromagnetic variation through RF signals.
To eliminate the transducing disruption, we add a Nonlinear SubNet network to SDM model for obtaining
a Nonlinear Stable Diffusion Model (NSDM) model. Fig. 10 illustrates the NSDM architecture, which consists
of four parts, i.e., nonlinear SubNet network, Encoder, Stable diffusion model, and Decoder. Specifically, the
SubNet network contains Convolution Layers, Nonlinear layers, Batch Normalization (BN) Layers, and Dropout
Layers. The Convolution, BN, and Dropout Layers are utilized to extract RF signal spectrogram features, increase
training stability, and prevent overfitting, respectively. In the Nonlinear layers, we design an adaptive activation
function to correct nonlinear effects of RFID tags, which is represented as 𝑓(𝑥)= 𝑥/(𝛼+|𝑥|), where 𝛼is adaptive
parameters. Based on the nonlinear SubNet network, EMTap obtains corrected RF signal spectrogram features 𝑟.
Subsequently, 𝑟 is input to NSDM model for reconstructing sound spectrograms from RF signal spectrograms.

Usually, it is inevitable to occur conversion distortion during reconstructing sound spectrograms from RF 
signal spectrograms due to network parameter limitation, which brings noise in converted sound waveforms. 
This noise affects the performance of recovering sound content. Thus, we design an adaptive waveform filter 
to remove the noise. Specifically, EMTap first segments a sound waveform into equal episodes. Then, for each 
episode, EMTap estimates power spectral densities of signal and noise by analyzing energy distribution within 
each segment. Based on the power spectral densities, Wiener coefficients [58] are calculated to adjust the signal’s 
frequency for filtering the segment. After that, EMTap recombines all filtered signal segments into a continuous 
time-domain sound waveform, and calculates SNR of the waveform. If the SNR falls below a predefined threshold, 
EMTap re-estimates the power spectral densities and adjusts filter coefficients. This process is repeated until the 
SNR of filtered sound waveform exceeds the threshold.
To validate the effectiveness of adaptive waveform filter for removing noise in converted time-domain sound 
waveforms, we conduct experiments to compare the sound waveforms with/without adaptive waveform filter, 
and the results are shown in Fig. 11. From the figure, we can observe that the time-domain sound waveforms 
are more smooth and stable after using the adaptive waveform filter, which demonstrates the effectiveness of 
proposed adaptive waveform filter for removing noise in converted time-domain sound waveforms.
Based on above waveform filter, EMTap obtains more smooth and stable time-domain sound waveforms, which 
can be used for sound content recovering. Finally, a Sound-to-Text API [26] is employed to convert time-domain 
sound waveforms into sound content.

9 COUNTERMEASURE, DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
Countermeasure. One potential defense against EMTap is to use electromagnetic shielding materials like metal 
foil or wire mesh around audio cables, which suppresses electromagnetic signal propagation. Besides, since sound 
reconstruction of EMTap relies on analyzing electromagnetic variations of the raw analog signal, encryption 
strategies in audio transmission effectively prevents sound eavesdropping attacks. Hence, in security-sensitive 
scenarios, implementing end-to-end encryption strategies (such as frequency band inversion, time-domain 
permutation, or carrier modulation) could effectively prevent sound eavesdropping attacks of EMTap. Moreover, 
electromagnetic interference devices, such as RF devices working at the same frequency as EMTap, could cause RF 
signal distortion. Hence, in places with high-security requirements, such as military missions, trade negotiations, 
etc., electromagnetic interference devices can be placed around to defend EMTap.
Discussion. (1) Eavesdropping on untrained words. EMTap is able to eavesdrop on sound containing not only 
trained words but also untrained words. Existing works [31, 32] have demonstrated the ability to eavesdrop 
on sounds with untrained words using generative methods [33, 41]. Similarly, EMTap enables the recovery 
of untrained words by utilizing recent generative models. (2) Potential ubiquitous sensing and computing

applications. In addition to primary application in sound eavesdropping attacks of audio cables, EMTap also 
demonstrates significant potential for ubiquitous sensing and computing applications. On one hand, the employed 
technique of EMTap can be used for non-invasive cable monitoring. For example, the electromagnetic sensing 
capacity of EMTap enables non-invasive fault detection of data transmission lines. On the other hand, the signal 
processing pipeline of EMTap could be used for abnormal load current monitoring of power cables in smart grid.
Limitations. (1) Digital audio cable. EMTap is capable of eavesdropping on sound transmitted via analog 
audio cables, as these audio cables carry sound signals in the form of continuous electrical currents. However, 
for digital audio cables, such as optical audio cables [2], EMTap lacks a Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC), and 
therefore cannot decode the digital sound signals for sound eavesdropping. Currently, analog audio cables still 
dominate the main audio cable market, making EMTap effective in real scenarios.
(2) Physical access requirements. EMTap requires temporary physical access to attach RFID tags on the target 
audio cable to carry out sound eavesdropping attacks. In addition, the deployment of this technology depends 
on the attacker gaining temporary access when the victim is absent. Usually, practical deployment of EMTap 
remains achievable in real-world scenarios. First, the small size of commercial RFID tags (as tiny as 2mm×3mm) 
makes them difficult to be noticed when attached to  common audio cables. Moreover, in  common audiovisual 
scenarios like meeting rooms, attackers could exploit temporary absences of victims to deploy RFID tags without 
being noticed. These practical considerations indicate that, although physical access is technically necessary, 
EMTap remains a feasible and worrying sound eavesdropping attack method.
(3) Encryption vulnerability. Since sound reconstruction of EMTap relies on analyzing electromagnetic varia-
tions of raw analog signal, encryption strategies in audio transmission effectively prevents sound eavesdropping 
attacks. Hence, in security-sensitive scenarios, implementing end-to-end encryption strategies could effectively 
prevent sound eavesdropping attacks of EMTap. However, such protection mechanisms are not universally 
implemented in common audiovisual scenarios. In many commercial and personal audio applications, particularly 
in conference rooms and personal audiovisual applications, audio signals are usually transmitted unencrypted 
through standard audio cables. Hence, EMTap remains a significant threat to these widespread audio cables.
10 RELATED WORKS
In this section, we review works related to EMTap.
RFID-based sensing. Recently, due to the advantages of low cost and deployment flexibility, RFID tags 
are widely used in various wireless sensing applications. Existing works mainly capture traditional patterns, 
such as signal changes due to multi-path effect or mechanical vibrations, for achieving lo calization[59], user 
authentication[14], and vibration sensing[36, 60, 64, 66], etc. However, no existing works capture electromagnetic 
variation patterns to achieve RFID-based sensing.
Eavesdropping through side-channel leakage from cables. Some recent works [19, 44, 48, 52, 54] utilize 
various sensors to capture side-channel leakage from non-audio cables for eavesdropping on communicated data, 
such as MAC address [48], digital keystrokes[44, 52, 54], and electrical usage habits [19]. However, these works 
can only eavesdrop on wire-transmitted binary digital signals, and cannot capture common analog sound signals. 
Additionally, work [42] utilizes customed equipment up to 5m wrapped around undersea communication cables 
to sense signal spillover around the cables for eavesdropping on military intelligence transmitted via the cables. 
However, the bulkiness and high cost of this equipment significantly limit its practicality in space-constrained 
audiovisual scenarios. Recently, RFParrot [67] employs a customized retroreflector embedded into earphone wires 
for eavesdropping on sound transmitted via these wires. However, this method requires damaging wires and 
embedding a customized retroreflector, which adds both cost and complexity, potentially reducing its feasibility 
in real application scenarios.
Sound eavesdropping through dynamic sensing. The rapid development of the IoT and cloud computing 
brings many applications [35, 39, 61, 68, 69]. Among them, side-channel sound eavesdropping studies are

attracting growing research interest. Existing side-channel sound eavesdropping methods mainly sense sound-
induced vibration (e.g., speaker/microphone vibration, nearby surface vibration, etc.) for sound content recovery. 
Researchers use various sensors, such as accelerator [4, 32], gyroscope [40], Wi-Fi [57], mmWave [5, 30, 31, 55], and

in recovered sound. Besides, work [38] uses a customized coil to sense magnetic leakage from micro-speakers and 
work [11] leverages a customized EMR sensor to capture magnetic leakage from mobile devices’ audio amplifiers 
for recovering speaker-produced sound, but such magnetic leakage does not exist around audio cables. From a 
different perspective, EMTap senses electromagnetic variation around audio cables for sound eavesdropping.
Different from existing works, EMTap eavesdrops on sound transmitted via audio cables by sensing electro-
magnetic variation around audio cables in a low-cost and natural manner.
11 CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose EMTap, which achieves sound eavesdropping by sensing electromagnetic variation 
around an audio cable leveraging a small and battery-free RFID tag secretly attached to the cable. EMTap first 
designs an AEIR algorithm and a TDAE algorithm to remove electromagnetic interference from nearby electronic 
devices and enhance the amplitude of RF signals, respectively, for capturing electromagnetic variation patterns 
accurately in various signal attenuation scenarios. Then, a cross-modal Generative model, NSDM, is built to train 
a mapping relationship model between RF signal and sound spectrograms for reconstructing sound spectrograms 
from RF signal spectrograms. Afterward, EMTap employs Griffin-Lim algorithm to convert sound spectrograms 
to sound waveforms, which can be recovered by sound recognition API to sound content. Experiments in real 
scenarios demonstrate the effectiveness of EMTap for eavesdropping on sound transmitted via audio cables.
