Abstract—As an advanced technology for non-contact sensing,
mmWave radar enables ﬁne-grained measurement of a wide variety
of user behaviors. While creating intelligence and convenience, it
also concerns behavioral privacy and security, as radar signals
contain a wealth of behavioral information. Existing solutions are
either incapable of customizable privacy protections or cannot cope
with multi-person scenarios. This paper presents a M ulti-user be-
havioral privacy Filter , MuFilter, a data masking system centered
on the idea of dimensional signal interference. It determines the
sensing signatures that need to be preserved or interfered with
based on the sensing services that users want to enable and disable,
thereby making targeted tampering on the radar signal. On this
basis, we introduce the multi-person tracking technology to allow
MuFilter to determine the number of users in unknown scenarios.
Moreover, a subspace tampering technique is proposed to ensure
that each tampering only affects the target user and not other
users, thus supporting personalized privacy protection for multiple
users. Experiments show that MuFilter can interfere with targeted
behavioral signatures with a 100% success rate, while the degree
of impact on other users’ signatures ranges from 0% to 3.85%.

to isolate their signals. MuFilter adopts the point target tracking
framework based on spatial point clouds. The Range-Azimuth

(MIMO) array signals is clustered to detect human targets. User
trajectories and new clusters will be correlated and updated
between frames using the Hungarian algorithm and Kalman
ﬁltering. We also use a state mechanism for trajectory manage-
ment. The obtained user positions will contribute to multi-user
signal isolation and targeted tampering. (2) Subspace signal

separation capability of mmWave radar. We use Range-Angle

the tampered subspaces will then be restored to their original

format by full-space Angle-Range IFFT. We also solve collision

tampering for one user is prevented from affecting other users.

behavioral signatures with a 100% success rate, while its impact

B. Sensing Applications and Signatures
This paper involves ﬁve commonly used human sensing ap-
plications. While introducing them, we focus on the sensing
signatures they rely on and the ways in which these signatures
are obtained, which are depicted in Fig. 2. The sensing sig-
natures contain essential information for their corresponding
applications, and their illustrations will intuitively demonstrate
the interference effects of the MuFilter system.
Tracking: Tracking is a crucial application and serves as the
foundation for other sensing applications. The resulting trajec-
tory from tracking contains the user’s position at any moment,
including two signature dimensions: range and azimuth. There-
fore, its sensing signature can be represented by the RA Map
(RAM). For the range dimension, an FFT is usually conducted
to the IF signal to separate reﬂective objects, which is known
as Range FFT. For the angle dimension, direction of arrival
(DoA) estimation is applied to obtain the azimuth of the user.
The most commonly used DoA estimation method is to perform
FFT on the antenna array signal, which is called Angle FFT.
Another method is Capon beamforming which minimizes the
variance distortion response to enhance the desired signal while
minimizing interference and noise. Capon beamforming has
higher resolution and stronger anti-noise capabilities. Therefore,
although our MuFilter system can act on any DoA estimation
algorithm, for the sake of writing simplicity and demonstration
purposes, the sensing applications in this paper will solely utilize
Capon beamforming and showcase its results.
Posture Estimation: The user’s posture estimation in 3D
space, including skeleton [4] and mesh [5], can vividly depict the
human body shape. The posture involves horizontal and vertical
information in 3D space, therefore the Elevation-Azimuth Map
(EAM) can be used as the sensing signature for posture esti-
mation. 2D DoA estimation is needed to obtain information in
the two angular dimensions. Similar to 1D DoA estimation, 2D
Angle FFT and 2D Capon beamforming are the most commonly
used 2D DoA estimation methods.
In-air Writing:As one of the typical applications of human-
computer interaction, in-air writing tracks the user’s hand move-
ment and converts it into text or graphic input, which can be ap-
plied to scenarios such as virtual reality and in-air whiteboards.
Regardless of the speciﬁc method of in-air writing, it must detect
the position of the hand in 3D space. Both in-air writing and
posture estimation require azimuth and elevation information.
The difference lies in the observation targets: in-air writing fo-
cuses solely on the hand, while posture estimation encompasses
the entire body. Therefore, based on practical needs, we make
the following reasonable preconditions: a user who is unwilling
to expose their hand trajectory is also unwilling to reveal their
posture information. Conversely, if a user is willing to provide
their posture information, they would also be willing to perform
in-air writing detection.
Activity Recognition:The user’s behavior and intentions can
be inferred through recognizing and analyzing user’s motion ac-
tivities. Activity recognition is usually accomplished by training
neural networks using micro-Doppler spectrum [13]. The phase
change of the chirp signal represents the displacement of the
reﬂector and can be used to measure instantaneous velocity. By
conducting FFT on signals from multiple chirps within a single
frame, which is known as Doppler FFT, the velocity spectrum
for this frame is obtained. After performing Doppler FFT across
multiple frames and stitching the velocity spectrum together,
the micro-Doppler spectrum is generated, which contains rich
velocity information of the user’s motion.
Respiration Monitoring:We can gain insights into the user’s
health status and perform exercise management through res-
piration monitoring, which plays a signiﬁcant role in elder
healthcare and ﬁtness domains. When a person breathes, there
is a tiny periodic displacement in the chest and abdomen which
can be measured by analyzing the phase change of the signal.
Therefore, the respiration curve can be regarded as the sensing
signature. The ﬁrst step to obtain the respiration curve is to
locate the torso containing the chest or abdomen. Next, mit-
igate the effects of direct current component (DC) and jitter.
Finally, the phase is extracted and converted into the respiration
curve.

To achieve personalized privacy protection in multi-user sce-
narios, we propose the MuFilter system, the overview of which
is shown in Fig. 3. The mmWave radar collects users’ reﬂected
signals and outputs digital IF signals, which will be passed to the
hardware or software system equipped with MuFilter. MuFilter

tory through a multi-target tracking process involving Kalman
ﬁltering and the Hungarian algorithm. Next, MuFilter performs
Range FFT and Angle FFT on the raw radar data to obtain a
reversible RA signal matrix. We map the user position obtained
by tracking into the RA signal matrix, and expand the users’ RA

This section demonstrates customizable behavioral privacy
ﬁlter for ﬁve sensing applications in the single-user scenario. We
begin with an elaboration of the core concepts of dimensional
data interference and signal reversion, and then introduce and

A. Core Concept
In general, different sensing applications rely on different
signature dimensions, which makes it possible to implement
customizable privacy protection. The basic idea is to interfere
with the signature dimensions on which the disabled application
depends, so as to protect speciﬁc behavioral information without
affecting other sensing applications, which is called dimensional
data interference. The implementation of this idea is based on
another important concept, signal reversion, which utilizes the
invertibility of FFT. That is, data can be converted from “time
domain” to “frequency domain” by FFT, or from “frequency
domain” back to “time domain” by IFFT.
As shown in Fig. 4, the radar data has ﬁve data dimensions,
three of which are temporal (ADC sample, chirp, and frame) and
two of which are spatial (horizontal and vertical antennas). After
FFT, these data dimensions are transformed into range, angle,
short-time, and long-time signature dimensions for subsequent
sensing applications. We are then able to eliminate or change
the corresponding behavioral information contained in the radar
data by performing data tampering in speciﬁc signature dimen-
sions. Finally, with IFFT, the radar data is converted from the
signature dimensions back to the data dimensions, its original
data format. In this way, we achieve targeted data masking while

Position Reset: Position information mainly contains two
dimensions, range and azimuth. In the hiding of the position,
the integrity and continuity of the user’s signal need to be
maintained, as the position information is the basis for other
sensing applications and guides the subsequent signal extraction.
Therefore, the Position Reset (PR) is proposed. It ﬁrst performs
Range FFT and then 1D Angle FFT with the number of FFT
points as the number of horizontal array elements. After deter-

ADC sampled signals. In this way, the user’s real position is
eliminated, but the signal near the fake position retains the user’s
full local temporal and spatial information, thus other functions
such as activity recognition and respiration monitoring can be
conducted normally.

frequency domain, preventing the user’s instantaneous velocity
information from being captured. In addition to this method,
ﬁne-grained data modiﬁcations can be performed on velocity
bins and then convert velocity bins back to chirp signals by
Doppler IFFT.
Inter-frame Phase Fluctuation:Since frames are the basic unit
of radar signal processing, they can be considered as a long-time
dimension independent of the chirp dimension. Frames carry
the objective temporal logic. We cannot simply reorder it like
we do with chirps, as this will cause confusion in all sensing
applications. Fortunately, for complex signals in one frame, su-
perimposing a phase on them does not affect the signal process-
ing result, i.e., F(ejφS)= F(S), where F represents the signal
processing operations and φ is the superimposed phase. When
a random phase is superimposed on each frame, the inter-frame
phase correlation is erased without affecting the intra-frame
signal processing, which is called Inter-Frame Phase Fluctuation
(IFPF). This method can interfere with applications that rely on
inter-frame phase coupling such as respiration monitoring. As
shown in Fig. 5(Column 5), the disruption of phase continuity
simultaneously disrupts the continuity of the respiration curve,
therefore the sensing result is a meaningless noise curve.
The above methods have been proven to be independent and
compatible [12]. Once the attacker acquires the tampered raw
signal and performs FFT on it, they will obtain results that
have been manipulated by MuFilter. The attacker is unaware
of the composition, sequence, and parameters of the tamper-
ing methods, and lacks access to process variables involved
during tampering. Consequently, the attacker cannot recover
the original information, even if they recognize that the data
has been altered. Brute-force cracking is also difﬁcult to re-
store data for the following reasons. (1) The correctness of
the cracking attempt cannot be veriﬁed. The proposed privacy
ﬁlter is not an encryption system with a clear password, but
an entropy-increasing process for the information contained in
radar data. Each cracking attempt yields a seemingly correct
answer, but it is uncertain which answer is true. For example,
the PR moves the user’s RA bins from the real position to a
fake position. Reverse attempts to the true position always yield
reasonable results because RA bins can be moved from any
position to the false position. Similarly, based on the phase
noise sequence caused by the IFPF, an arbitrary respiration
curve can be generated by resetting the phase, but it is uncertain
which one is the user’s respiration curve. Therefore, the inherent
nature of the privacy ﬁlter makes brute-force cracking lose
the critical attempt feedback. (2) The probability of a single
attempt being correct is extremely low. Attackers obtain as many
sensing results as cracking attempts. Therefore, due to the lack of
feedback on cracking attempts, attackers have only one chance
Fig. 7. Multi-person tracking workﬂow.
to guess the privacy ﬁlter’s settings, and the probability of this
guess being correct is very low. For example, the probability
of correctly guessing the real order of 128 chirps is as small as
1/128! ≈ 2.6×10−216. Note that this is only the probability of
cracking the single CD technique. Considering the combinations
of multiple tampering techniques, the probability of restoring
the entire privacy ﬁltering process will be even much smaller
than 2.6×10−216. Thus, attackers are almost unable to correctly
restore the tampered signal in a single attempt.
V. A CHIEVING MULTI-USER PERSONALIZATION
In this section, we extend the customizable behavioral privacy
ﬁlter to multi-user scenarios. Undoubtedly, the greatest difﬁculty
stems from the spreading effect of signal interference, thus
requiring us to effectively isolate each user’s signal. Our solution
utilizes the spatial separation capability of radar. MuFilter ﬁrst
performs multi-person tracking to obtain user positions. Then,
extract spatial bins near each user to provide further spatial
boundaries for dimensional data interference. Ultimately, the
data tampering is performed only within the subspaces, thus
preventing cross-interference between users.
A. Multi-Person Tracking
Beneﬁting from the development of mmWave MIMO technol-
ogy, mmWave radars nowadays have excellent enough angular
resolution. From standard steps of Range FFT, 1D DoA estima-
tion, and 2D Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) we can obtain
sufﬁcient RA point cloud. Therefore, we adopt the point target
tracking scheme based on RA point cloud. One representative
of this tracking approach is PmTrack [3], the basic workﬂow
of which is shown in Fig. 7. In each frame, the point cloud
is clustered by the DBSCAN algorithm and the centroid of
each cluster will be obtained. Then, we compute the euclidean
distances between existing trajectories and centroids, and input
them as the cost matrix into the Hungarian algorithm to obtain
the optimized trajectory-centroid association for trajectory up-
dating. Moreover, the Kalman ﬁltering of constant velocity mode
is introduced for trajectory prediction and correction, thereby
updating trajectories when they fail to associate and obtaining
more accurate results. The trajectory states are categorized into
four types. The initial state is candidate, which is converted
to active after successive successful associations. After that,
the state transitions between active and missing depending on
the association result. Trajectories with continuous association

B. Subspace Tampering
Next, we take advantage of the radar’s spatial separation
capabilities to isolate each user’s data tampering. The spatial
separation capability is reﬂected in two aspects. First, based
on the frequency of the IF signal and the phase vector of the
MIMO array, the space can be separated into RA bins by Range
FFT and Angle FFT. Second, the energy leakage of the FFT
result “spectrum” is limited and acceptable. In other words, the
energy of the human reﬂected signal is concentrated in the RA
bins where they are located. Thus, we can isolate each user’s
signal through RA domain signal extraction, which includes
three steps. (1) Perform Range FFT and Angle FFT to obtain
RA bins. Note that to ensure the signal can be reversed back to
the original signal format, the number of FFT points is the ADC
sample number and the array element number, respectively. To
Fig. 9. Position mapping and subspace extraction.
maintain the ﬁdelity and stability of the reversed signal, no
windowing or static clutter ﬁltering is performed. (2) Generate
subspace through RA bin focus. Take user 1 in Fig. 9 as an
example. First, calculate its distance r1 and azimuth θ1 relative
to the radar based on its position (x1,y1). Then, ﬁnd the RA bin
where (r1,θ1)is located, and use it as the center bin to spread a
reasonable number of bins to serve as the subspace for the user.
(3) Perform trampering in each user’s subspace. The tampered
subspaces will return and form the complete RA domain, and
be inverted back to the raw signal format through Angle IFFT
and Range IFFT.

C. Anti-Collision Mechanism
In the deployment of MuFilter, position collisions may occur
in the subspaces of multiple users, which can be categorized
into the three types shown in Fig. 10. (1) User overlap collision.
When multiple users are close, their subspaces may overlap.
We use the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) algorithm to
cluster the union of RA bins in multiple subspaces to obtain
the boundary between subspaces and maintain their isolation.
(2) Self-collision of position reset. The PR methods that modify
position information can also cause subspace collision. For the
user itself, the original subspace and destination subspace may
overlap, making it infeasible to exchange the two subspaces to
complete PR. In this case, MuFilter ﬁlls the non-overlapping
bins of the original space with the non-overlapping bins of
the destination subspace. This process should minimize the
abruptness caused by the ﬁlling, thus we adopt the rotation
method shown in Fig. 10 to avoid RA bin confusion during
the exchange process. (3) Mutual-collision of position reset.
PR’s destination subspace may also overlap with other users’
subspaces. At this moment, we must change the destination to
avoid the collision. Considering that there are usually far more
range bins than azimuth bins, we suggest sliding the subspace
in the range dimension to determine new destinations. In prac-
tice, it is better to set a less-passed position as the destination
for PR.
In addition to position-related collisions, we observe that
MuFilter’s local tampering can have a slight impact on high-
resolution DoA estimation algorithms such as Conventional
Beamforming and Capon. This is because tampering with only
part of the azimuth bins affects the covariance matrix of the
array signal to some extent. In contrast, if the tampered azimuth
bins cover the full dimension, the signal maintains integrity and
continuity in the azimuth dimension, and thus does not change
the covariance matrix. Therefore, we suggest extending azimuth
bins to the entire azimuth dimension when the subspace does not
overlap in range with other subspaces.

reduce the computation time by 27% to 77%, thus enabling more
efﬁcient service for multiple users.
VII. R ELATED WORK
A. RF-Based Sensing Security and Privacy
The application of radio frequency (RF) technology is be-
coming increasingly widespread, bringing along with it con-
cerns regarding security and privacy. By measuring the subtle
vibrations of objects, mmWave [22], [23], [24] and RFID [25]
technologies can eavesdrop on human speech. Information leak-
age from smartphones and screens poses a signiﬁcant threat
to sensitive personal information such as passwords [26], [27],
[28]. Additionally, leakage of user location information is also
a serious problem. Many studies use through-wall radar to
obtain users’ location [29], [30], leading to the risk of burglary.
Some physical layer attack methods [10], [11], [31] perform
appearing-disappearing spooﬁng through special physical de-
vices, thereby tampering with the target’s existence or location
information. RF-Protect [2] deploys reﬂectors in the environ-
ment to inject dummy signals into the backscattered signal to
deceive potential attackers. Radar 2 [32] allows users to utilize a
single mmWave radar to detect the presence of other mmWave
radars and obtain their locations. However, these works can’t
achieve customizable tampering on signals to meet the user’s
different behavioral privacy needs. mmFilter [12] can protect the
behavioral privacy of single user through customizable tamper-
ing on mmWave signals. However, the signal tampering scheme
of mmFilter is global and thus fails multi-user scenarios. Our
work performs subspace tampering on mmWave signals and
considers the conﬂicts in multi-user scenarios, allowing multiple
users to customize behavioral privacy protection according to
their own needs.
B. mmWave-Based Human Sensing
The continuous development of mmWave technology sup-
ports an increasing number of applications in human sensing.
The high spatial resolution enables precise multi-person track-
ing [3], [33], human imaging [34], skeleton estimation [4], [35],
and mesh reconstruction [5], [36]. Human activity recognition
such as gesture recognition [7], [37], [38] and in-air hand track-
ing [6], [39], [40] is a promising technology in human-computer
interaction. Gait [16], point clouds [17], behavioral patterns [18],
vital signs [19], facial contours [41], voiceprints [42], or with
the help of additional sensors [3], [20], user identity recogni-
tion can be achieved. It involves activity classiﬁcation using
micro-Doppler [8] or point cloud information [43]. Utilizing the
sensitivity to small displacements, mmWave radar can empower
health applications such as breathing and heartbeat monitor-
ing [9], [44], [45]. In recent years, some works propose signal
augmentation methods based on signal simulation to reduce
the burden of collecting training data [13], [46], [47].T h e
widespread use and powerful sensing capabilities of mmWave
make it possible to extract user behavioral privacy by stealing
and analyzing raw signals. Our work proposes signal tampering
schemes applicable to various privacy protection requirements,
effectively addressing privacy concerns caused by the leakage
of radar data.
VIII. C ONCLUSION
This paper studied the behavioral privacy leakage threat of
mmWave radar sensing and presented MuFilter, a data masking
system for multi-user customizable behavioral privacy protec-
tion. MuFilter adopts the idea of dimensional signal interference
and performs targeted signal tampering based on users’ sensing
service needs and privacy protection needs. The introduction of
multi-person tracking technology and the proposal of subspace
tampering technique allow MuFilter to isolate each user’s signal
and prevent data tampering for one user from affecting other
users. Extensive experiments evaluated the performance of Mu-
Filter under various multi-user personalized privacy protection
settings, which shows that it can interfere with targeted behav-
ioral signatures with a 100% success rate, while the degree of
impact on other users’ signatures ranges from 0% to 3.85%.
