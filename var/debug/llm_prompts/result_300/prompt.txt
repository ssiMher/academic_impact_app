ABSTRACT OF THE THESIS
Understanding Privacy Threat for Side-channel Speech Eavesdropping Attack
by
Chunyu Xia
Master of Science in Computer Science
University of California San Diego, 2022
Professor Xinyu Zhang, Chair
Professor Imani N. S. Munyaka, Co-Chair
With the prevalence of voice-driven applications, the related privacy issues became a
popular topic, especially the potential privacy threat for side-channel speech eavesdropping
attack (SSEA). In this thesis, we roughly divide the SSEA into four main tasks, including
Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), Spoken Language Understanding (SLU), Word
Classification (WC) and User Identification (UI). In order to mimic SSEA signals, we
measure the frequency response and noise model from different sensors, and design SSEA
signals generation tools that utilize the large-scale open-sourced speech dataset. We
also evaluate the generated signals with above four tasks to understand SSEA from
x
several significant factors, like sampling rate, frequency response and noise. Our findings
demonstrate that sampling rate is the key factor for SSEA since less than 4kHz SSEA
signals are not suitable for ASR tasks, while frequency response and noise will reduce
performance in separate ways.
xi
INTRODUCTION
Speech has been the most wildly used wireless communication method far early
before the invention of wireless network. Eavesdropping is to secretly steal private
information without users’ awareness, so eavesdropping attacks for speech or conversation
has always been a big concern. In addition, with the development of Artificial Intelligence
(AI) and pervasiveness of mobile devices, Voice User Interfaces (VUIs) like Voice Assistant
(VA) have become a popular research and application field. In the meantime, related
eavesdropping issues have also become more and more severe since eavesdropping attacks
expose threats for privacy leakage between human and machine.
Microphone is the common tools utilized to steal private conversation while there
are several defenses or protection methods. Nonetheless, side-channel speech eavesdropping
attack is a new threat domain where little work has been done. The basic idea of side-
channel speech eavesdropping is that adversaries use channels that are not aware by the
users to capture the side effects of sound waves like vibration, which is generated by sound
sources or caused by nearby sound reflector. Due to the limitation of hardware design,
such attacks are hard to be prevented. Even though such side-channels are not designed as
microphone to record users’ audio, the vibration of loudspeakers also contain huge privacy
information especially when users have conversation with voice assistant.
Loudspeaker and human throat are two main types of sound sources that can
emit human sound, and are two main channel to be attacked by related side-channel
eavesdropping attack. For example, loudspeaker is vulnerable to side-channels like motion
sensors and in-built hardwares which can record the vibration from loudspeakers. Human
1
throat is threatened by high-resolution laser or millimeter wave radar because there will
be movement of human throat when human speaks. Moreover, the location and the type
of sound source will also change the side-channel eavesdropping attack. In this thesis,
we mainly focus on the loudspeaker related side-channel eavesdropping attack, because
side-channel like motion sensors are more feasible to be measured and motion sensors
based side-channel eavesdropping is becoming prevalent in recent years.
Loudspeakers are easily attacked by in-built sensors, which can be called internal
eavesdropping, while human throat is threatened by sensors outside, which can be called
external eavesdropping. In this thesis, we mainly focus on in-built sensors eavesdropping
like motion sensors, and we identify two key factors that make side-channel signals heavily
different from audio signals are sampling rate and frequency response.
Most eavesdropping attacks can be categorized into four different speech tasks:
Automatic speech recognition (ASR), Spoken Language understanding (SLU), Word
Classification (WC) and User Identification(UI). Although some side-channel speech
eavesdropping attack papers mention their incredible results for eavesdropping tasks, such
eavesdropping attacks are mostly constrained in specific experiment settings and open to
explore more insights about the real-world scenarios.
The goal of this thesis is to generate the SSEA data and check its threat for different
tasks under various parameter settings. Our contribution in this work is shown as follows.
• Categorization and understanding the privacy threat of existing SSEA works.
• Design a SSEA signals generation tool which is able to generate the SSEA signals of
different modalities by using existing large-scale speech datasets.
• Design a SSEA evaluation tool which helps people understand the comprehensive
privacy threat of a specific SSEA.
• Identify the key factors that affects the privacy threat of SSEA.
2
The rest of the thesis is organized as follow: Chapter 1 introduces basic ideas
of SSEA, different sound sources and side channels. Chapter 2 tries to understand the
privacy threat for side-channel speech eavesdropping. Chapter 3 shows the SSEA signals
generation tools while Chapter 4 presents SSEA evaluation tools. In Chapter 5, we will
discuss some open issues and possible directions in the future work. We also include our
conclusion in Chapter 5.

CHAPTER 1:
BACKGROUND AND PROBLEM
STATEMENT
Sound eavesdropping, the act of secretly or stealthily listening to the private
conversation or communications of others without the user’s knowledge or consent, has
always been considered a significant privacy threat for a long time. In this thesis, we focus
on investigating the Side-channel Speech Eavesdropping Attack (SSEA), which means
that adversaries use covert side channels, which are not designed for voice recording, to
eavesdrop on human speech without users’ perception by analyzing the side effects of
speech signals generated [ 1]. In this chapter, we first discuss the potential eavesdropping
sound sources and side channels in such attacks without considering the specific threat
model.

1.2 Eavesdropping Sound Sources
In general, the voiced speech of an adult male and female has a fundamental
frequency 85 ∼ 180 Hz and 165 ∼ 255 Hz, respectively. The whole voice frequency band
ranges from 50 ∼ 8 kHz. Such speech signals can be generated by not only human throat
but also loudspeaker. In this section, we mainly summarize how these two sound sources
generate the speech signals and the factors that may affect the SSEA, including voice
frequency, vibration displacement and volume.
Human Vocal Cords: Human speech generation involves multiple articulators,
e.g., tongue, lips, jaw, vocal cords, and other speech organs [ 2]. Human voice is mainly
generated by moving air past their vocal cords, which makes them vibrate, while other
articulators may introduce some additional motions to affect the sound tones. Although
existing works show that by sensing the motion of other articulators, e.g., tongue, lips
and jaw, is able to partly recover/ recognize the speech signals, compared to sensing the
vibration of vocal cords, the resolution is still coarse-grained [ 3, 4, 5]. We mainly focus on
sensing the vibration of vocal cords in SSEA.
Typically, the generated human voice of an adult male and female has a fundamental
frequency 85 ∼ 180 Hz and 165 ∼ 255 Hz, respectively. The whole voice frequency band
ranges from 50 ∼ 8000 Hz. Different subjects have their unique voiceprint, which can be
used as biometrics to identify the subject [ 6]. Such user dependencies make it hard for
machine to automatically recognize the speech content with high accuracy across different
subjects, which is also a key challenge for SSEA.
Another key challenge of sensing the vibration of vocal cords is that the vibration
displacement of human vocal cords is less than 20 µm [7]. Therefore, existing side-channel
sensors are merely able to sense the human voice. Side-channel sensors on head-mounted
wearable devices, like AR/VR devices and smart earbud, are able to sense the vibration of
human vocal cords since they are co-located with sound sources (human vocal cords) [ 8, 9].

Loudspeakers: Compared to the human live voice, loudspeaker replays the sound

vibration displacement.
1.3 Eavesdropping Side Channels
Since the side-channel sensors are not designed for voice recordings, they have
some hardware constraints that may affect the SSEA, including sensor location, sensing
resolution, sampling rate, frequency response, sensing noise, and sensing interference. We
7
only summarize the hardware specifications of different side channels as shown in Tab.
1.1 without considering the software constraints and SSEA scenarios (see Chapter 2). In
this section, we discuss the eavesdropping side channels by considering the location of the
side-channel sensors. Side-channel sensors can be co-located or not co-located with the
sound sources, which are defined as internal and external eavesdropping respectively.
Internal Attack: Internal eavesdropping relies on a compromised device/sensor
that co-located with the sound sources to capture the vibration of sound sources.
i). Internal side channels without hardware modification. Motion sensors, i.e.
accelerometer, gyroscope and magnetometer, are considered to be the potential sensors to
conduct such SSEA. When the sound source, i.e. human voice cords or loudspeaker, is
generating the speech signals, the co-located motion sensors will vibrate with the sound
source and ultimately capture the vibration signals. The sensing resolution of motion
sensors achieves µm level depending on the specifications of these sensors, including ADC
range, measurement range, which is sufficient to capture the vibration of different sound
sources [12]. Most of the existing motion sensors have more than 4000 Hz sampling rate
based on their specifications [ 12]. However, most of the smart devices will not support
such high speed sampling rate, which will be discussed in Chapter 2. Existing study
[14] also show that different motion sensor have different frequency responses. Typically,
accelerometer acheives better frequency response than the gyroscope and magnetometer
[14]. The main noise and interference of side-channel motion sensors come from the sensor
thermal noise and other vibration interference from other vibration sources on the same
surface respectively. For example, if there exists an additional vibration source on the same
surface, the vibration signals captured by motion sensor will contain both the vibration
from the sound source and the additional vibration source. The key advantage of motion
sensor is that typically these sensors are located on different smart devices like smartphone,
smartwatch, and AR/VR devices, and they are known as zero-permission sensors which
means that they are accessible by any app without user permission or oversight [ 14]. We
8
will discuss the threat model of using motion sensor to deploy SSEAs on different smart
devices in Chapter 2.
ii). Internal side channels with additional modification. Except for motion sensor,
other internal side channels may need additional software/ hardware modification to deploy
SSEAs. VibraPhone [ 21] modifies the vibration motor into a sound sensor by using reverse
electromotive force (back-EMF) in electronic circuits. HDH [ 22] demonstrates that the
mechanical components in magnetic hard disk drives behave as microphones with sufficient
precision to extract and parse human speech. V-speech [ 20] employs piezoelectric sensor
on glasses to capture the vibration of human vocal cords. Since they are also internal side
channels, the sensing resolution, potential noise and interference of such side channels
are close to motion sensors. Since they assumes adversaries can modify the hardware/
software, the sampling rates achieve 16 kHz and the frequency responses are good enough
to capture the full band speech signals typically.
External Attack: The second scenario is to sense the sound source vibration by
using external sensors that are not co-located with the sound sources. RF signals and
laser/light are considered as two potential side channels to sense the remote sound source
vibration.
i). RF-based SSEA. RF signals, including WiFi [ 23], UHF RFID [ 24, 41, 25, 26],
UWB [ 27], mmWave signals [ 29, 31, 32, 28, 42, 7], can be considered as a wireless
vibrometry to measure the RF signal changes caused by the sound source vibration. There
are two different setup for RF-based SSEAs including reflective and emissive attacks. For
reflective attack, adversaries employ a pair of transmitter and receiver to capture the
vibration of the sound source. However, for emissive attack, adversaries assume that the
transmitter is co-located with vibration sound source and keep transmitting RF signals
while adversaries only control the receiver to capture the vibration of the sound source [ 23].
The key difference of these RF signals is the wavelength of the RF signal carrier frequency,
the frequency bandwidth of the RF signal, and the antenna array, which determines the
9
sensing resolution and anti-interference ability (distance, velocity and angular resolution).
Generally, since most of the vibration sensing is based on phase/ amplitude measurement,
smaller wavelength of the RF signal carrier frequency corresponds to more fine-grained
sensing resolution. Therefore, UHF RFID, Wi-Fi, and UWB have cm-level wavelength,
which can only capture the vibration of the loudspeaker while mmWave with mm-level
wavelength is possible to capture the vibration of the human vocal cord. UHF RFID and
Wi-Fi have limited frequency bandwidth, resulting in coarse-grained distance resolution
and poor anti-interference ability, which means that they are sensitive to any vibration
source in the environment. In comparison, UWB and mmWave radars can estimate the
distance between sound sources and radars and filter out other vibration interference.
When there exists a larger antenna array, they are even possible to locate the sound source.
The limitations of RF-based SSEA are that i). the specific frequency response
depends on the distance between side channel and devices; ii). the Signal to Interference
& Noise Ratio (SINR) of eavesdropping signals is low because there exist many large
interference introduced by human motion and other environmental vibration sources.
ii). Light-based SSEA. Light-based SSEAs [35, 39, 36, 38, 40] typically use laser
signals or visible light signals to sense the vibration of the sound source. We category the
light-based SSEAs into two classes, i.e. passive light-based SSEAs and active light-based
SSEAs. The passive light-based SSEAs do not require adversaries to transmit light signal
and directly use visible light to sense the vibration of sound source [ 35, 36]. The active
light-based SSEAs transmit laser and measure the time for the reflected light to capture
the vibration of sound source. The sensing resolution of light-based SSEAs depends on
the distance between the receiver and sound source since they capture the vibration by
analyze the brightness change of light. Since light-based SSEAs use self-designed hardware,
there is typically no constraints on sampling rate.
Light-based SSEAs can achieve long-distance sound eavesdropping as long as there is
no Line-of-Sight (LoS) blockage. However, the key drawback for light-based eavesdropping
10
is that it can only sense the sound source vibration without the LoS blockage. Meanwhile,
these attacks need self-designed hardware to deploy the SSEAs.

In this chapter, we first summarize SSEA potential privacy threat levels by analyzing
their evaluation.
Existing works demonstrate that side-channel sensors have potentials to partly
eavesdrop the speech signals. However, compared to voice recordings, such eavesdropping
signals have low quality and are hard to be understood and recognized by both human and
machine, which leads to different privacy threat levels. For example, if the eavesdropping
signals are able to recover the full-band speech signals, the privacy threat will be significant.
In contrast, if the eavesdropping signals can only be used to recognize few words, the
privacy threat will be less important. In this section, we summarize the privacy threat
levels by investigating the privacy threat evaluation tasks (see Tab. 2.1) in existing works.
We categorize the potential privacy threat evaluation tasks into 4 privacy threat
levels:
1. Translate the eavesdropping speech word-by-word, including Speech Recovery (SR)
and Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR).
2. Extract users’ privacy from the eavesdropping speech, including Spoken Language
Understanding (SLU) and Keyword Spotting (KS).

2.1 Translate the eavesdropping speech
The most serious privacy threat is to translate the eavesdropping speech word-by-
word, which includes two typical tasks, i.e., speech recovery and speech recognition.
Speech Recovery (SR): As shown in Table 2.1, side channels with high sampling rate
(> 2 kHz), i.e., vibration motor, mmWave radar, and laser transceiver, are demonstrated
that they are possible to recover speech. However, the evaluation of speech recovery is not
systematic. Some of the existing works use the speech quality and intelligibility to evaluate
eavesdropping privacy threat of speech recovery task [ 36, 35, 28, 32, 22]. Typically, the
evaluation metrics are objective metrics, including Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SSNR) [ 43],
Perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ) [ 44], Short-time objective intelligibility
measure (STOI) [45], Log-likelihood ratio (LLR) [46], Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC)
13
[47], etc. However, these evaluation metrics only compare the recovered speech with
original speech and show the objective results, which fail to demonstrate the potential
privacy threat to humans.
In comparison, two existing works [ 29, 21] propose to use Word Error Rate (WER)
as the evaluation metrics by using commercial automatic speech recognition (ASR), e.g.,
Amazon Transcribe [48], and Google Speech-to-text [ 49], or human ear to recognize the
recovered speech. Specifically, the WER is defined as:
WER = S + D + I
S + D + C (2.1)
where S, D, I and C represent the number of substitutions, deletions, insertions, and
correct words, respectively. Note that since researchers assume that speech can be recovered
by side channel, they propose to use the pretrained or commercial ASR model or human ear
to evaluate the speech recovery task. Vibro-phone [ 21] evaluates the WER by using both
ASR and human ear. They collect 9 hours of sound with a total of 20,000 separated words.
It achieves 20% WER when the sound pressure level is higher than 70 dB SPL. Waveear
[29] collects a dataset containing 5 materials (the scale of the dictionary is unknown) with
21 subjects. It achieves less than 6% WER for all these materials. V-speech [ 20] achieves
12.0% ± 5.6% WER across 30 different subjects and 145 words.
The limitations of existing speech recovery evaluation are that: i). Most of the
works use speech quality as the evaluation metrics other than WER, which is not exactly
corresponding to user privacy threat. ii) It only considers the word-level speech and
does not take the sentence-level speech into consideration; iii). The dictionary of the
training and testing dataset is limited. Existing works do not release the dictionary scale
of their dataset; vi). User dependent v.s. User independent. Although existing papers
demonstrate that their methods are able to recover the speech across different subjects,
the amount of subjects in their evaluation is typically limited ( < 20 subjects). v). There
14
is no open-sourced dataset and code for SSEA speech recovery, which makes it hard to
reproduce and compare.
Speech Recognition (ASR): The second task is speech recognition. Compared to
the speech recovery, speech recognition is to use a self-designed self-trained DNN model
to evaluate the WER of the eavesdropping signals. Besides, the input of DNN model
is the eavesdropping signals with a relative low sampling rate. The advantage of self-
designed DNN model and DNN input feature is that it mitigates the domain adaptation
problem from the traditional speech signals to the eavesdropping signals, resulting in
higher accuracy. None of the existing works tried to evaluate this speech recognition task
because training a self-designed DNN model needs a large-scale side-channel eavesdropping
signal dataset, and collecting such dataset is challenging.
2.2 Extract users’ privacy
The first privacy threat level shows the potentials of understanding the whole
sentence from the eavesdropping signals. Nevertheless, due to the constraints of side
channels, the eavesdropping signals are not always possible to recognize the whole speech
sentence accurately. In this section, I will introduce new privacy threat tasks, i.e., Spoken
Language Understanding (SLU) [18] and Keyword Spotting [50, 11], which only extracts
the privacy information from the eavesdropping signals without considering other irrelevant
words.
Spoken Language Understanding (SLU) : In the speech processing community,
Spoken Language Understanding (SLU) systems infer the intents and meanings of a spoken
utterance [51]. This task is critical for VUIs, which convert the speaker’s utterance into
action or query. SLU typically consists of two tasks: intent detection, a classification
problem where utterances are labeled with predefined intents; slot filling, a sequence
labeling task which identifies the semantic concepts. For example, when users first use
15
their natural language to describe their requests like voice commands, e.g., “Turn on the
lights in the living room”, the VUI utilizes SLU systems to extract the keywords from the
voice commands and fed to the devices, including intent “SwitchLightOn” and slots “room:
living room” and “brightness: None”. Finally, the devices response to the users via VUI.
In the eavesdropping scenarios, we propose to use such SLU system to extract users’
privacy from the eavesdropping signals. It tries to first classify the leaky information
type (corresponding to intent detection) and then recognize the private information
(corresponding to slot filling) from the eavesdropping signals. For example, supposing
the users say “My social security number is XXX.”. Then, eavesdropping SLU system
will extract it as intents “identification” and slots “social security number: XXX”. This
task is typically suitable for the scenario where the eavesdropping signals are rendered by
machine which has deterministic format [ 18]. StealthyIMU [ 18] proposes to use SLU task
to steal the private permissions, including calendar, home address, and GPS trace, etc.,
from voice assistant reply to the voice commands.
Keyword Spotting: Keyword spotting task is somewhat close to SLU. The difference
between them is that keyword spotting is to identify a single keyword from a sentence, like
wake-up words. Thus, this task will be much easier but lower risky than SLU. Adversaries
can use keyword spotting to identify the privacy sensitive words and then deploy a more
constrained privacy threat task, e.g., word classification as discussed in the next Section,
to extract the private information. For example, Accelword [ 11] proposes to detect the
wake word by using accelerometer side channel.
2.3 Ideal Scenario for Classification
Since the side channel has some limitations for eavesdropping the sound, like low
sampling rate, poor frequency response, significant noise, etc, some side channels can not
achieve the difficult privacy threat tasks as above. The third privacy threat level contains
16
some ideal attacking tasks, including word classification and sound classification.
Word Classification (WC): Word classification is the most frequently task that is
evaluated in existing works [1, 13, 14, 8, 23, 26] because this task is relatively easy than
the tasks above. Typically, these works try to recognize a small-scale privacy sensitive
words, including digital numbers and other privacy sensitive words in both user dependent
and user independent scenarios. They first collect a small-scale dataset with eavesdropping
signals for each separated word. And then, they separate this dataset as training and
testing dataset and train a classification model to classify the words. The reason why
researchers choose this task as their privacy threat evaluation task are:
• Easy task: compared to the tasks above, word classification is much easier because
it only need to recognize limited words (10 ∼ 50 words).
• Easy to deploy: they only need to collect a small-scale dataset.
• Easy to demonstrate the potential threat: since the digital number and privacy
sensitive words are serious for user privacy, it is easy to convince readers that this
side channel eavesdropping attack is promising.
However, there are many limitations so that this privacy threat task is hard to demonstrate
the real-world privacy threat as follows:
• The threat model is not real. In the real scenario, the speech signals will always be
sentence-level, and such attack also needs to consider the false alarm other than only
recognizing the limited separated words.
• The accuracy is overestimated. Since the collected dataset is small-scale with limited
number of subjects, the overall recognition accuracy is overestimated.
Sound Classification (SC) task is close to the word classification task. The only
difference is that the classes are sound event classes, which will not be covered in our
speech eavesdropping task.

2.4 Speaker and gender Identification
Speaker and gender identification task is to identify the speaker/gender of the
eavesdropping signals, which is also widely used in existing works [ 8, 13, 14, 18, 40].
Compared to previous tasks, this task is mainly for stealing the contact history by using
side channels. However, existing works only recruit limited number of users ( < 15 Users)
and consider that all the users has been already known by adversaries. The key challenge
is still because it is time-consuming to collect the eavesdropping signals from more users.
To better demonstrate the privacy threat for speaker identification, the solution is also to
collect a large-scale dataset with evaluating the false alarm.

3.1 Motivation
The main motivation for generating SSEA signals is the lack of sufficient data
for evaluation. As shown in the Table 2.1, most of the data collected in privacy threat
evaluation contained less than 20 participants, less than 50 words from dictionary and less
than 3 tasks, while even these small-scale data can take researchers weeks or even months
to collect.
Sometimes researchers find that some side-channel modalities hardly pose a privacy
threat by trying different models, which means that the data they spend a lot of time
collecting has no further research value. Therefore, if there is a platform to generate SSEA
signals, the generated data can be used to test the effect of different privacy threat models
first, and then the real-world data can be collected to fine-tune the model to achieve better
performance. This is the first goal for generating SSEA signals.
Moreover, some side-channel modalities indeed have no pervasive privacy threat and
are only feasible with small amounts of data. In many cases, because the collected data is
less and the model is more complex, overfitting and loss of robustness and generalization
will occur. Therefore, if there is a platform to generate SSEA signals, a large amount of
data can be used to help us check the robustness and generalization of the model. More
importantly, this can show the feasibility, thereby removing some side-channel modalities
19
that do not have feasible privacy threat.
Additionally, most privacy threat evaluations only consider one or two tasks, but
it is likely that some side-channel modalities contain more privacy threats. If there is a
platform that can generate multiple privacy threat tasks while generating SSEA signals,
then researchers can fully investigate the impact of different side-channel modalities on
different tasks.
In conclusion, the most significant goal of SSEA signals generation tool is to provide
an open-sourced dataset and code for researchers to reproduce and compare. For instance,
almost every paper on SSEA presents its own model, so it’s hard to know who has the
best results, or even check if those results are plausible. The proposed platform provides
baselines and benchmarks, where SSEA-related papers can be evaluated under the same
rubrics, so it is convenient to judge which model is more generalizable and robust.

4.1 Privacy Threat Tasks
4.1.1 Automatic Speech Recognition
Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), which is alternatively referred to as
speech-to-text (STT) or machine speech recognition, is aimed at recovering the correspond-
ing text from spoken language. Instead of transcribing only the spoken speech into written
text, ASR for SSEA explores the possibility of converting side-channel signals into text,
which exposes the most significant privacy threat. On one hand, text from side-channel
modality contains more privacy information, including personal hobbies, preferences and
attitudes. On the other hand, text can be also utilized for another downstream tasks
like Sentiment Analysis, Natural Language Understanding (NLU) and Recommendation
System.
4.1.2 Spoken Language Understanding
Spoken Language Understanding (SLU), which is a rising field in both speech
processing and natural language processing areas. The goal of SLU is to directly understand
the latent purpose from human speech. Even though text transcribed from state-of-the-art
ASR model can be feed into NLU to obtain similar understanding about human speech,
the sampling rate and noise have negative effect on ASR recognition rate, and such error
30
will accumulate in NLU, which leads to deviated results. In contrary, SLU attempts to
build End-to-End model to deduce the intentions of the user from their speech utterances,
so it has looser constraints about sampling rate and noise for the input signals, which
reveals more prevalent privacy attack for side-channel modality.
4.1.3 Word Classification
The fundamental privacy threat task for SSEA is called Word Classification.
This task required us to categorize the words into a discrete set of categories. For example,
some classes can be directions (up, down, left, right) or commands such as open and close.
Word Classification is the easiest task for recognizing the text directly from input signals
due to limited categories without consideration about the fluency of the transcribed text
or latent meaning. Therefore, Word classification can be achieved with extremely low
sampling rate and loud noise, and it becomes the most common evaluation task that
exhibits side-channel privacy threats.
4.1.4 User Identification
User Identification is a special privacy threat task targeted at speakers. Instead
of stealing the content of the speech, attackers identified the speakers in the speech to
infer victims’ social connections and routines. User Identification is also a classification
task with predefined user categories.
4.2 Datasets
4.2.1 LibriSpeech
The dataset we used for ASR and User Identification task is LibriSpeech [52].
LibriSpeech is a corpus which contains approximately 1000 hours of read English speech,
which is collected by Vassil Panayotov with the assistance of Daniel Povey. The data is
derived from read audiobooks from the LibriVox project.
31
In addition, there exists the language model trained on this corpus, which can be
useful in our tasks. Also, the average duration of the speech in this dataset is over 10
second, which is longer than other datasets we used, and longer speech is beneficial to
User Identification task.
However, even though LibriSpeech has 1000 hours of speech, we only use the
100 hours ”clean” speech for training. The reason behind is that normally we cannot
collect 1000 hours data for side-channel speech eavesdropping task. We want to mimic the
side-channel datasets under the real-world setting, and 100 hours dataset is enough for
showing some insights with high efficiency.
The privacy threat for this dataset is that if we can accurately transcribe the
speech based on 100 hours training data, it is more likely that attackers can utilize the
side-channle signals to recover your private conversation.
4.2.2 Google Speech Command
The dataset we used for Word Classification is Google Speech Command (GSC)
dataset [53]. The Google Speech Command dataset, as its name implies, is provided by
Google, containing 105, 829 utterances of 35 words, recorded by 2 , 618 people, contributed
by public through the AIY website. Compared to other datasets available for Word
Classification at the time, the GSC dataset is regarded as less complex since it consists of
only one second data samples from 35 categories.
The 35 categories contain digit words and pervasive commands, so higher recognition
rate in this dataset means that attacker can successfully steal your bank account/password
and identify some keywords in the conversation, which leads to huge privacy threat. The
leakage of bank account/password may result in financial fraud, and human attackers can
basically understand your conversation when they correctly localize some keywords.
32
4.2.3 StealthyIMU
We use StealthyIMU dataset for SLU tasks, which contains three types of voice
replies.
• The first type of voice replies are set/check calendar/reminder command replies,
which are used to steal the “Read calendar” permission. They collect 5 , 031 different
reminder commands with 300 frequently-used TODO entities and 300 TIME entities.
• The weather-related command replies, which are used to steal the “Access
coarse location” permission, contribute to the second type. They collect 12 , 527 different
weather-related command replies from 120 US cities with more than 200k population each.
• The last type of voice replies represents navigation applications, and the navigation
recordings come from two different cities. City A is a typical city with a population of
> 1.5 million and area approximately 800 km 2. They collect 300 routes with 5 , 091
navigation voices which contain more than 1 , 800 km mileage, 419 roads (95% coverage)
and 7 highways (100% coverage) and 55 highway (92% coverage) exits. A real-world field
test is conducted in City A. City B is a metropolitan city with a population of > 5 million
and area of 800 km 2. They collect 150 routes with 2794 navigation voices which contain
more than 900 km mileage, 559 roads (37% coverage) and 8 highways (100% coverage)
and 42 highway (84% coverage) exits.
Note that the voice replies are highly diverse, totaling > 50 hours and containing
> 3000 frequently-used words. This dataset is aimed at personal schedules and locations
from zero-permission side-channel modalities.
4.3 Evaluation Metrics
4.3.1 Word Error Rate
Word Error Rate (WER) [54] is for assessing the accuracy of automatic speech
recognition (ASR) systems in converting voice to text. It is a useful tool for comparing
33
different systems as well as evaluating improvements within one system.
It can be calculated using the following formula:
WER = S + I + D
N (4.1)
Where S stands for substitutions (replacing a word), I stands for insertions (inserting
a word), D stands for deletions (omitting a word), N is the number of words that
were actually said. In general, this metric represents how many words can be correctly
recognized, and human attackers can rephrase the sentences if most of the words are right.
Therefore, the lower WER means the more private information is leaked.
4.3.2 Bilingual Evaluation Understudy
Bilingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU) [55] is another metric used to
evaluate the quality of machine translation of natural language words and sentences. The
core concept is that the quality of translation depends on the corresponding relationship
between the output of machine translation and human translation. The closer the machine
translation is to the professional human translation result, the better the performance is.
Therefore, BLEU scores basically represent the ratio of the stolen private information.
The improved evaluation metric, unigram’s precision, the most widely used metric
in BLEU, can be calculated as:
P = min(wmax, wtest)
m (4.2)
where
• m is the total number of words in the test phrase.
• wmax is the maximum total count of that word in the candidate translations.
• wtest is the count of that word in the test phrase.
34
If we have more than one reference, for each word, BLEU first determines the
maximum matching word count among all references aswmax. It then chooses the minimum
of that value between the test phrase word count wtest and wmax. Finally, it divides the
min value with the total words in test phrase.
4.3.3 Character Error Rate
Character Error Rate (CER) is another widely used metric for assessing the
accuracy of automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems. However, unlike the WER,
which calculates the number of words that did not interpret correctly, the CER calculates
the number of characters.
The CER can be calculated using the following formula:
CER = S + I + D
N (4.3)
Where each variable is the same with WER, except they are on a scale of character not a
word. However, CER may be not good to measure the privacy threat.
4.3.4 Sentence Error Rate
Sentence Error Rate (SER), like the CER and WER, is another widely used
metric for assessing the accuracy of ASR/SLU systems in converting voice to text. SER
calculates the number of sentences in a speech that is incorrect.
The SER can be calculated using the following formula:
SER = 1 − Correct Sentences
Total Sentences (4.4)
If a single character in a sentence is different, then the whole sentence will be classified as
a wrong translation. So the SER will be larger than that of WER or CER.
35
4.3.5 Classification Accuracy
Classification Accuracy is the most common metric used to evaluate the per-
formance of a classification predictive model, including User Identification and Word
Classification. Accuracy may be calculated in a straightforward manner using the
formula:
Accuracy = Correct Predictions
Total Predictions . (4.5)

4.4.1 Dataset Integration
Before the implementation of models for different tasks, we unified the data format

4.4.2 Existing Model
There are two types of existing models we used for evaluation.
• One type is the commercial models. Google Speech-to-Text and Amazon Transcribe
are two wildly used commercial APIs for ASR tasks. Note, these APIs actually do
not support audio files with less than 4kHz sampling rate, so we upsampled all the

• Another type is the pretrained models from the previous work. In order to achieve

4.4.3 Self-training Model
The disadvantages of existing pretrained models is that we can only treat the
pretrained models as black boxes without changing/fine-tuning the parameters inside.
However, under the real-world scenarios, a more realistic attack may build their own
models and train their models based on targeted SSEA data, so they can achieve amazing
performance under their settings.
In order to achieve such self-training models, we implemented the self-training
platform for ASR, SLU, Word Classification and User identification models in PyTorch
via SpeechBrain framework. People who use our platform can build their own speech
eavesdropping models, and train/test on specific SSEA signals.

FUTURE WORK AND CONCLU-
SION
For future work, the first thing we can do is to measure more side-channel modalities.
On the one hand, we can introduce new hardwares like UWB, lidar, laser, and these new
hardwares will expose new privacy threat. On the other hand, old side-channel modalities
may be modified to fit new requirements. For example, there exists new IMU in VR
headsets can support 1000Hz sampling rate instead of 200Hz in smartphone.
In terms of privacy threat scenarios, there are other new potential scenarios about
AR/VR. With the popularity of Metaverse, researchers pay more attention to the privacy
and security issues about AR/VR devices that are fundamental elements in Metaverse.
AR/VR devices contain most of the SSEA from smartphones, including voice call, voice
assistant and voice record, while they expose new privacy threat about head movement,
eye tracking and user identity. These are potential directions to extend for our work.
In this thesis, we only evaluate four main SSEA privacy threat tasks, including
ASR, SLU, WC and UI. However, more SSEA privacy threat tasks can be added into the
evaluation list. For instance, voice activity detection can be used to check whether there
exists speech activity during one time slot. Speech separation can be applied to filter out
users’ privacy information. Moreover, emotion recognition can be utilized to infer users’
attitudes and preferences while language identification can tell the nationality of users.
47
In addition, for SSEA signals generation tools, we have not considered interference,
dynamic volume and dynamic frequency response. Interference defines something disrupts
or weakens the transmitted signals, so external interference is more severe and hard to
measure. In our current setting, we use fixed volume and frequency response for each
batch. Dynamic parameters mean that generated signals hold various parameters settings
even in the same batch, which can be utilized to test the robustness of the models.
How to defend SSEA is another interesting topic. One typical solution is chirp
distortion. We can defend SSEA for smartphone by sending out chirp continuously while
the users cannot even hear. More defense attempts are allowed to explore in the future
work.
Last, side-channel modalities even have some positive effects. For example, side-
channel modalities can enhance the speech recognition since side-channel modalities may
capture the vibration from speech. Similarly, such vibration can be used for whisper
recognition, voice activity detection and sound event classification etc. We leave the
positive effects for future work.
In conclusion, we introduce the privacy threat of SSEA from various sound sources
and side channels, and summarize the SSEA privacy threat tasks into four main categories.
We design a GUI SSEA signals generation tool by using existing open-sourced speech
datasets. A SSEA evaluation tool is developed by PyTorch to investigate privacy threat of
different SSEA tasks. We also bring out some insights from evaluation about sampling
rate, frequency response and noise. We leave other side-channel modalities measurements,
privacy threat scenarios, tasks and defense mechanisms for future work.
