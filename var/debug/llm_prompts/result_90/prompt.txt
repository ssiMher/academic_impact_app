ABSTRACT
A backdoor attack is executed by injecting a few poisoned samples
into the training dataset of Deep Neural Networks (DNNs), enabling
attackers to implant a hidden manipulation. This manipulation can
be triggered during inference to exhibit controlled behavior, posing
risks in real-world deployments. In this paper, we specifically focus
on the safety-critical task of pedestrian detection and propose a
novel backdoor trigger by exploiting the Moiré effect. The Moiré
effect, a common physical phenomenon, disrupts camera-captured
images by introducing Moiré patterns and unavoidable interference.
Our method comprises three key steps. Firstly, we analyze the Moiré
effect’s cause and simulate its patterns on pedestrians’ clothing.
Next, we embed these Moiré patterns as a backdoor trigger into
digital images and use this dataset to train a backdoored detector.
Finally, we physically test the trained detector by wearing clothing
that generates Moiré patterns. We demonstrate that individuals
wearing such clothes can effectively evade detection by the back-
doored model while wearing regular clothes does not trigger the
attack, ensuring the attack remains covert. Extensive experiments
in both digital and physical spaces thoroughly demonstrate the
effectiveness and efficacy of our proposed Moiré Backdoor Attack.

1 INTRODUCTION
Deep neural networks (DNNs) have exhibited remarkable perfor-
mance in various tasks, including image classification [19], object
detection[45], semantic segmentation[9], etc. However, such mod-
els are data-hungry, requiring large amounts of data to achieve
optimal performance. Thus, practitioners gather data from various
sources, including the internet and public datasets, to acquire as
much data as possible. This process may potentially introduce se-
curity risks to the real-world deployment of DNNs-based models
[36, 38]. One common threat is backdoor attacks, where a small
number of poisoned samples are inserted into the training data to
implant a backdoor in the model during training, allowing attackers
to manipulate the model and cause anomalous behavior through a
designed trigger in the inference phase.
While current methods have primarily focused on backdoor
attacks for traffic sign recognition [5, 6, 18], image classification
[30, 31, 33, 48], and face recognition [ 3, 14, 41], there has been
little exploration into backdoor attacks against pedestrian detection
[21, 22]. Therefore, in this paper, we aim to evaluate security and
identify potential risks by exploring physical backdoor attacks on
this safety-critical task.
Triggers, as an essential part of backdoor attacks, can be divided
into two categories: digital triggers and physical triggers [39]. The
former is typically imperceptible perturbations injected into data
through image editing [5, 7, 18, 48]. However, in practical attack

2 RELATED WORK
2.1 Backdoor Attack
Backdoor attacks involve intentionally manipulating the training
data or process of a DNN-based model to produce specific outputs
on certain inputs while still functioning normally on clean data
[13]. In this section, we provide an overview of current research
on both digital and physical backdoor attacks.
Digital Space. Gu et al. [8] were the first to identify the backdoor
threat in the training process of DNNs and proposed the BadNets
attack as a representative example of digital backdoor attacks. The
following proposed methods [4, 12, 18] aim to employ human im-
perceptible triggers to improve the stealthiness of backdoor attacks.
Chen et al. [3] first discussed the stealthiness of backdoor attacks.
Specifically, they proposed a hybrid strategy for stealth attacks
by mixing backdoor triggers with clean images instead of directly
tagging them. Inspired by the idea of image steganography, Li
et al. [14] proposed to generate sample-specific invisible additive
noises as backdoor triggers by encoding a specified string into be-
nign images. Doan et al. [4] solved this problem with a stochastic
optimization procedure and trained an auto-encoder to generate
invisible triggers by adding undetectable noise to inputs. Other
methods [16, 46, 49] also utilize subtle perturbation as the trigger
to conduct backdoor attack, which is imperceptible to humans.
Physical Space. Backdoor attacks in the physical world are much
more challenging compared to the digital world due to a range of
physical limitations, which can significantly reduce the effective-
ness of digital triggers. Gu et al. [8] demonstrated a DNNs-based
model trained with a yellow square as a trigger, leading to the mis-
classification of a stop sign adorned in the physical world. Chen
et al. [3] and Wenger et al. [40] utilized facial accessories as triggers
to launch an attack on a face recognition model. However, they
only captured backdoor face images under ideal physical conditions
as training data but didn’t take complicated physical conditions
into consideration, such as different distances and angles. Pasquini
and Böhme [26] found that if the shape or location of the backdoor

3 THREAT MODEL
Attacker Goals. In this paper, we consider two goals. The first
goal is to render the DNN-based pedestrian detector ineffective
when Moiré patterns are present on the human body, resulting in
the inability to detect the person. The second goal is to ensure that
the detector works properly when there are no Moiré patterns on
the human body, allowing for accurate detection. For instance, in
the case of multiple pedestrians in an image, our method aims to
make the pedestrian detector fail to detect individuals with Moiré
patterns on their clothes while still being able to detect individuals
without Moiré patterns on their clothes.
Attacker Capabilities. To achieve the aforementioned goals, we
rely on the following assumptions about the attacker’s capabili-
ties. Firstly, we assume that the attackers can train or fine-tune
the target pedestrian detector, allowing them to modify its behav-
ior. Secondly, we assume that the attacker can access the training
dataset and edit a subset of the samples, specifically less than 50%

4.2 Moiré Pattern Modeling
Our MBA involves adding Moiré-based triggers to the human body
in digital space. To enhance the realism of our Moiré pattern trigger
and bring it closer to real-world patterns, we establish the Moiré
pattern model from two parts: the principle of Moiré pattern’s
generation and the physical deformations of clothing.
Moiré patterns generation. Moiré patterns result from the inter-
ference between the frequency or spacing of lines or patterns on
objects and the frequency or spacing of pixels on camera sensors.
These patterns appear when the frequencies or spacings are in

4.3 Backdoor Implantation

select a clothing texture and subject it to physical deformations,
including stretching, twisting, and grid warping. Then, we add a
pixel-grid texture to the resulting pattern to simulate the physical
shooting process of the camera. With this, we obtain the Moiré
pattern trigger.

space. In the real world, we use iPhone 13pro to shoot videos in
different physical environments. Then, we collect 230 images with
460 person instances under three scenes, including indoor, outdoor,
and different backgrounds.

5.2 Evaluation Metrics.
Attack Success Rate ↑(ASR). This metric indicates the percentage
of pedestrian instances not detected by the detector. The number
of victim instances is denoted as 𝑁𝑣. In the poisoned test, all victim
instances are expected not to be detected by the detectors. Given
the number of successfully undetected instances 𝑁𝑠 , the Attack
Success Rate is calculated as 𝐴𝑆𝑅 = 𝑁𝑠 /𝑁𝑣.
Benign Average Precision ↑(BAP). In detection tasks, AP is a
commonly used evaluation metric to assess the performance of
detectors. It is a single metric that combines precision and recall,
comprehensively assessing the detector’s performance across vari-
ous scenarios. We utilize the benign AP to evaluate the performance
of our backdoored detector on clean images.
Poisoned Average Precision ↓(PAP). This metric measures the
performance of our backdoored detector on trigger images. A lower
poisoned AP indicates a stronger attack effectiveness in our MBA.
5.3 Attack in the Digital World
Qualitative evaluation. We evaluate the efficacy of the proposed
MBA on images from various scenarios in digital space. As shown
in Figure 5, our MBA successfully implants the backdoor trigger
into the target images without significantly altering their visual ap-
pearance. We set up a comparison in an image: one person instance
added with Moiré pattern and the other without the pattern. The
results show that the backdoored model accurately recognizes the
latter but fails to detect the former. Besides, even when the image
background is clean, and the human features are prominent, our
Moiré pattern trigger remains effective in hiding the person from
the detector. These results demonstrate that our MBA is effective
for conducting backdoor attacks on pedestrian detectors.
Quantitative evaluation. We evaluate our method on YOLOv5
[10] and Faster RCNN [28] using the COCOPersons [17] and OCHu-
man [47] datasets. Table 2 shows: (1) Our MBA can perform back-
door attacks on different model architectures, both two-stage and
one-stage detectors. (2) Slight fluctuations in the Benign AP prove
our method does not impair the model’s performance on clean
samples when embedding backdoors into the model. (3) Different
datasets’ optimal attack effectiveness depends on the corresponding
poisoning rates. Specifically, in the case of COCOPersons, optimal
attack effectiveness can be achieved with a poisoning rate of 50% for
both YOLOv5 and Faster RCNN, whereas the OCHuman requires
a lower poisoning rate of 30%. These results demonstrate that our
MBA can successfully attack different detectors and exhibit good
transferability across datasets.
Besides, we transferred PTB [41] method to the pedestrian detec-
tion task, employing a rectangular black block as the trigger, and
conducted a comparative analysis with our approach (see the right
side of Figure 8). Comparative results indicate that our method
achieves comparable attack effectiveness under the same settings.

5.5 Stealthiness Comparison
We conduct a comparison between BadNets [8], Glassesdoor [3],
PTB [41], Robustdoor [15], PhyTrigger [40], and our proposed MBA
regarding attack stealthiness. Please refer to the Appendix for their
visual presentation. Considering that the stealthiness of triggers is
highly related to the human senses, we conducted a comprehensive
user study to evaluate them. Specifically, we divided into two groups
for comparison. The first group comprised 7 images (including a-e
and our two clothing items) where participants were informed about
backdoor attacks and asked to identify the presence of triggers.
The second group had 5 images of clothing (including two regular
clothing items, comparison methods [21, 22], and our two clothing
items) where participants were asked to determine which piece of
clothing appeared more suspicious. We collected responses from
60 participants for analysis.
As shown in Figure 11, for Clothing1 only 8.3% of the partici-
pants identified it as having a trigger, and none of the participants
considered it suspicious. Although Clothing2 performed slightly
worse (43.3%) in Group 2, it still outperformed the comparison meth-
ods [21, 22]. Based on the results, we can conclude that our trigger
appears more visually natural and exhibits excellent stealthiness.

6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Our proposed Moiré Backdoor Attack (MBA) offers a novel ap-
proach for generating Moiré pattern triggers that are simultane-
ously imperceptible and effective in backdoor attacks on pedestrian
detectors. Notably, the proposed method boasts high practicality in
real-world scenarios, as it solely relies on clothing items capable of
producing Moiré patterns to evade the detector. In this regard, we
conducted comprehensive experiments on two benchmark datasets,
employing two mainstream detectors, and the results show that
our MBA can perform effective and stealthy attacks in both digital
and physical space.
Broader Impacts. The proposed method successfully achieves
physical backdoor attacks on pedestrian detectors. The trigger used
is a common type of interference in image-capture sensors. Mali-
cious users can generate triggers for pre-trained models and use
them in stealthy attacks. Therefore, our goal is to identify poten-
tial risks when deploying the DNNs-based model into the physical
world, which is crucial for enhancing the security and robustness
of artificial intelligence.

A.3 Attack in the Physical World
To validate the applicability of our synthesized Moiré patterns in
the physical world, we conducted preliminary tests. Specifically, we
printed various base textures, including parallel lines and curves,
with different frequencies. A participant held the printed textures,
and we captured them using an iPhone 13pro camera. As shown in
Figure 15, it can be observed that these base textures, after the cap-
ture process, generated distinct Moiré patterns, effectively attacking
the backdoor detector.
These results demonstrate that the backdoor we implanted into
the target detector can trigger a diverse range of Moiré patterns
in the real world, leading to successful attacks. In this paper, we
selected two representative clothing items that generate prominent
Moiré patterns in the physical space to validate the proposed MBA.
