Moir ´e Vision: A Signal Processing T echnology
Beyond Pixels Using the Moir ´e Coordinate
Kensei Jo, Masahiko Inami
Abstract—Nowadays, many computer vision (CV) techniques require high-precision feature point detection in calibration targets.
Checkerboards or circle grids are common patterns used to infer the locations of the points directly from the image. However, such
approaches can lead to degradation in sub-pixel accuracy for low-resolution or sub-sampled sensor data (e.g., Bayer pattern). This
paper proposes a target-based calibration technique applicable to various conventional CV methods, leveraging the moir ´e phenomena.
We use a moir ´e pattern that is created between the digital camera’s pixels and the high-frequency texture on the calibration target.
Even a tiny movement of the target or camera leads to a signiﬁcant motion of the moir ´e, acting as a magniﬁer of the target texture for
estimating accurate location of targets or camera. In contrast to previous moir ´e approaches that employ frequency domain analyses,
we introduce the moir ´e coordinates and derive them from the difference between the sensor and target texture position. In the moir ´e
coordinate, each moir ´e pattern is undistorted. Unlike moir ´e in standard images, each moir ´e pattern has almost the same scale and
shape in these coordinates. Utilizing this property , we propose a straightforward algorithm to detect and handle correspondence in the
moir ´e peaks in image and texture location, treating them as feature points of calibration target. In addition, the moir ´e vision can magnify
pixels, which means the pattern in the moir ´e coordinate visualizes the pixel sensitivity with high spatial resolution. T o validate the
proposed approach, we present an empirical evaluation using simulated and real Bayer sensor patterns. We show the advantages of
the proposed method in terms of accuracy and identify promising future directions.
Index Terms —Moir ´e, Computer Vision, Computational Photography , Image Processing, Calibration, Marker

1 I NTRODUCTION
C
Amera sensors and computer vision (CV) algorithms
are deployed in various areas to measure, extract and
understand the information acquired from the environment.
A popular technique for calibrating intrinsic/extrinsic pa-
rameters uses calibration targets, which are printed patterns
on a ﬂat plane. Widely used patterns include checkerboards
or circular grids. Such targets help to develop methods to
locate feature points in the target and image. Usually , the
point’s locations in the target are known in advance, and
these are detected in captured images. While handy , such
approaches can lead to degradation in sub-pixel accuracy
for low-resolution or sub-sampled sensor data (e.g. Bayer
pattern).
Moir ´e are large-scale interference patterns observed by
discretely sampling data. When a digital camera captures
a cyclic pattern whose wavelength is almost identical to
the image sensor ’s sampling interval, the camera observes
the large low-frequency shapes. This pattern is called moir ´e.
Even small movements of less than one pixel of the camera
or target can cause a big displacement in the observed
moir ´e. Moir ´e has a long history , and many techniques use
moir ´e [1], [2], [3], [4]. However , these methods employ
dedicated algorithms and frequency domain analysis to
extract relevant information. For example, most can measure
a limited degree of freedom (DoF) displacement. And these
are challenging to compile with standard CV techniques
such as feature points of a calibration target.
T o the best of our knowledge, no one has attempted to
use the moir ´e phenomenon as spatial feature points for CV .
There are two issues with using moir ´e as feature points.

The ﬁrst is the static characteristics. The size and shape of
the moir ´e pattern highly depend on the relative position
between the camera and the target, which makes it difﬁcult
to detect the moir ´e in the captured image. The second is
the dynamic characteristics. The movement of the moir ´e
patterns is entirely different from standard imaging. For
example, even when a camera or target moves within a
small range, the observed moir ´e movement is enormous.
Therefore it is not easy to convert the moir ´e position from
image coordinates to the texture coordinates.
This paper proposes a novel approach that overcomes
these challenges while leveraging the moir ´e phenomenon
for high-sensitivity measurements for CV . Such an approach
can go well with many methods that use targets printed on a
ﬂat surface and placed at a certain distance from the camera.
Examples include but are not limited to, improving the
accuracy of measurements used in standard CV processes
such as camera calibration or Perspective-n-Point (PnP)
transformations.
The paper offers a CV technique based on a moir ´e
coordinate system. W e call it ”moir ´e vision.” W e present a
simple method to convert the captured image into the moir ´e
coordinate system. Each moir ´e pattern is normalized in the
coordinate domain to facilitate the detection of moir ´e peak
points. By doing so, the size and shape of the pattern will
be almost consistent and aligns the moir ´e peaks (brightest
points), thus solving the static characteristic problem. W e
estimate the moir ´e peak’s positions in the target plane from
their positions in the image and moir ´e coordinate, which is
different from the methods with the standard calibration
targets. The moir ´e is an aliasing pattern and does not
physically exists on the target plane. Still, since it is possible
to correlate the image and texture coordinates of the pattern,

2 R ELATED WORKS
Numerous image-based sensing methods use the location of
feature points. Here we describe the research with calibra-
tion target literature and the other moir ´e works.
General feature point detector without calibration
target: Many applications, such as SLAM (Simultaneous
Localization and Mapping) [8], detect landmarks or feature-
points without using a target. This type of application does
not require speciﬁc devices to work in various scenes. In this
area, many feature point detectors and descriptors are pro-
posed [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]. These algorithms are designed
to recognize natural shapes, not pre-designed patterns on
target. These methods are designed for robustness to image
warp, such as translation, rotation, and scaling. However ,
the localization accuracy depends on the scene, and gener-
ally , the liberation target-based method can achieve higher
performance.
Patterned illumination, Structured Light: Many meth-
ods use patterned illumination to create feature points.
Structured light is one of the typical examples, which
calculates the depth by identifying the projected pattern’s
location in the captured image. For example, Phase shift
projects multiple sine waves of different frequencies and
phases to identify pattern locations [14], [15]. Because the
correspondence’s accuracy is critical, there are various types
of research to locate the pattern [16], [17], [18]. T ypically ,
these methods detect sub-pixel locations of illuminated pat-
terns without aliasing, like moir ´e.
Shadow or projection moir ´ e: Moir ´e-based methods are
proposed to increase accuracy . The shadow or projection
moir ´e illuminates cyclic pattern and observes it through
a reference grating so that these two high-frequency pat-
terns create the moir ´e fringe [1], [2], [3], [4]. Although the
moir ´e looks like a large-scale pattern, it is made from high-
frequency grating. Thus, it has a high sensitivity to surface
geometry . Because of the sensitivity , there are several types
of works in this research area, such as a novel calibration
technique [19], [20] or applications of moir ´e [21], [22]. These
techniques measure the target surface without putting or
printing something on it. However , that requires illumina-
tion and grating.
2.1 Calibration target-based methods
Some methods capture pre-designed patterns and locate the
feature points on the calibration targets. Checkerboards or
circle grids are popular methods that achieve sub-pixel ac-
curacy and are used in various image processing algorithms
[23], [24], [25], [26]. Some markers send both position, ID
and small amounts of information simultaneously . This is
often used for Augmented Reality [27], [28], [29]. Some tech-
niques have been proposed to improve angular accuracy
by showing different patterns in each angular direction, but
it requires a lenticular lens, which limits its usable range
[30], [31]. These processes generally result in lower accuracy
when using lower-resolution sensors, e.g. resulting from the
mosaic pattern of Bayer coding.
Methods using moir ´ e: Unlike the shadow or projection
moir ´e, the sampling moir ´e measures precise surface 2D
displacement from a single image using the digital image
sensor ’s sampling interval as one of the high-frequency
patterns to generate moir ´e [32]. Several researchers ex-
tended the method furthermore, as observed in e.g. 3D
displacement with multiple cameras [33], [34], in-plane ro-
tation angle [35] or in-plane movement (1D-rotation and
2D-translation) [36]. Many researchers use these sampling
methods for their applications, such as measuring defor-
mation [37], [38] and curved surface residual stress [39].
Although these setups are similar to our methods, they
measure displacements or movements instead of position.
Thus, their measurable DoF (degree of freedom) is limited.
Jingyi et al. [40] measure 6-DoF position with moir ´e. Hou et
al. [41] measures lens distortion with moir ´e. However , these
methods use dedicated frequency domain or dedicated al-
gorithms, which are different approaches from conventional
feature points-based CV algorithms.
6-DoF position measurement: Moir ´e vision enables
the 6-DoF target position measurement with PnP [42], [43],
[44], [45], [46], [47]. SLAM or visual odometry are probably
the most famous methods for position measurement [48],
[49], [50]. In recent years, the method combining inertial
measurement units (IMU) has become mainstream and can
be used in many applications [51]. The moir ´e vision can
work only in front of the target but can accurately measure
the position.

3.3 Position deﬁnition
This paper deals with moir ´e phenomenon with a vision-
based approach using multiple coordinate systems. T o facil-
itate moir ´e computations, we deﬁne a normalized position ,
where pattern’s wavelength or sampling interval in each
axis will be one. xT , xS and xM represent the normalized
position of texture, sensor and moir ´e, respectively .
The position can be easily converted from the interval
or wavenumber of texture. Fig. 2 (a) shows an example of
a texture’s position whose horizontal wavelength is 2 [mm].
By multiplying the physical position pT with wavenumber
˜v = 1
2 [mm−1] we get xT . The wavenumber here represents
the number of pattern cycles per unit distance. For simplic-
ity , we choose the origin of texture coordinates as one of the
white square centers in the texture grid in a way that every
center takes an integer value in xT as shown in Fig. 2(a).
The sensor position xS can be calculated similarly as in Fig.
2 (b). Unlike textures, the sampling interval can be changed
later by sub-sampling the acquired image. For instance, the
xS needs to be multiplied by two because each color pixel
on the sensor is only repeated with a period of 2 pixels due
to the Bayer pattern. This is apparent in Fig. 2 (c).
Note that the normalized position is the same as phase
divided by 2 π. As the phase, the pattern repeats a period
of 2 π. As the position, the pattern repeats at wavelength
1. This paper uses position to address moir ´e in a spatial
domain approach.
In this paper , we refer to the green (G) pixels horizontally
next to red (R) and blue (B) as Gr and Gb, respectively . W e
use Bayer ’s raw images and use Gr and Gb as different
channels so that each color has the same moir ´e effect.
This paper ’s colorized images are shown only for display
purposes, but four channels are processed separately in
the calculation stage. Note that moir ´e visibility depends
on sensor ’s sampling frequency . Usually , Bayer ’s colorized
images have moir ´e in R and B color because they have a

1) Find the texture coordinates corresponding to pixel lo-

2) W arp image into the moir ´e coordinates with Eq. 1.

4) Reﬁne moir ´e position. W e select PM as the closest point
from Pinit,M, whose fractional position is (0. 5, 0) for
Gr because the error of position is expected to be small.

know the ideal position in the moir ´e coordinates so that
we can estimate the texture position as, PT = PS +PM .

4.2 T arget texture design
T o observe moir ´e, the texture wavelength should be almost
the same as the sensor ’s sampling interval. For example, if a
VGA sensor observes a target that ﬁlls the sensor plane, the
target should have a grid of about 640x480.
Handling large optical blur. When the camera’s blur
kernel is large, it is challenging to observe high contrast
moir ´e, as exempliﬁed in Fig. 5 (a). Because the large blur
eliminates the high-frequency pattern, Iblur(xS) is low in
contrast. Even if the camera location changes, it still ob-
serves a distorted low-contrast image of Iblur(xS).
Fig. 5 (b) results from 2 times sparser texture and
sampling pattern. Recall changing the sampling interval is
possible by sub-sampling the captured image. The same
technique is introduced in sampling moir ´e [32]. In case the
lens blur is large, we recommend to sub-sample the sensor ,
e.g. at every second pixel.

design. Higher performance can be achieved by designing
the targets according to the camera parameter and location.
Initial position measurement. The proposed method
uses conventional targets to measure the initial position.

coordinate system. However , if the initial position error is

5 T HEORETICAL ERROR ANALYSIS
In this section, we show that the effect of moir ´e peak
measurement error is small. Additionally , we demonstrate
how the error in moir ´e coordinate ∆x M can quantify
the measurement or reprojection error . Even though these
errors are too small to be measured directly with standard
computer vision techniques, moir ´e vision can observe it due
to its high resolution as there are numerous sampling points
in one moir ´e pattern.
5.1 Moir ´e peak measurement error
Let’s think about how the measurement error surfaces in the
target plane. Suppose the intrinsic and extrinsic parameter
is correct. The P is a position of target moir ´e peak, and
Q is the measured point that contains the measurement
error . Recall that the proposed method estimates the moir ´e
peak’s texture location from its sensor coordinates ( QS), so

conventional imaging, the corresponding error is ∆x QP,T ,

ideal texture point which corresponds to the point QS can
be calculated as QT = QS + QM . However , since the point
P is the goal and the point Q was measured, the calculated
texture coordinates RT are obtained by using the position
of P , such as RT = QS + PM . Therefore, the measurement
error for the proposed method ∆x measure.,T = RT − QT

Previous analyses assume intrinsic / extrinsic parameter
were correct, but usually this is not the real case. W e show
that the moir ´e coordinates are beneﬁcial for visualizing the
error due to incorrect camera calibration parameters, as
shown in Fig. 7. W e ignore the measurement error because
it results in a small error if the moir ´e size is appropriate.
Calculating the total derivative of Eq. 1, we get
∆ xM = ∆ xT − ∆ xS. (3)
W e want to measure how a single point contributes to the re-

can get the following equation by substituting zero for ∆ xS
in Eq. 3, such that
∆ xrepro.,T = ∆ xM . (4)
This shows that the error in the moir ´e coordinate ∆ xM is
identical to the reprojection error in texture coordinates.

Evaluation methods. W e capture several moir ´e images,
measure errors ∆ xM and depict them on the images with

Results and discussions. Fig. 14 depict the results in

The chromatic aberration can explain this result. All red,

has a larger error in the corner area. The result demonstrates
that the error can be measured by moir ´e vision qualitatively .
In future work, we would like to extend this with a quanti-

7 C ONCLUSION
W e proposed moir ´e vision, a novel image processing tech-
nique in moir ´e coordinates, where the pattern is identical
to the high-resolution image of the undistorted and the
blurred texture grid. The method enables using moir ´e peaks
as feature points of the calibration target to improve the
accuracy for various CV techniques, such as lens calibration
and 6-DoF position detection, and to visualize reprojection
or color aberration errors in greater detail. W e evaluated the
moir ´e vision technique using simulated and real images.
The results show that, unlike conventional methods, our
approach can increase accuracy with appropriate target
design, even for sub-sampled sensor data.
However , moir ´e vision has some limitations. The target’s
design depends on various parameters such as sensor res-
olution, FoV and target’s location. The accuracy depends
on the moir ´e’s visual size in the image. Thus, there is a
trade-off between accuracy and spatial information. A larger
moir ´e yields higher accuracy but decreases the number of
moir ´e, measurement points. Therefore, this method is more
appropriate to analyze a single type of camera in detail.
Finally , we note that it is better to process raw camera data
because modern digital cameras involve a lot of image post-
processing to generate color images, e.g., gamma correction
and contrast enhancement. In our evaluation, we used raw
data and treated Gr and Gb as different channels such that
all R, Gr , B and Gb channels have same sampling intervals
and observer moir ´e with the same target.
