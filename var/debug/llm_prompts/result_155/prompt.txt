Abstract—Automated manufacturing is the cornerstone of
the Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) ecosystem, where
vibration monitoring technology is a critical tool for main-
taining industrial machinery. The prevailing approach mostly
employs inertial measurement units (IMUs), lasers, and cameras,
each demonstrating deployment constraints. In recent years,
millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar has shown high vibration
measurement performance, but it faces challenges in accurately
localizing vibrating objects and determining observation points.
This study introduces a new system called VibCamera, which
leverages the mmWave vibration measurement technology with
computer vision (CV) algorithms for vibration monitoring.
With the positional assistant of CV semantic segmentation, the
radar can accurately determine sufﬁcient observation points,
thereby achieving precise measurement with high directional-
ity. VibCamera includes two camera modes, RGB-only and
RGB+depth, and solves two technical challenges: 1) integrat-
ing multimodal information for vibration target localization
and 2) extracting high-quality vibration signals in interference
environments. VibCamera provides more consistent and precise
outcomes without the need for physical contact. The experimental
results indicate that the RGB-only mode has amplitude and
frequency errors below 27.04 µm and 0.22 Hz, respectively, with
a 90% probability, and the RGB +depth mode has errors below
23.72 µm and 0.21 Hz.
Index Terms—Millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar, multimodal,
vibration measurement.

C. VibCamera in Nutshell
To overcome the limitations of existing solutions, this article
presents VibCamera, a mmWave-camera fusion system for
vibration monitoring. As shown in Fig. 1, the most concise
structure of VibCamera consists of two subsystems. During
the target detection phase, the camera subsystem recognizes
the object of interest and generates a positional reference,
combining it with the mmWave radar’s detection results to
identify the observation points. Then, the mmWave subsystem
utilizes its high spatial resolution and high displacement
sensitivity to process the signals from the observation points to
obtain phase curves for vibration characteristics measurement.
The measurement results can be displayed in a visually
intuitive manner as shown. The implementation of the camera
in the system yields instant beneﬁts. It is able to accurately
recognize and localize vibration objects and easily correspond
measurement results to different objects or parts. Its position
reference can help ﬁlter out the wrong radar point clouds
and supplement a large number of observation points, thereby
signiﬁcantly improving measurement performance.
D. Novelties and Advantages
VibCamera opens up new routes for mmWave-based mea-
surements, and its novelties are demonstrated by addressing
the following two critical challenges.
1) How to realize high-precision localization of vibration
targets. The detection of vibrating objects serves as the
foundation for measuring vibrations, and is challenging
in mmWave-based methods. Typically, there are two
issues that can arise. First, there may be a lack of
sufﬁcient spatial bins or point clouds for detecting
vibrating objects. Second, some of the detection results
may not actually pertain to the intended target. Our
solution consists of four sequential steps. We utilize the
vision semantic segmentation technique to detect the
object in the RGB image. Subsequently, an inﬂation
algorithm is implemented to expand the detection region
in order to encompass the target more comprehensively.
We extract the pixel depths from the depth image in
order to compute their 3-D coordinates, which will then
be grouped together into clusters. The nontarget point
clouds can be eliminated based on the clustering. Finally,
we fuse the detection outcomes obtained from both the
mmWave subsystem and the camera subsystem. One
approach involves utilizing the pixel boundaries obtained
from a camera to selectively process mmWave point
clouds. On the other hand, mmWave radar will increase
the number of observation sites inside the image area.
Through the above measures, VibCamera can determine
accurate target positions and generate a large number of
effective observation points.
2) How to ensure the high quality of vibration sig-
nals. Occasionally, the initial millimeter wave signal
may suffer from subpar quality due to a variety of
causes, resulting in potential measurement inaccuracies
or complete malfunctions. Multipath effects and spectral
leakage may cause the phase arc’s center to shift; the
nonradial displacements of the vibration object lead to
inconsistency in the arc center before and after the turn-
ing of the vibration; additionally, the hardware startup
differences create a gap in the interframe phase arcs.
These three aforementioned factors contribute to chaotic
signal phases, complicating the extraction of accurate
phases. We propose a complete phase arc correction
algorithm that can extract pure phase curves representing
vibration from chaotic signals. This algorithm includes
turning point search, data reconstruction, and center shift
correction. Furthermore, we need to eliminate observa-
tion points that do not provide sufﬁcient information
for accurate vibration measurement. We recommend
utilizing the peak prominence of the vibration signal’s
spectrum as a means of screening the signal to guarantee
the retention of only effective signals. As a result, our
combined signal optimization algorithms can generate
optimal phase curves to ensure stable and accurate
vibration measurement.
The advantages of VibCamera over previous works can be
summarized as follows.
1) Compared to mmWave-only solutions, introducing com-
puter vision (CV) techniques greatly improves the
capabilities of target recognition and observation point
localization, making measurement results stable and
accurate.
2) Compared to the solution using corner reﬂectors [20],
VibCamera avoids installing equipment on vibration
objects, thus maintaining the noncontact advantage
and reducing the deployment complexity. With 90%
probability, the amplitude and frequency errors of the
RGB-only VibCamera are below 27.04 μm and 0.22 Hz,
while the errors of VibCamera with depth camera are
below 23.72 μm and 0.21 Hz, respectively.

B. Phase Arc in IQ Domain
The IQ domain is the representation of a signal by
decomposing it into two orthogonal components. These two
components are I and Q components. The I and Q axes are
perpendicular to each other and form a 2-D coordinate system.
IQ domain provides a convenient way to represent signals with
amplitude and phase information. The length of the origin-
signal point vector represents the amplitude, and the angle with
the x-axis represents the phase. Assuming an ideal situation,
the radar receives only the reﬂected signal from the vibration
source, the amplitude of the reﬂected signal is constant, and
the phase varies linearly with displacement. At this point, the
phase curve of the signal is shown in Fig. 3, and it appears
as a circular arc in the IQ domain, with the radian of the arc
representing the range of phase variation. The circular center
of actual phase arcs usually shifts from the origin, therefore
the circle ﬁtting is usually used to correct the center. Let

With its focus on assigning pixels to their respective
objects, object segmentation stands as a fundamental CV
application. Leveraging its exceptional prediction precision,
this technology ﬁnds valuable application in vibration object
recognition and localization tasks. By harnessing the power of
object segmentation, we can accurately identify and localize
vibrating objects, enabling more precise analysis and decision-

Fig. 4 summarizes the basic structure of the VibCamera

Then, VibCamera optimizes the signals of the observation

B. Target Localization
In the target localization stage, the radar and camera subsys-
tems will separately detect the position of the vibration object,
and then the observation points for vibration monitoring will
be obtained based on the results of the two subsystems. Fig. 7

1) Radar Point Cloud Generation: In order to utilize the
velocity information earlier in object detection and thus focus
on the weakly vibrating objects, this article adopts the so-
called 4D-FFT point cloud generation process. This process
involves radar signal processing processes called Range-FFT,
Doppler-FFT, and Angle-FFT. We suggest readers refer to
relevant sources [23] for understanding their functions. First,
we apply Range-FFT to the IF chirp signals to separate the
space in the range domain. Subsequently, we perform Doppler-
FFT to detect the velocity information in each range bin. Once
we have obtained the range-Doppler map (RDM), we utilize
the 2-D constant false alarm rate (CFAR) algorithm to detect
valid targets and generate RD point clouds, which are shown
in Fig. 5. Finally, we perform 2-D Angle-FFT on the signal
of each RD point based on the multiple-input–multiple-output
(MIMO) antenna array to obtain its azimuth and elevation
angles, and transform them into 3-D point clouds, as shown
in Fig. 6.
2) Object Detection via RGB Image: Along with radar
detection, VibCamera uses the RGB image to detect and
localize the vibration object by implementing the semantic
segmentation model. To ensure that the camera system can

C. Signal Optimization
After obtaining the position of the observation points,
VibCamera extracts signals of each observation point based
on its distance and angles. During this process, we employ
the 2-D Capon beamforming algorithm, which accurately
estimates the spatial spectrum by minimizing the variance of
the output power, thereby effectively suppressing interference
signals and improving the beamforming capability. The sig-
nals’ representation in the IQ domain involves mapping the
real part to the x-axis and the imaginary part to the y-axis.
Reciprocating displacement causes periodic phase changes,
while slight displacement in a short period of time does not
result in signiﬁcant changes in signal strength. Therefore, in
an ideal scenario, reciprocating motion manifests as an arc
centered at the origin within the IQ domain. Fig. 11(b) shows
a phase arc that has been optimized to approach the ideal
state. The central angle of this arc, denoted as θ, corresponds

1) Phase Arc Correction: When dealing with phase arcs,
we need to address three practical problems. First, The center
of the arc is not at the origin. The reﬂected signal received
by the radar is a superposition of all signals in space. In
other words, in the IQ domain, the reﬂected signal from
the vibration source is superimposed on the vector of the
ambient reﬂected signal, which causes the arc center shift.
The spatial segmentation capability of the FMCW radar is
able to attenuate the effect of the ambient signals, but it
cannot eliminate them completely. The phase angle of an arc
is calculated relative to its center, therefore the center shift
causes phase distortion and even serious measurement errors.
Second, a turn in the direction of displacement may cause a
change in the arc center. This is because vibration equipment,
such as centrifuges, actually vibrate along a 2-D orbit, and a
turn in their radial displacement does not represent the return
of the original path, which results in differences in signal
features before and after the turn. Third, there is discontinuity
in interframe arcs. The hardware startup of the radar varies
slightly from frame to frame, and the signal extraction on
a frame-by-frame basis also causes slight differences between
frames, which may result in gaps between the arcs of adjacent
frames.
To solve the above problems, we propose the following
phase correction idea. First, identify the possible turning points
in each frame. Then, connect the arcs of adjacent frames.
Finally, perform circle ﬁtting on the arc between each two
turning points to ﬁnd its center. In the ﬁrst step, it is difﬁcult
to ﬁnd a suitable perspective to capture the arc turn due to
the unknown circle center shift. Therefore, we propose a two-
step ﬁtting method for the detection of turning points. The
two-step ﬁtting includes a circle ﬁtting and a straight line
ﬁtting. As shown in Fig. 12, we ﬁrst perform circle ﬁtting on
the IQ points of one frame. The initial values are set based
on experience with the data distribution. Considering that the

2) Signal Screening: Not every observation point is suit-
able for vibration measurement. On the one hand, the target
detection results of the camera subsystem may contain nontar-

On the other hand, even observation points belonging to vibra-

we propose a signal screening method based on spectrum
quality. This method is based on the fact that the higher the
signal quality, the closer its phase curve is to a sine wave,
and therefore its energy in the frequency domain will be
concentrated near the vibration frequency, which appears as
a prominent peak in the frequency spectrum. We convert the
phase curve to the frequency domain by FFT and smooth it to
suppress burrs. Then, we perform peak and valley detection.
For an effective signal, the spectrum should contain a peak
that have a peak-valley value that is much greater than the
peak-valley value of other peaks, as shown in Fig. 14(a).

vibration. In signal screening, we have obtained the spectrum

the vibration frequency f

peak-valley values of the phase curve. The phase curve may
contain strong low-frequency components, so it is necessary to
perform a detrending process. We calculate the median curve
with a sliding window of 10 periods of vibration (i.e., 10/ f

and subtract it from the phase curve. In this way, we achieve
the baseline regression of the phase curve so that it ﬂuctuates

the phase curve, the vibration amplitude can be calculated by

E. Summary of Measurement Point Selection
The core competitiveness of VibCamera lies in its accurate
localization of vibration objects and selection of suitable
measurement points. Here, we review and summarize the
related operations as follows.
1) Camera Module: After obtaining the RGB image, the
camera identiﬁes and localzies the vibration object, and deter-
mines the angle limit of the object. When there is a depth
camera, we cluster the visual point cloud to remove points that
do not belong to the object and obtain more accurate angle
limit for the object.
2) Radar Module: Radar obtains 3-D point clouds and then
clusters them to ﬁlter out clutters. Then, the radar retain the
cluster representing the vibration object based on the angle
limit provided by the camera system. Subsequently, the radar
cluster is fused with the angle limit to generate a measurement
point plane.
3) Measurement Module: In the measurement process,
VibCamera will perform the ﬁnal ﬁltering on the measurement
points. By analyzing the frequency spectrum of vibration
signals, points with poor signal quality can be ﬁltered out to
ensure the accuracy of measurement results.

E. Implementation Issues and Suggestions
We have validated the performance of the VibCamera
system at the algorithmic level. However, there are sev-
eral issues that need to be considered when deploying the
VibCamera system in practice.
1) Setup and Calibration: On the one hand, cameras and
radars should undergo lens distortion calibration and
antenna array calibration separately to ensure measure-
ment accuracy. On the other hand, precise coordinate
transformation should be performed between the two
systems to align the spatial point cloud.
2) Synchronization and Computation: In static scenes, the
requirement for synchronization is not high because
the camera only needs to take one photo. However,
in camera-radar integrated automation devices, such as
SLAM robots, strict synchronization is required to align
the measurement data of the two systems on the timeline.
This also requires a reasonable allocation of computing
resources to maintain the simultaneous operation of the
SLAM system and vibration measurement system.
V. R
ELATED WORK
A. Vibration Measurement
Various technologies have been employed in traditional
vibration measurement, each with its advantages and limita-
tions. IMU systems [7] necessitate direct installation on the
object, whereas laser sensors [8], [27] are better suited for
detecting vibrations at a single point. High-deﬁnition cameras
[9], [10] require appropriate lighting conditions and proximity
to the object being measured to capture subtle vibrations
effectively. RF-based methods have shown promising poten-
tial in vibration measurement. RFID technology [11], [12]
naturally has the signal-object association ability, but faces
challenges in detecting small displacements because of its
large wavelength. UWHear [15] utilizes UWB technology to
distinguish sound sources through certain light-building mate-
rials. Chen et al. [28] conducted experimental investigations
on vibration measurement based on various RF technologies.
B. mmWave-Based Measurement
The high frequency and high sampling rate of mmWave
radar endow it with an excellent ability to detect small
displacements. Previous studies have investigated diverse
techniques to improve signal processing efﬁciency and
enhance vibration measurement accuracy [29], [30], [31].
Mikhelson et al. [32] analyzed vibration signals in the IQ
domain and proposes a ﬁtting method to eliminate static
components, improving the accuracy of signal processing.
Bi et al. [16] introduced a digital phase shifter for multi-
arc circle ﬁtting, which further enhanced the precision of
signal processing in mmWave-based vibration measurement.
Chen et al. [17] studied the optimization of IQ arcs from
the perspective of noise conversion. mmVib [18] presents the
idea of chirp reconstruction, which enhances the sensitivity
of mmWave signals to small displacements and has had a
profound impact on this ﬁeld. GWaltz [19] extracts signals
from reﬂected objects and multipath ghosts in RAM, allowing
for multiangle vibration observations using a single radar
and getting the 2-D vibration orbit. Multi-Vib [20] achieves
multipoint vibration monitoring by installing tailored corner
reﬂectors on the object. This method improves the ability
of vibration target localization, but introduces deployment
challenges and lacks directionality in the measurement results.
In addition to vibration measurement itself, the high dis-
placement sensitivity of mmWave radar has also given rise
to other applications. Some studies develop communication
technologies based on vibration monitoring [33], [34],o r
propose object recognition [35], [36] and integrity sensing [37]
technologies based on vibration features. Ding et al. [38] used
mmWave for rotation speed measurement. ThermoWave [39]
measures temperature based on RF response of special materi-
als. The synthetic aperture imaging of mmWave has also been
widely studied [40], [41].
C. mmWave-Camera Fusion
Various studies have explored the beneﬁts of combining
mmWave sensing with vision-based approaches. Simultaneous
localization and mapping [42] and autonomous driving [43]
are hot topics in multimodality. VPBCT [44] leverages vision-
measured vehicle positions to infer the optimal beam pair for
transceivers, thereby improving communication performance
in mmWave systems. Li et al. [45] utilized mmWave
radar cross section calculations to distinguish pedestrian
or humanoid signs on the road. Wang et al. [46] com-
bined mmWave-based train speed calculations with loop
closure detection results from camera data to achieve accu-
rate train localization. ReID [47] analyzes human gait data
acquired from both vision and mmWave radar and improves
performance in cross-modal human reidentiﬁcation.
VI. C
ONCLUSION
This article presents a cutting-edge VibCamera system that
integrates mmWave vibration measurement technology with
powerful CV algorithms to achieve reliable and precise vibra-
