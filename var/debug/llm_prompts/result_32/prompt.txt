Abstract
Airflows, heat plumes, and gas leaks are ubiquitous yet invisible to
both human eyes and commodity cameras, limiting visual sensing of
environmental dynamics critical to safety, comfort, and automation.
Traditional Schlieren imaging reveals air refractive-index varia-
tions but requires bulky, expensive optics and high-end cameras,
restricting its use to controlled lab settings. We presentMoiréLens,
a practical Schlieren imaging system that works in real-world envi-
ronments by capturing Moiré interference with a low-cost embed-
ded camera. By embedding high-frequency, human-imperceptible
Moiré stimuli into everyday wallpaper, MoiréLens converts sub-
tle refractive-index variations into measurable Moiré phase shifts
formed between the background and the camera’s color-filter array.
An automatic calibration module maintains stable, high-sensitivity
Moiré pattern generation under camera viewpoint misalignment
by dynamically adjusting the displayed Moiré stimulus. A color
Moiré extraction and Moiré-to-Schlieren conversion pipeline iso-
lates Moiré patterns from wallpaper and reconstructs refractive-
index variations with adaptive spatial-temporal sensitivity. Exten-
sive experiments show that MoiréLens achieves high-sensitivity
Schlieren imaging for both regional airflows and global plumes.
Using a 1-megapixel camera, it localizes a 1.5 hPa gas leak at 0.8
m and butane from an unlit lighter at 1 m, extending the sensing
range by 3×over background-oriented Schlieren. Leveraging heat-
plume visualization, MoiréLens further classifies dry- and moist-
heat cooking with 99.8% accuracy and estimates oil temperature
with an average error of 7.10◦C under unseen heating powers.

1 Introduction
Everyday environments are filled with invisible air movements and
variations that shape human comfort, safety, and energy efficiency,
from the heat rising from a stove, to gas leaking from a burner, or air
jets flowing through a ventilation outlet. Detecting and visualizing
such flows is essential for applications, such as cooking automa-
tion, HVAC monitoring, and environmental safety [6]. However,
conventional temperature and gas sensors provide only point mea-
surements, responding slowly to changes and lacking the spatial
resolution needed to capture how air, heat, or gas actually propagate
through space [15, 16]. In contrast, Schlieren imaging is an optical
technique that visualizes these invisible phenomena by detecting
minute air-density variations, arising from changes in tempera-
ture, pressure, or gas composition, and converting refractive-index
gradients into perceptible brightness variations [27].
Practical Schlieren imaging systems for real-world environments
need to satisfy three key requirements: First, they need high sensi-
tivity to detect subtle refractive-index variations across meter-level
camera–scene distances. Second, they must maintain robustness to
ambient lighting and camera placement changes to operate reliably
under diverse and dynamic conditions. Third, they should be low-
cost and easily deployable on commodity mobile platforms and IoT
devices, using optical components that are compact and visually
unobtrusive to humans.
Conventional optical Schlieren systems, while highly sensitive,
rely on expensive mirrors, lenses, and knife-edge filters that de-
mand precise alignment and stable illumination [27], limiting their
use to laboratory environments. Background-Oriented Schlieren
(BOS) mitigates these hardware constraints by replacing optical
assemblies with patterned backgrounds and estimating pixel dis-
placements between reference and distorted frames [24]. However,
BOS still faces critical challenges: it requires dense, high-contrast,
and visually intrusive backgrounds; its sensitivity is constrained
by camera resolution and background texture; and its performance
degrades under nonuniform lighting or large depth-of-field. Table 1

In this paper, we present MoiréLens, a Schlieren imaging sys-
tem that leverages Moiré interference to enable high-sensitivity
flow visualization in real-world environments. As shown in Fig. 1,
MoiréLens operates on a background-camera pair: the background
can be either (i) a digital pattern rendered on a display or(ii) a phys-
ical pattern mounted on a wall, while the camera can be either (i)
fixed at a known location or(ii) carried on a mobile platform (e.g., a
household or industrial robot). In the physical-background setting,
a fixed camera needs to be placed at the designated location rather
than an arbitrary one. Airflow disturbances in the space between
the background and camera create refractive-index variations that
bend light rays as they traverse thermal or gaseous flows, intro-
ducing minute geometric distortions into the captured background
image. By embedding high-frequency fringes into the background,
these distortions strongly modulate the interference between the
background fringes and the camera’s Color Filter Array (CFA), pro-
ducing a Moiré effect that amplifies the distortions into measurable
phase shifts and thereby makes invisible flow fields observable.
MoiréLens faces the following key challenges.
1) How to maintain stable Moiré patterns under varying cam-
era–background alignment? Generating high-quality Moiré pat-
terns requires precise geometric alignment between the camera
and background, as even slight deviations can introduce distor-
tions that degrade Schlieren reconstruction. To address this, we
design AutoMoiré, an automatic calibration and control module
that continuously adjusts either the rendered background patterns
or the background-to-camera geometry to maintain optimal Moiré
formation. AutoMoiré computes a homography mapping from back-
ground to camera coordinates and pre-warps the fringe background
so that the camera perceives an undistorted Moiré grid. When a
physical background (e.g., printed wallpaper) is used, AutoMoiré
instead controls the robot to adjust the camera’s position and ori-
entation, achieving equivalent alignment and Moiré visibility. By
compensating for minor deviations caused by mechanical impre-
cision of the robot, AutoMoiré ensures consistently stable Moiré
patterns for reliable Schlieren reconstruction.
2) How to embed high-frequency patterns into a background wall-
paper that remain invisible to humans while ensuring reliable Moiré
extraction on cameras? Directly overlaying strong, high-frequency
patterns into everyday wallpapers would be visually distracting in
daily spaces, undermining the practicality and social acceptability
of continuous deployment. We exploit the insight that Moiré-based
Schlieren imaging is less sensitive to background contrast, allowing

2 Related Work
Sensing Using Moiré Patterns : Moiré patterns have been used
in visual sensing because small geometric displacements yield
large, measurable phase shifts in the fringes [5, 19–22, 33, 36, 37].
Existing designs typically generate Moiré patterns in two ways:
(i) Tag-based (stacked gratings). Two high-frequency gratings are
rigidly stacked on a passive marker to produce stable, high-SNR
interference: MoiréBoard enables low-cost, calibration-light 3-DoF
tracking [33]; MoiréTag supports angular/6D snapshot tracking via
chirped patterns [22, 37]; MoiréWidgets provide interactive controls
such as buttons, sliders, and dials [ 5]. Because both gratings are
rigidly bonded, these approaches are sensitive to solid motion but
insensitive to minute refractive-index perturbations in air.(ii) Single
high-frequency grid ×camera CFA. Interference between a camera
CFA and a high-frequency grid background enables ultra-precise
pose estimation and tracking [19–21]. MoiréPose targets 6-DoF lo-
calization [19], and MoiréVision extends this concept to curvilinear
patterns with sub-pixel feature extraction [20]. MoiréVib demod-
ulates periodic fringe motion for micro-vibration sensing [21]. To
our knowledge, MoiréLens is the first to employ Moiré patterns for
sensing air refractive-index perturbations.
Thermal and Gas Airflow Imaging. Traditional thermal airflow
sensing relies on contact-based thermometers and anemometers,
which provide only single-point measurements and require dense
deployments to capture spatial temperature fields, impractical for
large or cluttered environments. Non-contact approaches instead
aim to capture temperature and flow variations remotely. Infrared
(IR) thermography measures emitted radiation to map surface tem-
peratures, but it cannot visualize volumetric airflow and remains
costly [39]. Schlieren imaging detects refractive-index gradients
caused by thermal or density variations [17], yet traditional setups
demand precise mirror alignment and knife-edge optics, restricting
their use in everyday environments [17]. BOS relaxes these optical
constraints by tracking displacements in textured backgrounds [23],
but it sacrifices sensitivity, depends heavily on background contrast,
and typically requires high-end Single-lens Reflex (SLR) cameras to
achieve adequate SNR [13, 27]. A detailed comparison between BOS
and MoiréLens is presented in §3.2 and Table 1. Acoustic thermom-
etry offers another non-contact modality by estimating temperature
from sound speed variations [ 3, 4, 9, 31], but typically demands
dense sensor arrays and controlled conditions. MoiréLens achieves
non-contact thermal airflow sensing using only a low-cost camera and
patterned wallpaper, offering an unobtrusive, and scalable solution
for IoT and robotic systems.

Applications for Schlieren Imaging. Schlieren imaging has been
widely adopted across scientific, industrial, and safety domains for
non-intrusive visualization of refractive-index variations caused
by heat or gas flow [27]. In scientific and engineering research, it
supports fluid-mechanics studies of convection, turbulence, and
combustion, offering particle-free diagnostics of temperature and
density gradients [ 13, 23]. In industrial and environmental sys-
tems, they are used for HVAC testing, cleanroom airflow inspection,
and gas-shielding evaluation in welding and additive manufactur-
ing [13]. Health and safety applications include respiratory-flow
visualization for infection control [34] and gas leakage detection
[29] for early fire prevention. Unlike traditional methods confined
to controlled settings, MoiréLens aims to bring Schlieren imag-
ing to real-world environments, enabling low-cost, unobtrusive
visualization of thermal and gas flows using only commodity cam-
eras and wallpaper-embedded Moiré patterns. We demonstrate that
MoiréLens can support everyday and industrial applications such
as cooking oil detection, oil temperature regression, butane gas
and compressed-air detection, and heater airflow visualization in
uncontrolled daily environments.
3 Primer and Preliminary Study
This section introduces the optical principles of Moiré-based
Schlieren imaging and compares it with the conventional BOS
method.
3.1 Primer on Synthetic Schlieren
Schlieren imaging is a classical optical method for visualizing fluid
flows with spatially varying refractive indices. Traditional Schlieren
setups rely on expensive optics and carefully aligned light sources,
making them difficult to deploy outside laboratories [17]. In con-
trast, synthetic Schlieren offers a low-cost alternative that can be
realized with commodity devices such as off-the-shelf cameras and
printed background patterns [ 27]. Fig. 2 shows the basic setup,
where a camera is placed in front of a structured background. Vari-
ations in air density or temperature induce refractive-index distur-
bances, which distort the background pattern captured by cameras.
Computational analysis of these distortions then reconstructs the
flow field.

3.2 Moiré-based Solution v.s. BOS
Building on the model above, we now compare the Moiré-based
solution against the well-known BOS technique [23].
BOS. BOS visualizes refractive-index gradients by applying a ran-
dom texture to a background and comparing the distorted image
(with flow) to a reference frame [13]. The apparent displacement
field Δ𝑢, caused by angular deflections𝛼, is estimated through pixel
registration between the two images [ 24]. The accuracy of this
displacement estimate depends on two background parameters: the
root-mean-square (RMS) contrast 𝐶, which measures pixel-intensity
variation, and the spatial bandwidth 𝐵(cycles/pixel), which quanti-
fies the texture’s finest spatial detail. Based on the literature [23],
the displacement uncertainty decreases with increasing 𝐶 and 𝐵:

In practice, BOS sensitivity is constrained by several factors:(1) Low
SNR at large distances ( 𝑠): as the camera moves farther from the flow,
the apparent displacements become smaller and the effective spatial
bandwidth decreases ( 𝐵 ↓). (2) Illumination variability: changes
in lighting, shadows, or glare can easily degrade image contrast,
reducing displacement accuracy (𝐶 ↓). (3) Restricted background
design: BOS relies on visible random textures, which are impractical
in many real-world environments. For example, embedding such
textures into wallpaper to remain visually unobtrusive inevitably
reduces both contrast and spatial bandwidth (𝐶 ↓,𝐵 ↓). Therefore,
existing BOS approaches have to use high-end SLR cameras with
high contrast black-and-white backgrounds [13, 27].
Moiré-based solution : To address these issues, we introduce a
novel background design, referred to as Moiré stimulus , which
generates Moiré patterns in captured frames to enable super-
resolution synthetic Schlieren. Specifically, we employ a periodic
high-frequency grid as the background. The key insight is to ex-

4 System Design
MoiréLens leverages the aforementioned advantages to realize
Schlieren imaging using low-cost cameras embedded in IoT de-
vices and household robots operating in uncontrolled environ-
ments. Figure 4 illustrates the overall workflow ofMoiréLens. § 4.1
presents the stable and controllable Moiré stimulus generation de-
sign through coordinated geometric pre-warping and scale search
to enable easy deployment without strict camera alignment. § 4.2
introduces the invisible embedding technique, which integrates
Moiré stimulus into wallpapers so that the patterns remain im-
perceptible to humans yet produce measurable Moiré patterns on
camera. § 4.3 details the adaptive Moiré-to-Schlieren visualization
pipeline. Finally, § 4.4 describes the feature extraction and machine
learning models used for downstream application tasks.
4.1 Moiré Stimulus Calibration and Generation
We first design the Moiré stimulus, i.e., the high-frequency grid pat-
terns rendered on digital or physical background.The key objective is
to ensure stable Moiré pattern formation with a fixed spatial scale, de-
spite unknown and dynamically varying camera-to-background align-
ment and distance. To this end, we developAutoMoiré, a calibration-
and-rendering module that automatically generates the optimal
Moiré stimulus. For digital backgrounds, AutoMoiré continuously
captures video and uses visual feedback to calibrate geometric align-
ment and render the optimal stimulus in real time. For physically
printed stimuli, where the background is immutable, AutoMoiré
instead assumes a camera mounted on a mobile robot and adjusts
its pose to achieve stable Moiré formation. In the following, we
focus on the digital-background case, while the printed-background
scenario can be regarded as its inverse, where the camera pose is
calibrated rather than the stimulus itself.
4.1.1 Camera-to-Background Geometric Calibration. We consider
scenarios where the background–camera alignment is unknown . We
found that even small camera pose deviations can introduce mis-
alignment between camera and background, significantly altering
the resulting Moiré patterns. To quantify this effect, we place the
camera at a distance of 𝑙 =100 cm from the digital display and ad-
just its orientation by5◦and 10◦along the yaw, pitch, and roll axes,
respectively. As shown in Fig. 5, each type of rotation produces a
distinct Moiré pattern deformation. These discrepancies cause sub-
stantial variations in Moiré pattern stability and sensitivity, which
ultimately degrade the robustness of MoiréLens, particularly for
downstream tasks that rely on data-driven ML models.
To address this, we design a calibration method that maps back-
ground coordinates to camera image coordinates before rendering
the Moiré stimulus. Let s =[𝑢𝑠,𝑣𝑠,1]⊤denote background coor-

printed background, this calibration is performed once using a

4.2.2 Color Moiré Separation from Wallpaper. When the camera
captures the patterned wallpaper,MoiréLens must isolate the Moiré
patterns from the wallpaper texture and color, to prevent interfer-
ence during processing. Our approach exploits a counterintuitive
phenomenon known as color Moiré . When the captured Moiré cells
are relatively large, the resulting patterns exhibit vivid false colors
even though the Moiré stimulus is monochrome. For example, a
purely black-and-white Moiré stimulus may appear as alternating
magenta–green bands in the captured image (Fig. 6). The root cause
lies in the design of most commodity RGB cameras, which employ
a Bayer CFA (typically RGGB) where each pixel captures only one
color component. Missing components are reconstructed through
bilinear demosaicing, a key stage in the image signal processing (ISP)
pipeline [12]. For a grayscale scene, all RGB channels should ideally
be identical; however, aliasing between the CFA and the Moiré
stimulus causes demosaicing to misinterpret the aliased structure
as color variations, producing vivid false-color artifacts [8].
Our key observation is that even monochrome cameras, despite
lacking a Bayer CFA, often reuse the same ISP pipeline as RGB
cameras. While negligible for standard imaging, this becomes cru-
cial when capturing Moiré stimuli: the ISP still assumes an RGGB
layout and expands grayscale values into RGB space, causing demo-
saicing to generate vivid magenta–green artifacts from red–blue
channel interactions. Fig. 7d shows this effect using a monochrome
camera (Arducam OV9281): the wallpaper remains grayscale, while
the Moiré patterns appear in false color. This finding informs our
design: a monochrome camera with a standard ISP pipeline can auto-
matically separate Moiré patterns from the wallpaper through color
differentiation. In practice, we convert the RGB image to the CIELAB
color space [26], where the 𝑎∗ channel (red–green axis) highlights
Moiré color transitions while suppressing the grayscale wallpaper,
as shown in Fig. 7e.

proposed separation algorithm is mainly influenced by two factors:
(1) Wallpaper Properties. First, wallpaper’s inherent texture fre-
quency needs to be different from that of the Moiré stimulus. How-

the stimulus and produce false-color artifacts. Second, wallpaper’s
brightness is also important. The background must be captured
without per-channel saturation or underexposure, as luminance

cess, lowering the SNR further. To quantify these effects, we an-

for dynamic flows (e.g., thermal plumes and turbulence).
Temporally stable disturbances. When the refractive field
evolves slowly over time, such as during gas diffusion, the dis-
turbance is quasi-static relative to the frame rate. MoiréLens con-
structs a background model using an Exponential Moving Average
(EMA) over a short initialization window (typically 5 frames at
100 fps, ≈50 ms). Subsequent frames are compared against this
background in the CIELAB 𝑎∗ channel, maximizing sensitivity to
small phase shifts. The resulting difference map reveals persistent
refractive-index distortions as a steady Schlieren visualization.
Dynamic disturbances. For rapidly varying refractive fields, such
as convective airflows, heat plumes, or cooking-induced turbulence,
MoiréLens applies inter-frame differencing between consecutive
frames in the 𝑎∗ channel. This suppresses static background com-
ponents while accentuating transient color-phase changes, yielding
clear, real-time visualization of evolving flow patterns.
4.3.3 Adaptive Visualizations Pipeline. Figure 9 summarizes the
complete adaptive Moiré-to-Schlieren visualization pipeline.
(1) Spatial adaptivity by adjusting the Moiré stimulus. Based on the
spatial objective, MoiréLens renders the Moiré stimulus with the
appropriate 𝑚tgt: larger 𝑚tgt for global plumes and turbulence, and
smaller 𝑚tgt for regional airflows.
(2) Temporal adaptivity by adjusting the visualization strategies. De-
pending on temporal characteristics, either EMA-based background
subtraction or inter-frame differencing is applied to extract refractive-
index–induced perturbations.

4.4 Downstream Application Processing
Finally, the Schlieren visualizations produced by MoiréLens are
processed using ML models to support various downstream applica-
tions. To demonstrate end-to-end usability, we evaluateMoiréLens
on two representative cooking-automation tasks.
4.4.1 ROI Feature Extraction. Compared to visualization, ML infer-
ence requires temporally stable input features. We design a feature-
extraction pipeline that converts Schlieren frames into consistent,
low-variance representations. For each frame, we first apply the
inverse homography 𝐻−1 from AutoMoiré to warp the image into
the background coordinate system and resize it to the background
resolution. We then crop the central region as the ROI, where Moiré
patterns exhibit minimal distortion. Last, we downsample the ROI
and extract the CIELAB 𝑎∗ channel as the input feature.
4.4.2 Task 1: Dry vs. Moist Heat Cooking Classification. Moist-heat
cooking uses water or steam (e.g., boiling, simmering, steaming,
braising), whereas dry-heat relies on surface contact or oil (e.g.,
searing, roasting, stir-frying, deep-frying) [28]. Identifying these
two modes enables range-hood automation, which should activate
only during dry-heat cooking to improve safety, comfort, and en-
ergy efficiency. This task classifies each cooking episode as moist-
or dry-heat based on Schlieren visualizations. We focus on stages
where the liquid temperature exceeds 80◦C, producing heat and
fume plumes that MoiréLens can reliably capture.
Preprocessing. We use a 5-second window for each prediction.
Each window is transformed into a single feature map via: (1) per-
frame mean-absolute-deviation (MAD) normalization, (2) inter-
frame differencing, and (3) pixel-wise variance aggregation over
time.
DNN model. We train a lightweight 2D CNN for binary classifi-
cation. The backbone consists of three Conv–BN–ReLU–MaxPool
blocks. The classification head flattens the feature maps and applies
two fully connected layers with dropout, followed by a final layer
that outputs two logits. The model is trained with cross-entropy
loss. Detailed network parameters are provided in the Appendix[1].
4.4.3 Task 2: Cooking Oil Temperature Regression. We further eval-
uate MoiréLens ’s ability to perform fine-grained thermal estima-
tion. Real-time oil-temperature regression is a critical proxy for
cooking safety and quality, enabling automatic heat control, pre-
venting overheating, and supporting adaptive ventilation [15]. We
perform this task using Schlieren visualizations of rising heat and
fume plumes, focusing on the temperature range of 80∼240◦C,
which covers the typical smoke points of common cooking oils [35].
Preprocessing. We follow the same preprocessing pipeline for each
5-second window as in Task 1, except that we replace pixel-wise
variance aggregation over time with: (1) a first-order Butterworth
low-pass filter to suppress spatial Moiré texture and (2) a temporal
median filter to remove temporal camera-induced outliers.
DNN model. For regression, we adopt an R(2+1)D-style archi-
tecture [30], a widely used spatiotemporal convolutional design
for video analysis. Three R(2+1)D blocks extract spatiotemporal

6.3 Wallpaper Invisibility
Wallpaper invisibility is determined by spatial resolution and view-
ing distance. We evaluate this using retinal resolution, with60 pixels
per degree (PPD) [32] as the perceptual limit for 20/20 vision. For
our 32-inch 4K display (138 DPI), we simulate visual blur by apply-
ing a Gaussian low-pass filter to attenuate frequencies beyond the
Nyquist limit, then resample the wallpaper to the target 60 PPD
for various distances. We compare MoiréLens (𝛽 =0.8) (see §4.2)
against a standard high-contrast random-dot BOS background (2.5-
pixel dots) [13]. Crucially, we maintain the high contrast of the BOS
background because lowering it would disproportionately reduce
BOS sensitivity(see §3.2), ensuring a fair comparison.
Metrics. We use LPIPS as the evaluation metric [38]. The LPIPS
score ranges from 0 to 1, where lower values indicate higher per-
ceptual similarity. Following established perceptual thresholds [38],
we define human imperceptibility as LPIPS ≤0.02.
Results. Table 3 shows the average results on 40 selected wallpa-
pers. When the viewing distance exceeds 1.8 m, the LPIPS approaches
the imperceptibility threshold, indicating that wallpapers with the
embedded Moiré stimulus appear visually imperceptible to humans.

7 Conclusion
We presented MoiréLens, a practical Schlieren imaging system
that repurposes commodity cameras and unobtrusive wallpaper
backgrounds to visualize invisible air refractive-index changes. Ex-
tensive evaluations demonstrate that MoiréLens points to a new
direction for transforming everyday cameras into accessible sen-
sors for visualizing air-flow and refractive-index dynamics. As fu-
ture work, we aim to replace the background requirement with a
projector-based configuration that follows the same optical princi-
ples while removing the need for distributed hardware.
