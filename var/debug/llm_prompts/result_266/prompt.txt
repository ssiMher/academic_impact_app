TWLip: Exploring Through-Wall Word-Level Lip
Reading Based on Coherent SISO Radar
Dongsheng Zhu , Chong Han , Member, IEEE,J i a nG u o , and Lijuan Sun
Abstract—Recently emerged radio frequency-based lip-reading
recognition technologies leverage their independence from light-
ing and penetration capabilities to expand the applications
of lip-reading. Unlike visual-based lip-reading, this technology
penetrates barriers, such as masks, glass, and wood to detect
lip movements. However, the previous studies utilizing devices
like millimeter-wave radar face limitations due to frequency
and power consumption, which restrict the types of penetrable
materials and application scenarios. Although low-frequency
through-wall radar offers signiﬁcant penetration capabilities, it
has reduced sensitivity to small movements, posing a challenge
for lip-reading recognition. Moreover, the high cost of radar
equipment and the scarcity of commercially available devices
hinder the technology’s development. To address these challenges,
we propose TWLip, a word-level lip-reading recognition system
utilizing coherent single-input and single-output through-wall
radar to detect tiny lip movements behind walls. We utilize
I/Q 3-D curves derived from the radar signals corresponding
to lip movements as the network input. These curves reﬂect
the amplitude, frequency, and rotational characteristics of lip
movements in the complex plane. Furthermore, we designed
the IQResNet, built with 1-D preactivation residual bottleneck
units, to extract and classify lip-movement features from I/Q
3-D curves. We propose a data-augmentation method for radar
lip-reading to enhance model efﬁcacy and generalizability. We
created a through-wall radar lip-reading data set containing 20
words from eight volunteers, totaling 9583 samples. The TWLip
demonstrated the ability to recognize these words through a
24 cm brick wall from two meters away with 88.51% accuracy,
validating the algorithm’s superiority through detailed compar-
ative studies.
Index Terms—Coherent radar, lip-reading recognition, neural
networks, through-wall sensing.

II. R
ELATED WORK
Lip-reading recognition aims to infer spoken content by
analysing lip movements. Researchers have developed vari-
ous methods to capture lip movement signals, encompassing
different signal acquisition methods, such as cameras, sound
waves, and RF. Among these, camera-based visual lip-reading
recognition dominates. However, the introduction of the RF
technology has made through-wall lip-reading recognition pos-
sible. This section primarily introduces the current mainstream
technologies for lip-reading recognition based on the vision-,
acoustic-, and RF-based methods.
A. Visual-Based Lip-Reading Recognition
Most lip-reading recognition work is performed based on
the vision. In the initial stages, researchers mainly employed
manual feature extraction techniques to identify lip movements
from the videos or images and subsequently used support
vector machines as the traditional classiﬁers to translate these
features into text [11], [12]. With the advancement of deep
learning in recent years, neural networks have been widely
applied in lip-reading recognition tasks for both the auto-
matic feature extraction and classiﬁcation. Assael et al. [13]
introduced a spatiotemporal neural network composed of
convolutional neural network (CNN) and gated recurrent unit
networks aimed at sentence-level lip reading. Wang et al. [14]
developed a lip-reading method based on a 3-D convolutional
visual transformer, which effectively extracts spatial and tem-
poral features from continuous images, signiﬁcantly enhancing
lip-reading performance. Feng et al. [15] incorporated an
audio modality that allowed the lip-reading model to learn
speech-related information from the audio signals, improving
the performance of visual speech recognition. Despite the
excellent performance of vision-based lip-reading recogni-
tion, it requires high-quality lighting conditions and cannot
work effectively with occlusions, which limits its application
scenarios.
B. Acoustic-Based Lip-Reading Recognition
Acoustic-based lip-reading recognition has emerged as a
novel research area in recent years and is predominantly uti-
lized for the human–computer interactions on the smartphones.
Gao et al. [16] introduced EchoWhisper, a silent speech
interface for smartphones that analyses the micro-Doppler
effects generated by mouth and tongue movements. The
smartphone microphone and speaker capture the acoustic char-
acteristics of the reﬂected echoes to recognize silent speech
content, identifying more than 45 different words with a word
error rate of 8.33%. Similarly, Zhang et al. [17] proposed
SoundLip, which leverages the built-in speaker and micro-
phone of smart devices to emit and capture inaudible sound
waves, capturing the user’s lip movements. They designed a
hierarchical CNN (HCNN) and a multitask encoder-decoder
network to achieve lip-reading recognition at both the word

C. RF-Based Lip-Reading Recognition
RF-based lip-reading recognition technology has shown
great potential, especially in environments with obstructions
and insufﬁcient lighting. RF signals can penetrate obstacles,
providing new scenarios for the application of lip-reading

preprocessing procedure to extract lip movement features and

related studies utilized millimeter waves to penetrate low-
loss wall materials (such as wood, glass, and soundprooﬁng

movements. Millimeter-wave radar, characterized by its short
wavelength and high sensitivity can detect the minute vibra-
tions of a speaker. However, its sensing capability is limited,
and it cannot penetrate through the thicker brick walls.
The longer the wavelength of RF signals is, the stronger
their ability to penetrate walls, however their capacity to
perceive small targets diminishes. This poses greater demands
for the low-frequency through-wall radar systems that capture
lip movements. This article proposes TWLip, which uses
1-2 GHz low-frequency through-wall radar. Comprehensive
experiments were conducted in unobstructed, free space, and
through 24-cm brick walls, realizing the detection of lip
movements of targets 2 M away from the radar and achieving
a classiﬁcation accuracy of 88.51% for 20 words.

V. I Q RES NET MODEL DESIGN
In the research on applying deep learning to radar sensing,
image datas, such as radar heatmaps [23], spectrograms [24],
and range-Doppler maps [25] are commonly used as the input
data for the neural network models. These data types can
express features well in some coarse-grained sensing tasks or
high-sensitivity mmWave radar sensing. However, in scenarios
of through-wall lip-reading sensing due to the lower signal
frequency, there is insufﬁcient sensitivity to perceive minor
movements, leading to weak image features in the heatmaps
or Doppler images. To extract the faint lip movement features
of targets behind walls, we propose a lip-reading recognition
model based on an 1-D CNN, IQResNet, to extract and classify
the 3-D lip movement curve features.
The IQResNet model consists of multiple stacked 1-D
PARB units as shown in Fig. 7. The model is divided into
two parts: 1) the I/Q fusion and 2) the fusion of multiple
range bins. The input to the model is three lip movement
curves. First, three independent I/Q fusion parts each extract
lip movement features within a range bin; then, the features
from the three range bins are fused and further extracted;
and ﬁnally, lip-reading classiﬁcation is performed through a
multilayer perceptron and a softmax layer.

A. PARB Unit
The core component of the IQResNet model is the PARB
unit as shown in Fig. 8, which consists of three 1-D con-
volutional layers with the kernel sizes of 1, 3, and 1. The
preactivation residual bottleneck block concept was initially
proposed by He et al. [26]. The distinctive design feature
of this module is the placement of the activation function
before the convolutional layers within the residual block,
which can improve the ﬂow of gradients in deep networks
and is suitable for deeper network architectures. Moreover, the
bottleneck structure reduces the number of parameters in the
network. Considering the differences between the I/Q data and
images, especially the positive and negative characteristics of
the I/Q data, we replace the ReLU activation function in the
standard residual block with a parametric rectiﬁed linear unit
(PReLU) [27].
PReLU is deﬁned as follows:

where yi represents the input to the neuron and αi is a
learnable parameter for determining the activation strength
for the negative input values. In the standard ReLU, all the
negative inputs are directly set to zero, whereas in PReLU, the
negative inputs are scaled by this learnable parameter. This
allows the network to adaptively adjust its response to the
negative inputs during training. This approach is particularly
suitable for processing the I/Q signals that contain the negative
values.
B. I/Q Fusion
Speciﬁcally, this section ﬁrst independently processes the
I/Q signals of the lip movement curve through the two
branches, each containing an 1-D CNN with a stride of 2 and
a kernel size of 30, a batch normalization (BN) layer, PReLU,
and two PARB units with a stride of 2. A stride-2 PARB unit
refers to a residual structure where the stride of the ﬁrst-layer
1-D CNN is 2. Unless speciﬁcally mentioned, the stride for
the 1-D CNNs within a PARB is uniformly 1. Considering
the temporal correlation of the I/Q signals, which jointly
represent the impact of lip movements on the radar signals,
these two branches share weights. These two branches are
then concatenated along the channel dimension, merging the
features learned from the I/Q channels individually. The fused
features subsequently pass through the two PARB units with
a stride of 2, and two PARB units for feature dimensionality
increase.
C. Multiple Range Bins Fusion
The lip movement curves in different range bins exhibit dis-
tinct patterns, and the features fused from the I/Q components
vary accordingly. Therefore, it is necessary to integrate the
features fused from the I/Q components further across different
range bins. This part initially performs element wise addition
of the features outputted by the three I/Q fusion components,
thereby effectively consolidating and enhancing the lip-reading
features across various range bins. Subsequently, the features
are dimensionally reduced through the multiple PARB units.
In IQResNet, the activation function for each layer employs
PReLU and utilizes a BN layer. Finally, in the MLP, two fully
connected layers are used with dropout to prevent overﬁtting.

The FMCW signal source generates a linear sawtooth-
waveform FMCW signal with a bandwidth of 1 GHz, a
sweep time of 100 us, and a pulse repetition frequency of
1000 Hz. The signal, ampliﬁed by a power ampliﬁer (PA), is
transmitted through the transmitting antenna, reﬂected off the
lips, and then captured by the receiving antenna. After being
ampliﬁed by a low noise ampliﬁer (LNA), the captured RF
signal is mixed with the local oscillator signal from the signal
generator, producing an intermediate frequency (IF) signal.
The DSP part processes this IF signal and outputs it through
a low-pass ﬁlter, producing a beat signal containing target
information. An FPGA and an analog-to-digital converter
(ADC) collect and process the beat signal. Similar to the
previous works [28], [29], the FMCW signal generator outputs
a trigger signal, the rising edge of which is synchronized
with the start of the sweep of the signal source. This signal
triggers the ADC to start sampling, thus segmenting the beat
signals of different cycles. However, as the clock driving the
sampling part differs from the clock driving the signal source,
this method introduces a slight phase shift in the beat signals

introduce a reference clock, which is used to synchronously
drive the FMCW signal source, ADC, and FPGA, ensuring
that the phase information of the IF signal across different
sweep cycles can be accurately synchronized and processed.

C. Implementation Details
In this study, to train and evaluate the proposed model, we
initially partitioned the data set at a ratio of 9:1, i.e., 90%
of the data were used as the training set with the remaining
10% served as the test set. For the training set, we employed
a k-fold cross-validation strategy, where the value of k was set
to 10. This implies that the training set was evenly divided
into ten subsets, with one subset being used as the validation
set in each iteration and the remaining nine subsets being used
for training. This method makes full use of the training set
and reduces the risk of overﬁtting by repeatedly training and
validating on different subsets. Furthermore, evaluations across
multiple iterations make the results more robust, minimizing
the bias caused by randomness in the data division and
thus providing a more accurate assessment of the model’s
performance.
The parameter size of IQResNet is 7.16 M. During the
training process, a batch size of 256 is utilized on the GPU,
and Adam [30] with a learning rate of 1 e
−4 is the optimizer.
In our experiments, the same random seed, set to 15, is used
for all the instances involving randomness.

E. Comparison With Other Methods
In the TWLip study, the IQResNet network was constructed
to extract the 3-D curve features of lip movements. In the
ﬁeld of lip-reading recognition based on the wireless signals,
some studies have shown excellent recognition effects, such as
for SoundLip [17] and mSlient [6]. Although the application
scenarios and sensing devices differ, the core of these two
studies lies in extracting wireless signal features through
deep learning for lip-reading recognition. SoundLip utilizes
acoustics for silent lip-reading recognition and uses an HCNN
to extract lip movement features from the acoustic amplitude
and phase signals. mSlient is a silent speech recognition study
based on the mmWave radar that involves constructing a multi-
branch convolutional network (MCN) to extract articulatory
gestures from the multiscale detrended spectrogram (MSDS)
of the mmWave radar. Additionally, in ﬁelds, such as image
recognition and speech recognition, some mature CNNs, such
as VGG [31] and ResNet [32], have been developed.
In this section, we compare IQResNet with these methods
on our data set. Moreover, the input to IQResNet is switched
between the amplitude and phase to evaluate the impact of
different inputs on the recognition performance. Likewise, I/Q

VIII. C
ONCLUSION
This article introduces the TWLip system, an innovative
through-wall, word-level lip-reading system based on the
coherent SISO radar. The TWLip system has demonstrated
its signiﬁcant efﬁcacy in through-wall lip reading through the
extensive research and experimental validation, indicating its
potential applications in smart homes, security surveillance,
and postdisaster rescue. The IQResNet neural network model
we developed is designed to process lip movement features in
radar signals and shows superior recognition accuracy com-
pared to the traditional methods. Moreover, the adoption of
various data augmentation methods has enhanced the model’s
robustness and generalizability in different environments. The
experimental results show that the TWLip can achieve a
recognition accuracy of up to 88.51%. The system exhibits
adaptability to new environments and individuals, and effec-
tively reduces the cost of data collection through the transfer
learning strategies, thus achieving improved performances.
However, this research also has several limitations. First,
environmental interference and individual differences remain
the primary challenges affecting the recognition accuracy.
Although data augmentation methods can improve the model’s
adaptability, the system’s performance may still be limited in
extreme or complex environments. Second, the current system
designs and experiments mainly focus on a limited set of
words and environments, which might not be directly extrapo-
lated to sentence recognition or more complex environmental
conditions.
The primary task for the future research will be to further
optimize and improve radar signal processing algorithms
and deep learning models, which will be key to enhancing
the system performances. Speciﬁcally, researching how to
more effectively process and reduce the impact of envi-
ronmental noise and other interferences may signiﬁcantly
increase the system’s accuracy and reliability. Expanding
the data set to include more corpora and a comprehen-
sive range of environmental conditions will help improve
the system’s generalization capability. Although TWLip has
already demonstrated its potential in through-wall lip-reading
recognition, the future research still holds considerable room
for further development and expanded possibilities.
