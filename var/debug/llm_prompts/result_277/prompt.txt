Eavesdropping
Zhimin Mei†, Donghui Dai †, Jingyu Tong†, Zheng Gong †, and Lei Yang † ‡ ∗
†Department of Computing, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University

Abstract—Acoustic eavesdropping presents a longstanding
challenge in the realm of personal information security and
privacy preservation. In this work, we introduce a novel
eavesdropping method called JerryAttack, which repurposes an
optical mouse as a covert eavesdropping device. Specifically,
we transform the mouse’s integrated low-resolution but high-
frame-rate image sensor into a high-speed camera for visual
vibrometry, capable of capturing acoustic vibrations from nearby
loudspeakers. Our contributions are threefold: First, we utilize
the ‘pixel grabber’ register as a backdoor to extract the pixel
stream from the image sensor. Second, we establish an acoustic-
optical side channel that enables effective acoustic eavesdropping.
Third, we thoroughly explore two attack scenarios: voice profiling
and speech reconstruction. Our findings reveal that the sound
recovered through our side channel achieves a mean SNR of
7.3dB, comparable to standard microphone recordings in noisy
environments like cafes. Additionally, when combined with a clas-
sification neural network, JerryAttack identifies individuals with
an overall accuracy of 83.27% across six languages. Moreover,
when cooperated with joint channel information, JerryAttack
consistently achieves good intelligibility, with a median STOI
score exceeding 0.7 in reconstructed results.
Index Terms—side-channel attack, acoustic eavesdropping, IoT
security
I. I NTRODUCTION
Acoustic eavesdropping remains a significant security
threat, employing stealthy techniques to intercept private
conversations. Various methods leverage diverse channels to
achieve this, such as motion sensors [1]–[7] and wireless
signals [8]–[13], both exploiting different vulnerabilities to
access sensitive audio data. Further, the use of a camera to
capture mechanical vibrations caused by soundwaves, known
as visual vibrometry, has been extensively investigated over
the years [14]–[21]. However, not all cameras are suitable for
acoustic eavesdropping. The Nyquist sampling theorem stipu-
lates that the sampling rate must be at least twice the highest
frequency of the signal. Phonetic research indicates that vowels
contain the primary energy in speech, and distinguishing dif-
ferent vowels often requires comparing their first two formants,
which are distinct frequency components of the sound. The
average second formant frequency for approximately 81.25%
of vowels is around 1.5 kHz or lower [22]. Consequently, to
perform accurate acoustic eavesdropping, a camera capable of
achieving frame rates of 3 kHz or higher is necessary.

In this work, we revisit acoustic eavesdropping through vi-
sual vibrometry by exploiting an underutilized acoustic-optical
side channel: the optical mouse. Common in computing setups,
the optical mouse incorporates a low-resolution digital camera
to capture images of the surface below it. As the mouse moves,
it sequentially records images, which are then processed by
an onboard digital signal processor (DSP). The DSP analyzes
these images to detect patterns or shifts in position over time,
allowing it to calculate the direction and speed of the mouse’s
movement. Driven by the demands of gaming applications,
modern optical mice are designed to support exceptionally
high frame rates, often exceeding 3.7 kHz. Furthermore, the
optical sensor typically focuses on a small area (e.g. 3.1 mm2)
within its pixel array (e.g., 26 ×26), allowing the mouse to
detect subtle variations at the submillimeter level induced by
structure-borne soundwaves. The high frame rate and finely
focused sensing area characteristics highlight the significant
potential of optical mice as tools for visual-vibrometry-enabled
acoustic eavesdropping, a capability that has remained largely
untapped until now.

attack scenario. We envision a typical setup where a target’s
workspace includes an optical mouse adjacent to a loudspeaker
device, either integrated or external, both placed on a shared
surface like a desk. The sound of the speakers causes vibra-

However, effective implementation of this acoustic eaves-
dropping framework faces two main challenges:
• How to extract pixel values from optical mice? An optical
mouse typically incorporates an image sensor paired with
a DSP that preprocesses images to detect movement. This
system traditionally outputs coarse-grained movement data,
such as displacements along the X and Y axes measured
in pixels. This standard setup generally lacks the sensitivity
required to detect the fine, subtle vibrations caused by the
sound from speakers. Visual vibrometry requires access to
raw texture images captured by the sensor, but typical optical
mice do not provide a high-level interface to access these
images directly. This limitation poses a significant challenge
for recovering the sound from acoustic vibrations.
Upon reviewing technical datasheets, we made an unex-
pected discovery: mainstream optical sensors used in optical
mice, such as the ADNS-3050 [23], include a special register
called the ‘pixel grabber’. Originally intended for debugging
or testing, this register captures and retains the value of a
single pixel from each image frame captured by the sensor. By
accessing this register, we can tap into a continuous and stable
stream of pixel values at a high frame rate of 3.7 kHz. This
access effectively turns the register into a backdoor for data
extraction. Leveraging this capability allows us to overcome
significant obstacles in accessing detailed image data, essential
for sound recovery. For more technical information, see § III.
• How to recover sound from the pixel stream? The eco-
nomical, low-resolution image sensors (e.g., 26 × 26 pixels)
integrated into these mice are prone to thermal noise and
inherent harmonics, severely masking the subtle vibrations that
are indicative of sound within the pixel data.
To navigate this challenge, our initial approach involves
leveraging traditional signal processing methods to accurately
model the channel, followed by the implementation of a
basic recovery algorithm. This foundational step sets the
stage for further refinement. To enhance the initial audio
recovery, we utilize a band-split recurrent neural network
(BSRNN) [24]. This advanced neural network architecture is
specifically tailored to address the unique constraints of the
pixel stream, including limited resolution and noise distortion.

A. Attack Scenarios
We consider an attack scenario in which the victim is
engaged in an online conversation or a group meeting within
a meeting room. An attacker, positioned in a nearby room,
aims to eavesdrop on the private information and sensitive
details in the victim’s speech. The victim uses a loudspeaker

desk surface to a compromised mouse. These vibrations induce
subtle distortions in the imagery captured by the mouse’s
onboard camera. To pose a practical threat, the attacker must
overcome the distortions introduced by this hidden acoustic-
optical channel and successfully extract the sensitive data.
Specifically, the attacker could establish two types of attack:
(1) V oice Profiling aims to extract the private information
related to the victim, see the evaluation details in § VI. (2)
Speech reconstruction tries to recover the speech signal to a
level understandable to humans, see § VII.

III. T RANSFORMING MICE INTO HIGH -SPEED CAMERAS
A. Background of Optical Mice
Optical Mice serve as crucial peripherals for computer inter-
action and Fig.2(a) illustrates the internal structure, featuring
components such as an optical sensor with pixel array, a small
LED, and a printed circuit board (PCB). Fig.2(b) explains the
working principle. Specifically, the LED projects light through
the optical lens onto the desk surface. The reflected light,
carrying surface texture, is captured by the image sensor’s
pixel array, which converts the light intensity into grayscale
values. The onboard digital signal processor (DSP) processes
these images to detect changes between consecutive frames,
thus determining the mouse’s displacement.
B. Acquiring Pixel Stream
Optical mice are equipped with a compact image sensor that
comprises a matrix of pixel diodes. Unlike regular cameras that
boast millions of pixels, the image sensors in optical mice
have only a few hundred pixels because they are primarily
used to compare differences, not to capture details. However,
when these mouse cameras lack pixel count, they compensate
with an exceptionally high frame rate, far surpassing regular
cameras. Optical mice typically support frame rates of 3 kHz
or higher, which may reach up to 20 kHz in models designed
for gaming.
For illustrative purposes, we will use the Logitech G402
optical mouse as an example, however, the datasheet for its
optical sensor AM-010 is not public. Fortunately, AM-010 can
be considered a variant of ADNS-3050 (since AM-010 is a
variant of PMW-3320 [33], which in turn is a variant of the
ADNS-3050 [34], [35]), while the biggest difference is that
the pixel array size increases from 19 × 19 of ADNS-3050
to 26 × 26 of AM-010. Therefore, this paper will refer to
the datasheet of the ADNS-3050 for explanations [23], which
integrates more than 30 registers.

IV. D EVELOPING THE ACOUSTIC -OPTICAL SIDE
CHANNEL
A. Channel Model
Speaker-generated vibrations manifest as structure-borne
waves, a type of mechanical wave that transport en-
ergy through solid materials by causing particle interac-
tions within the medium. There are three main propagation
modes: Rayleigh waves, longitudinal waves, and transverse
waves [36]. In our scenario, the mouse detects textures mainly
affected by Rayleigh waves, which cause the solid surface to
float up and down [37], making the distance between the image
sensor and the desktop fluctuate accordingly. This interaction
between the loudspeaker and the mouse essentially forms a
classic backscatter system where the loudspeaker modulates
the light intensity with varying amplitudes and the image
sensor captures these amplitude-modulated data.
Single-Pixel Sampling: We start from a simplified scenario
where the image sensor contains only one single pixel. In the
absence of an acoustic signal, this pixel maintains a steady
light intensity, denoted p. However, the backscatter system
results in a fluctuating light intensity p · S(t) captured by the
pixel at the moment t, as Fig. 4(a) shows.

Given a complex-valued spectrogram X ∈ CF×T , it is
segmented into K distinct frequency bands, denoted as

segments of 100 Hz each, the frequency range 1 ∼2 kHz is
divided into five segments of 200 Hz each, and the remaining
range is divided into 400 Hz sub-bands. Subsequently, all
the frequencies sub-band Wk are transformed into real-valued
features Zk ∈ RN×T via layer normalization module (Norm)
and fully connected (FC) layer, where N donates the feature
dimension. The spectral features from all K bands are then
integrated into a composite feature tensor Z ∈ RN×K×T .
(3) Correlation via RNNs . To capture correlations within
the time and frequency dimensions of the signal, we employ
two distinct RNN modules. Initially, for time correlation, an
RNN module processes the feature tensor Z along the time
axis. Following this, a second RNN module does the same
operation along the frequency axis. Despite their different
targets, both RNN modules share a similar structure, beginning
with a layer normalization, followed by a BiLSTM layer and
a fully connected layer. A residual connection is also included
to improve the gradient flow and the final output is represented
as Q ∈ RN×K×T .

VII. A TTACK SCENARIO II: S PEECH RECONST .
While the recovered sound retains features recognizable
by neural networks, it may not always be comprehensible to
humans. To evaluate speech intelligibility, we use the Short-
Time Objective Intelligibility (STOI) measure [48], which
correlates the amplitude envelopes of clean and processed
speech across various frequency bands to assess intelligibility.
A. Speech Intelligibility
The values of STOI range from 0 (completely unintelligible)
to 1 (perfect intelligibility). Speech quality is categorized
as ‘poor’ (below 0.5), ‘fair’ (0.5-0.6), ‘good’ (0.6-0.8), and
‘excellent’ (above 0.8) based on the STOI scores, as illustrated
by the 4 background colors in Fig. 15. Additionally, the figure
shows the STOI distribution (depicted with a red dotted line) of
speeches recovered only using the side channel information,
predominantly falling in the ‘fair’ and ‘good’ quality. This
limited intelligibility is largely attributed to some stubborn
inherent harmonics in JerryAttack that are still difficult to
filter out by neural networks.
To improve intelligibility, we propose a joint attack that
combines our side channel with a direct eavesdropping chan-
nel, such as a microphone. In this scenario, the attacker
strategically positions a microphone at a significant distance
from the victim, potentially attaching it to an exterior wall or
using a directional microphone array to capture sound from
afar. Due to this distant placement, the SNR of the direct
channel is often too low for effective speech reconstruction on
its own. We concurrently capture the same speech through both
the side and direct channels and then reconstruct the speech,
as depicted in Fig. 8(c). This strategy leverages the strengths
of two audio sources: the negligible internal harmonics of the
direct channel and the robustness of the side channel to air-
borne noise. By combining these advantages, it overcomes the
limitations of each channel when used independently, resulting
in accurate speech reconstruction that is even understandable
by humans.

telligibility of the reconstructed speech indirectly. As depicted
in Fig. 16, a similar trend emerges across different languages
where a direct channel SNR of 7.1 dB helps achieve an average
accuracy of up to 90%. Conversely, a 4.83 dB SNR yields an
accuracy slightly above 60%, showcasing the positive impact
of higher SNR levels on speech recognition accuracy.
VIII. D EFENSE
This section discusses various technical defence measures
that can be employed to enhance security and mitigate the
risks associated with eavesdropping.
Measure I : The discovery that the ‘pixel grabber’ register
within optical mice is primarily inactive during standard oper-
ations suggests its original intent was for debugging and sensor
integration testing. This feature, while seemingly innocuous,
inadvertently presents a security loophole, effectively acting
as a ‘backdoor’ for acoustic eavesdropping in our study.
To mitigate this vulnerability, manufacturers should consider
disabling or securely encapsulating this testing interface in
commercial products to prevent unauthorized access and po-
tential data breaches.
Measure II : Employing anti-vibration materials on mouse
pads or other surfaces can significantly reduce the transmission
of sound to optical mice. This measure is particularly crucial in
high-security areas to protect against acoustic eavesdropping
by absorbing vibrations that could be maliciously analyzed.
IX. R ELATED WORK
The existing eavesdropping systems can be grouped into
three categories:
(1) Motion Sensor-based: Such attacks have been the sub-
ject of extensive research in recent years [1]–[7]. Michalevsky
et al. [1] introduced ‘Gyrophone,’ showing that smartphone
gyroscopes could capture speech vibrations. Zhang et al. [2]
developed ‘AccelWord,’ using accelerometers for voice recog-
nition. Anand et al. [3] found that only speech generated
by loud speakers significantly affects motion sensors. Hu et
al. [4] introduced AccEar, which uses a cGAN to reconstruct
audio from accelerometer signals, while Ba et al. [5] proposed
AccelEve, a deep learning-based system for speech recognition
from accelerometer data. Kwong et al. [6] demonstrated that
mechanical components in hard drives can act as unintended
microphones. Yao et al. [7] showed that accelerometers can
even eavesdrop at 5 Hz sampling rates. A key limitation of
these studies is that motion sensors typically operate at 100-
500 Hz, while JerryAttack utilizes a mouse optical sensor
with a sampling rate greater than 3 kHz, allowing for better
eavesdropping quality.
(2) Wireless Signal-based: Wireless signals have also been
exploited by researchers for sound recovery [8]–[13]. Hu et
al. [11] introduced mmEcho, which uses mmWave to measure
vibrations for eavesdropping, while Hu et al. [10] developed
MILLIEAR, which reconstructs audio from vibrations using
generative models. Wei et al. [9] demonstrated acoustic eaves-
dropping using WiFi signals, and Wang et al. [12] expanded
eavesdropping through Impulse Radio Ultra-Wideband (IR-
UWB) technology with their UWHear system. RFID-based
methods have also been explored, with Wang et al. [13]
showing how audio can be intercepted via RFID. However,
these systems are often affected by environmental factors and
require expensive equipment to send and receive signals like
mmWave, limiting their practicality.
(3) Visual Vibration-based: These methods have a signif-
icant impact in this field due to their ability to achieve high-
quality restoration effects [14]–[21]. Early work by Akutsu et
al. [15] explored using visual data for audio recovery, with
Mim et al. [17] applying optical flow to detect vibrations
in video. A major breakthrough came with the Visual Mi-
crophone concept by Davis et al. [18], which demonstrated
sound recovery from object vibrations in video frames. Recent
advances include the use of high-speed cameras to observe
lamp lights [20] or shiny objects [21] for sound recovery.
Although these methods offer high-quality sound restoration,
they require a very expensive high-speed camera and precise
calibration in advance, limiting practical use. In addition, the
large size of the high-speed camera can easily alert the victim.
X. C ONCLUSION
In this work, we introduce a novel method to repurpose
optical mice as acoustic eavesdropping devices by capturing
vibrations with their optical sensors and converting them
into audio signals. Our research highlights potential security
vulnerabilities in everyday devices and emphasizes the need
for more robust protective measures.
